    Antibiotic failure

    Why patients stay febrile on treatment and what to check before you broaden

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: Dr Miller — IDSA Chalk Talk faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 62-year-old man is admitted with community-acquired pneumonia. He is started on ceftriaxone and azithromycin. Blood and sputum cultures are sent. By hospital day 3 he is still febrile to 38.6 °C, tachycardic, and requires 2 L of oxygen by nasal cannula — about the same as on admission. The white blood cell count is 14,200 per microlitre, down from 15,800. The team wonders whether to broaden to vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Define antibiotic failure versus expected slow response and set a realistic timeline for reassessment.
    • Apply a five-part framework — infection versus mimic, syndrome, organism, drug and penetration, and source control — to a patient not improving on therapy.
    • Identify common gaps in empiric coverage and when broadening is not the right next step.
    • Recognise host factors, drug fever, and superinfection that masquerade as failure.

    What do we mean by antibiotic failure?

    Antibiotic failure is shorthand for "the patient is not doing what I expected on this regimen." Most bacterial infections do not defervesce overnight. For pneumonia, fever and tachycardia often persist for 48 to 72 hours even when the antibiotic is correct; for Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia, blood cultures can remain positive for several days despite appropriate therapy. Calling failure too early drives unnecessary broadening and toxicity.

    A better bedside question is: what exactly is not getting better, and over what interval? Vital signs, oxygen requirement, blood pressure, mental status, and white blood cell (WBC) count trend are more informative than a single temperature. A patient with persistent fever but improving oxygenation and WBC count is on a different trajectory from one whose oxygen needs are climbing.

    Set a clock when you start antibiotics and reassess around 48 to 72 hours for most non-critically ill patients — sooner if worsening. That window lets cultures return and the inflammatory response lag behind bacterial killing.

    Pearl

    Failure is defined by the patient trajectory, not by the thermometer alone. Compare vital signs, oxygen needs, blood pressure, and WBC count trend at 48 to 72 hours — not just the current temperature.

    Background

    Use 72 hours as a prompt to reassess systematically, not as an automatic trigger to add methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or gram-negative coverage.

    First, is it infection at all?

    This is the most common reason antibiotics appear to fail: the fever was never bacterial. Non-infectious mimics account for a large share of "not improving" calls after 48 hours.

    On every recheck ask: what else explains fever or leukocytosis? Contributors include drug fever, venous thromboembolism, acute pancreatitis, chemical aspiration pneumonitis, malignancy, and inflammatory disease. Drug fever often starts 7 to 10 days after the agent, with relative bradycardia or rash, and persists despite antibiotics. Pulmonary embolism and pancreatitis raise WBC count without responding to antibacterials.

    Colonisation also misleads. A sputum culture with "mixed flora" is oropharyngeal colonisation, not pneumonia. Treat the syndrome and radiology, not the culture in isolation.

    Common pitfall

    Adding antibiotics because "we cannot rule out infection" when the picture now favours a non-infectious cause. Each added agent carries toxicity, selects for resistance, and makes subsequent diagnosis harder. Pause and name the alternative diagnoses before you broaden.

    Second, did we get the syndrome right?

    If infection is likely, the next error is the wrong syndrome. An antibiotic that covers pneumonia may miss cellulitis, intra-abdominal infection, or line infection. Re-examine and ask whether the admission diagnosis still fits.

    For our patient, re-examine the radiograph, focal findings, and timeline. Consider mimics: heart failure with vascular congestion, pulmonary embolism with infarct, or organising pneumonia. On the wards, cellulitis is often overcalled — bilateral symmetric stasis erythema is rarely bacterial — and urinary tract infection is overcalled when pyuria and a positive culture occur without urinary symptoms.

    Name the expected organisms for each syndrome. If the syndrome shifts, coverage must shift — often toward narrowing or stopping. If pneumonia becomes heart failure, the answer is diuresis, not vancomycin.

    The man in the vignette is on day 3 of ceftriaxone and azithromycin for pneumonia but remains febrile. Blood cultures are negative, WBC count is trending down slightly, and oxygen need is stable. What is the most useful next step before broadening?

    Persistent fever alone does not prove inadequate coverage, and double covering Pseudomonas aeruginosa without a reason adds toxicity. The highest yield at 72 hours is a structured reassessment — confirm that the syndrome still fits, check culture data and trends, and look for non-infectious causes and source issues — before committing to broader therapy. Procalcitonin trends can inform but do not replace clinical reassessment.

    Third, are we missing the organism?

    Once the syndrome feels right, ask whether the regimen covers likely pathogens — and whether the pathogen would respond to antibacterials at all.

    Three gaps matter. First, resistant organisms: recent health care exposure, antibiotics within 90 days, known methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) Escherichia coli or Klebsiella colonisation, and local antibiogram data. Second, intrinsically uncovered organisms: anaerobes in aspiration with putrid sputum, Pseudomonas aeruginosa with structural lung disease, or Legionella pneumophila if atypical coverage was omitted. Third, non-bacterial infection: influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), or fungi can mimic bacterial pneumonia.

    Use negative data too. Negative cultures after antibiotics are less reassuring, but a Gram stain with no organisms or no good-quality sputum tells you the culture is unreliable. If you add MRSA or Pseudomonas aeruginosa coverage, state the stop rule — "pending nares screen and blood cultures, stop if negative at 48 hours" — rather than leaving broad coverage running.

    Pearl

    When you broaden for a missing organism, write the stop rule at the same time. "Start vancomycin pending MRSA nares and blood cultures; discontinue if negative at 48 hours" prevents indefinite broad coverage.

    A 55-year-old woman with healthcare-associated pneumonia was started on ceftriaxone. She has a history of MRSA nasal colonisation and received clindamycin 2 weeks ago. She is not improving at 48 hours. Which gap is most plausible?

    Ceftriaxone does not cover MRSA. Prior colonisation plus recent antibiotics markedly raises risk. Metronidazole does not add atypical or MRSA coverage, and amoxicillin is also inactive against MRSA. Stopping antibiotics entirely ignores the still-plausible bacterial pneumonia — you need to cover the likely resistant pathogen and reassess.

    Fourth, is the drug reaching the site?

    The right drug fails if it never reaches the site at an effective concentration. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) issues are underappreciated.

    Check dose and interval for this patient: weight-based dosing for vancomycin and aminoglycosides, renal function for beta-lactams, and augmented renal clearance in young critically ill patients. An obese patient dosed like a 70-kg adult will be underdosed; a patient with rising creatinine may be overdosed today and underdosed tomorrow.

    Consider penetration. Beta-lactams penetrate the central nervous system (CNS) poorly without inflamed meninges; nitrofurantoin does not reach blood or kidney parenchyma and should never be used for pyelonephritis or bacteraemia; aminoglycosides are inactive in acidic abscess fluid. Missed doses and interactions matter — a patient who vomits oral doxycycline or is kept nothing by mouth without an intravenous alternative is not receiving therapy.

    Use therapeutic drug monitoring where it exists — vancomycin area-under-the-curve dosing is the clearest example — and involve pharmacy early.

    Pearl

    The right antibiotic at the wrong dose, by the wrong route, or to the wrong compartment is still the wrong regimen. Check renal function, weight, route, and site penetration before you call it failure.

    Background

    Beta-lactams need time above the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC); fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides need peak relative to MIC. Give beta-lactams at the correct interval and infusion time.

    Fifth, have we achieved source control?

    Antibiotics cannot sterilise an undrained collection, an obstructed tract, or an infected device left in place. Source control is the forgotten half of treatment and the most fixable cause of apparent failure.

    Ask about every source: a central or peripheral line placed days ago, an undrained parapneumonic effusion or empyema with persistent pleuritic pain, sinusitis from a nasogastric tube, cholecystitis or cholangitis with biliary obstruction, intra-abdominal abscess, pressure injury, or retained secretions. An undrained effusion or a 4-cm abdominal fluid collection on computed tomography (CT) often matters more than the next antibiotic.

    For pneumonia this means clearing the airway, mobilising, avoiding ongoing aspiration, and evaluating pleural collections. For bacteraemia it means removing infected lines rather than "treating through" them. For urinary infection it means relieving obstruction, not swapping cephalosporins.

    Pearl

    Undrained pus, an obstructed duct, or a retained infected line will not resolve with antibiotic changes alone. Source control beats spectrum every time.

    A 70-year-old patient on broad-spectrum antibiotics for pneumonia has persistent fever. Chest CT shows a moderate right pleural effusion with loculations not previously sampled. Blood cultures are negative. Next best step?

    A loculated pleural collection can explain persistent fever despite adequate antibiotics. Sampling is diagnostic and often therapeutic; empyema requires drainage. Changing antibiotics without addressing the collection misses the fix, and de-escalating to oral amoxicillin without source control risks relapse.

    Host factors, drug fever, and superinfection

    The host and the drugs themselves can mimic failure.

    Immunosuppression — neutropenia, chemotherapy, high-dose corticosteroids, advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), or functional asplenia — blunts improvement and widens the differential to opportunists and fungi. Prolonged neutropenic fever on antibacterials may need evaluation for invasive fungal disease rather than endless escalation.

    Drug fever is common and often missed. Beta-lactams, sulfonamides, and anti-seizure agents are frequent culprits. Clues include fever starting days into therapy despite improvement elsewhere, relative bradycardia, rash or eosinophilia, and defervescence within 48 to 72 hours of stopping the agent. Clostridioides difficile infection can also generate fever and leukocytosis that look like worsening pneumonia.

    Superinfection is less common than invoked. Extending antibiotics increases C. difficile and resistance risk without benefit if another cause explains the fever.

    Pearl

    When a patient improves clinically but fever lingers after a new drug was started 7 to 10 days ago, consider drug fever. A pause and review of the medication list is safer than adding another antimicrobial.

    Common pitfall

    Treating persistent fever with additional antibiotics without reviewing for drug fever, C. difficile disease, or an undrained source. If you add, document why and when you will stop.

    A patient with cellulitis on cefazolin is afebrile with improving erythema by day 3, but on day 8 develops new fever to 39.0 °C, rash, and eosinophilia with stable vitals and no new focus of infection. blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine are slightly up.

    New fever with rash and eosinophilia a week into beta-lactam therapy, with improving original infection, favours drug hypersensitivity — including acute interstitial nephritis — over superinfection. Broadening without considering drug fever adds harm and delays stopping the culprit. Evaluate renal function, urinalysis, and medication timeline.

    A bedside checklist for day 3

    Use the same order every time so you do not skip a step. Run it with the nurse and pharmacist.

    1. Reassess trajectory: temperature curve, respiratory rate, oxygen need, blood pressure, heart rate, and WBC count from day 1 to now.
    2. Name alternatives: drug fever, embolism, pancreatitis, heart failure, or other non-infectious cause?
    3. Confirm syndrome: does the admission diagnosis still fit or has a new syndrome emerged?
    4. Audit microbiology: what grew, what did not, and specimen quality? Review Gram stains, cultures, nares screens, and viral PCR.
    5. Check the drug: dose, interval, route, penetration, renal function, weight, interactions, and missed doses.
    6. Check source control: lines, pleural space, biliary and urinary obstruction, abscess, wound, and aspiration risk.
    7. Decide and document: continue, narrow, broaden with a stop rule, image, sample, drain, or stop — and reassess in 24 hours.
    Pearl

    Good antibiotic decisions on day 3 are often about diagnostics and drainage, not about a bigger gun. If you reach for a broader drug, reach for the phone to microbiology, pharmacy, or radiology at the same time.

    The five-minute version

    • Set the clock at 48 to 72 hours and judge trajectory — vital signs, oxygen and pressure trends, and WBC count — not a single fever.
    • First, ask whether fever is infection at all; drug fever, emboli, pancreatitis, and colonisation masquerading as infection are common causes of apparent failure.
    • Then confirm the syndrome: if the diagnosis has changed, the antibiotic should change with it — and sometimes that means stopping, not broadening.
    • Check for missing organisms with intent: add MRSA or Pseudomonas aeruginosa coverage only when risk factors support it, and write the de-escalation rule when you start.
    • Verify dose, route, renal function, weight, interactions, and penetration to the infected compartment; a drug that cannot reach the site cannot succeed.
    • Prioritise source control — drain pus, relieve obstruction, remove infected lines — before assuming you need a broader spectrum.
