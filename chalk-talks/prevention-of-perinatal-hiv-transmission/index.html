    Case

    A 26-year-old at 10 weeks gestation presents for a first prenatal visit. Routine screening is positive for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) with HIV-1 RNA (ribonucleic acid) 18,400 copies per mL and CD4 420 cells per mm3. She takes no antiretroviral therapy and asks whether she can have a baby without passing HIV on. At the same time, a 34-year-old with known HIV and a last RNA of 85 copies per mL at 36 weeks calls labour and delivery with ruptured membranes at 37 weeks and 4 days.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Describe when perinatal HIV transmission occurs and why sustained viral suppression prevents it.
    • Apply routine testing and immediate antiretroviral therapy for any pregnant person with HIV.
    • Use the viral load near delivery to guide mode of delivery, intrapartum zidovudine, and infant risk stratum.
    • Counsel on infant feeding and testing after birth using current shared decision-making guidance.

    Why perinatal transmission still matters

    Perinatal transmission is now almost entirely preventable. Without intervention it occurs in 15 to 25 percent; with early ART, sustained suppression, and infant prophylaxis, risk falls below 1 percent.

    The goal is elimination, and the resident closes three gaps: test every pregnancy, treat immediately, and plan delivery and infant care by the viral load near birth. When HIV-1 RNA is durably below 50 copies per mL, cohorts report no transmission during pregnancy or delivery, and very low risk during breastfeeding with monitoring.

    Pearl

    Perinatal HIV is a cascade. Testing, immediate ART, the 34- to 36-week viral load, and infant prophylaxis must all succeed — a break at any step raises risk.

    When and how transmission happens

    Three windows exist.

    In utero. Transplacental transmission is more likely with high viraemia or primary infection during pregnancy. Starting ART as soon as HIV is diagnosed — even in the first trimester — is the intervention.

    Intrapartum. The highest-risk window without ART is exposure to blood and genital secretions during labour. Risk climbs above 50 copies per mL and is highest above 1,000. Prolonged rupture, invasive procedures, and genital infection add risk. Delivery planning and intrapartum zidovudine target this window.

    Postpartum via breast milk. Without ART, risk accumulates with duration. With sustained suppression, United States and international cohorts report transmission well below 1 percent, but not zero — hence the 2023 shift from “do not breastfeed” to shared decision-making with monitoring.

    Inconsistent adherence, genital infection, and no prenatal care also raise risk. A load undetectable by the early third trimester predicts an excellent outcome if adherence continues.

    Background

    HIV-1 causes almost all perinatal transmission in the United States; HIV-2 is rare and handled differently. A new HIV diagnosis should prompt current screening for syphilis, hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), tuberculosis risk, and gonorrhoea/chlamydia, which often share testing with HIV.

    Test every pregnancy — and test again

    The most common reason an infant is exposed in 2024 is that the parent was never tested. CDC and HHS recommend opt-out HIV testing as early as possible in every pregnancy, with notification and opportunity to decline, not separate written consent.

    • First visit: HIV antigen/antibody with reflex HIV-1 RNA if reactive, plus syphilis, HBV surface antigen, HCV antibody, and gonorrhoea/chlamydia testing.
    • Repeat: again at 28 to 32 weeks for people at increased risk — higher-prevalence area, sexually transmitted infection during pregnancy, partner with HIV, or injection drug use — and at many centres universally. Repeat any time risk changes.
    • At labour if status unknown: rapid HIV test on admission; do not wait for confirmation to initiate infant precautions if reactive.

    Treat suspected acute HIV as an emergency — viral syndrome with recent exposure and negative antibody warrants HIV-1 RNA testing, same-day ART, and specialist input. Offer partner testing and pre-exposure prophylaxis for a partner without HIV when exposure continues.

    Pearl

    Order HIV testing as routine prenatal labs — opt-out means drawn unless declined, not “offered if at risk.” Every week without a result is a week without ART if positive.

    A patient at 11 weeks gestation declines HIV testing at intake and reports a monogamous partner. What next?

    Opt-out allows declining but requires documentation and re-offering, including on admission for delivery. Risk-based prediction misses most infections; HIV RNA is not a screening substitute.

    Maternal antiretroviral therapy is the cornerstone

    Start and sustain ART — everything else follows. HHS recommends ART for every pregnant person with HIV regardless of CD4, RNA, or trimester, and to continue it through delivery and postpartum without interruption.

    Start immediately. Draw baseline labs — genotype, HIV RNA, CD4, renal and hepatic panels, HBV serologies, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701 if abacavir considered — and start ART the same day with infectious diseases or maternal-fetal medicine by phone if needed overnight. Adjust once resistance returns; do not delay the first dose.

    What to start — guideline-derived preferred options (2024 HHS, verify with pharmacy).

    • Dolutegravir plus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide plus emtricitabine or lamivudine — now preferred throughout pregnancy with rapid suppression and high barrier to resistance.
    • Raltegravir twice daily plus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide plus emtricitabine or lamivudine — useful when diagnosis is late because of fastest decay.
    • Bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine single tablet if already suppressed and tolerating before pregnancy — often continued.

    Usually avoided for initiation in pregnancy: cobicistat-boosted elvitegravir or darunavir (markedly low levels late in pregnancy) and long-acting injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine due to limited data. Abacavir requires HLA-B*5701 negativity; efavirenz is no longer considered teratogenic and is an alternative. Tenofovir plus emtricitabine or lamivudine also treats HBV — do not stop abruptly if co-infected.

    Monitoring: HIV RNA 2 to 4 weeks after starting or changing ART, monthly until undetectable, then every 1 to 2 months and at 34 to 36 weeks to guide delivery and infant planning. Treat nausea with antiemetics, not held doses.

    Common pitfall

    Holding ART for hyperemesis or “to avoid fetal exposure” harms both parent and infant. Give antiemetics and support adherence; if interruption is unavoidable, involve infectious diseases urgently.

    Pearl

    The 34- to 36-week HIV RNA drives every delivery and infant decision. Ensure it is drawn and in the chart with a plan before 37 weeks.

    Newly diagnosed at 10 weeks with RNA 22,000 copies per mL, genotype pending. Best step tonight?

    Prompt ART is recommended every trimester. Monotherapy is obsolete and waiting increases fetal exposure.

    How delivery planning changes with viral load

    Decisions follow the viral load closest to delivery, usually 34 to 36 weeks and again on admission if prior result was detectable or adherence uncertain. Discuss at 30 to 32 weeks to allow time to intensify ART.

    Common pitfall

    Waiting for a repeat RNA on admission to decide on zidovudine when the 35-week result already exceeded 50 copies per mL. Above threshold or unknown means infusion — do not re-triage while labour progresses.

    At 35 weeks RNA is 1,450 copies per mL despite ART, membranes intact, no labour. Guideline-concordant plan?

    At or above 1,000 copies per mL near delivery is the threshold for scheduled cesarean at 38 weeks. Waiting increases labour/rupture risk; induction for vaginal delivery at this load is not preferred.

    Infant prophylaxis and testing after birth

    Every HIV-exposed infant needs antiretroviral prophylaxis and virologic testing — antibody tests reflect maternal antibody. Classify risk at birth by the 34- to 36-week RNA, adherence, and timing of ART start; confirm with neonatology and paediatric infectious diseases.

    Dosing is weight-based and changes quickly: term zidovudine is commonly cited as 4 mg per kg orally twice daily, with separate preterm bands for lamivudine, nevirapine, and raltegravir. Verify with neonatal pharmacy and HHS tables. Do not stop early because the infant looks well — prophylaxis can suppress viraemia.

    Testing: HIV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or RNA nucleic acid test (NAT) — not antibody — at birth (not cord blood), 2 to 6 weeks, 2 to 3 months, and 4 to 6 months; add 1 to 2 months for higher risk. Maternal antibody persists to 18 months. A negative NAT on prophylaxis does not exclude infection until testing after prophylaxis.

    Pearl

    Label risk at birth: “low-risk, 2- to 4-week zidovudine, NAT at birth/2 wk/2 mo/4 mo” versus “higher-risk, presumptive 3-drug, frequent NATs.” One line prevents dropped doses after nursery handoff.

    Term infant, vaginal birth, maternal RNA below 20 copies per mL throughout, adherent on dolutegravir, birth NAT negative. Correct?

    Even durably undetectable maternal load still warrants infant prophylaxis — often 2 weeks for strictly low-risk term infants, 4 weeks otherwise. Single-dose nevirapine alone and infant antibody are incorrect.

    Infant feeding, counseling, and follow-up

    Formula or donor milk eliminates postnatal risk; shared decision-making supports breastfeeding with sustained suppression and monitoring. Name both explicitly.

    If not breastfeeding: support exclusive formula, safe preparation, and access. Avoiding milk is lowest risk.

    If breastfeeding with sustained suppression: document the choice and plan — maternal RNA every 1 to 2 months while feeding (and promptly if adherence slips), infant NATs at routine intervals and after weaning, and urgent review for mastitis, cracked or bleeding nipples, or infant thrush/gastroenteritis. Stop and re-evaluate if load becomes detectable.

    Adherence postpartum is vulnerable — sleep loss, depression, stigma, and coverage gaps. Confirm before discharge: maternal HIV visit in 2 to 4 weeks, ART filled without interruption, case management active, and infant follow-up booked. Discuss contraception and safer conception.

    Pearl

    The postpartum handoff is the highest-risk moment for loss to care. Book maternal HIV, infant NATs, and lactation support before delivery — do not leave to “outpatient follow-up.”

    Common pitfall

    A flat “no” or flat “yes, safe” on breastfeeding without counselling. Guideline asks for non-judgmental exploration, adherence assessment, a clear monitoring and action plan if load rises, and support either way.

    Background

    Without ART, postnatal risk is about 1 percent per month in the first 6 months. With sustained undetectable load and monitoring, observed transmission is uncommon but not proven zero — hence ongoing testing remains essential.

    The five-minute version

    • Test every pregnancy opt-out at entry, repeat at 28 to 32 weeks and at labour if unknown; treat acute HIV as an emergency with same-day ART.
    • Start ART the same day HIV is diagnosed in pregnancy and continue uninterrupted — dolutegravir- or raltegravir-based plus tenofovir/emtricitabine or lamivudine are commonly preferred in 2024 guidance; the 34- to 36-week RNA is pivotal.
    • Below 50 copies per mL supports vaginal delivery; 50 to 999 still favours vaginal with zidovudine; at or above 1,000 or unknown offers cesarean at 38 weeks plus zidovudine.
    • Every infant gets prophylaxis and NAT at birth, 2 to 6 weeks, 2 to 3 months, and 4 to 6 months: low-risk term often zidovudine 2 to 4 weeks, higher-risk presumptive three-drug with gestational dosing verified by pharmacy.
    • Counsel feeding with shared decision-making — formula eliminates risk; if breastfeeding with sustained suppression, monitor maternal RNA every 1 to 2 months while feeding and infant NATs, with a clear stop-and-re-evaluate trigger if detectable. Close the postpartum handoff for both.
