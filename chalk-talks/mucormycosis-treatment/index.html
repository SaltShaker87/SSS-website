    Mucormycosis treatment

    Early amphotericin, early surgery, and fixing the host — a practical ward approach

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 58-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus and poor control presents with 3 days of right facial pain, nasal congestion, and periorbital swelling. Glucose is 420 mg/dL, venous pH 7.18, ketones positive — diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Examination shows right periorbital oedema, chemosis, reduced vision, and a dusky necrotic area on the hard palate. He recently received high-dose glucocorticoids for asthma. Sinus computed tomography (CT) shows right maxillary and ethmoid opacification with bony erosion. The admitting team starts vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam for bacterial sinusitis with orbital cellulitis.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Recognise the host factors and clinical patterns that should trigger suspicion for mucormycosis.
    • Explain why angioinvasion makes early antifungal therapy and surgical debridement decisive.
    • Choose first-line antifungal therapy and describe the role of step-down azoles and agents with no activity.
    • Outline the non-antifungal measures that determine outcome — correcting acidosis, glucose control, and reversing immunosuppression.

    Why mucormycosis behaves so aggressively

    Mucormycosis is invasive infection caused by fungi of the order Mucorales — most often Rhizopus species, but also Mucor, Lichtheimia, Rhizomucor, Cunninghamella, Apophysomyces and Saksenaea. Spores are ubiquitous in soil and decaying matter; disease follows inhalation or traumatic inoculation, not person-to-person spread.

    What makes Mucorales dangerous is angioinvasion. Hyphae invade vessel walls, cause thrombosis and infarction, then spread along vessels into orbit, brain, or lung. Necrotic tissue is both the diagnostic clue — black eschar on palate, turbinate, or skin — and the reason drugs alone often fail. Drug cannot reach tissue that has lost its blood supply.

    Aspergillus can also be angioinvasive, but its hyphae are narrow, septate, with acute-angle branching, while Mucorales show broad (5–25 micrometer), ribbon-like, pauciseptate hyphae with irregular, often right-angle branching. That distinction on frozen section guides therapy, because aspergillosis drugs do not treat mucormycosis.

    Pearl

    Necrosis is both clue and obstacle. Infarcted palate, turbinate, or skin means angioinvasive mould until proven otherwise — and medical therapy alone will not sterilise dead tissue.

    Background

    Mucorales were once grouped as zygomycetes; that term is obsolete. The clinically relevant group is order Mucorales.

    Who is at risk — and why diabetes plus iron matter

    Healthy hosts rarely develop disease. Risk concentrates where phagocyte function is impaired or free iron is abundant.

    • Diabetes mellitus with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — the most common worldwide risk, classic for rhino-orbital-cerebral disease. Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophils; acidosis releases iron from transferrin, and Rhizopus uses free iron and ketones as growth factors.
    • Haematologic malignancy, prolonged neutropenia, and haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Pulmonary disease predominates, often as breakthrough on voriconazole prophylaxis that covers Aspergillus but not Mucorales.
    • Glucocorticoids and other immunosuppression. Steroids blunt macrophage killing and raise glucose.
    • Iron overload and deferoxamine. Deferoxamine is a bacterial siderophore that delivers iron to Mucorales. Deferasirox and deferiprone do not share this effect.
    • Trauma, burns, and contaminated wounds — cutaneous disease after soil exposure, even minor.
    • Voriconazole exposure. No activity against Mucorales; prior use selects for breakthrough.

    When factors overlap — for example, diabetes plus steroids for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) — risk multiplies. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) framework emphasises host plus syndrome, not host alone.

    Pearl

    Acidosis plus free iron is fuel. Correcting diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), stopping deferoxamine, and avoiding unnecessary iron are therapeutic.

    Which patient most warrants immediate work-up for mucormycosis rather than bacterial infection alone?

    Mucormycosis favours impaired phagocytes or free iron. Diabetic ketoacidosis with facial pain, orbital signs and necrotic palatal tissue is classic for rhino-orbital disease. The other cases lack the host defect and necrosis.

    The three clinical syndromes you cannot miss

    Syndrome determines urgency, imaging, and which surgeon you call.

    Rhino-orbital-cerebral disease

    Starts as sinusitis — facial pain, headache, congestion, epistaxis — that does not behave like bacterial disease. Look for periorbital oedema, proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, or vision loss. Examine palate and turbinates for eschar, but absence does not exclude disease; early mucosa may be only pale. Spread proceeds orbit to orbital apex to brain. Mortality climbs once the central nervous system (CNS) is involved.

    Pulmonary disease

    Common in neutropenia and haematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Fever, cough, pleuritic pain, and sometimes massive haemoptysis from vessel erosion. Chest CT may show nodules, consolidation, cavitation, or the reverse halo sign (central ground glass surrounded by consolidation) — suggestive but not specific. Often mistaken for aspergillosis, especially when galactomannan is negative.

    Cutaneous and soft-tissue disease

    After direct inoculation — trauma, surgery, burns, contaminated dressings. An erythematous plaque rapidly becomes necrotic with black eschar and oedema. Pain may be severe early then lessen as nerves infarct. Gastrointestinal and disseminated forms occur rarely, more in neonates or malnourished hosts.

    Common pitfall

    Do not wait for a black eschar to suspect rhino-orbital disease. Early disease shows only oedema, pain, and congestion; expecting eschar delays biopsy.

    Pearl

    Host plus syndrome is the trigger. Diabetic ketoacidosis with sinus or orbital signs, or neutropenia with a reverse halo nodule on mould-active prophylaxis, should be treated as mucormycosis until proven otherwise.

    A neutropenic leukaemia patient on voriconazole develops a cavitary nodule with reverse halo. What is the most appropriate assumption?

    Voriconazole has no activity against Mucorales and selects for them. Reverse halo in a neutropenic host suggests mucormycosis. Echinocandins are not active alone.

    Making the diagnosis before the lab catches up

    Do not wait for culture. Culture is positive in only about half of histology-positive cases. Histopathology is more sensitive and, when positive, should drive therapy.

    • Biopsy early, from centre and edge. Sinus disease — nasal endoscopy with turbinate or sinus mucosa; skin — deep punch including fat. Send fresh tissue for histology, culture, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) if available. Frozen section can give a rapid presumptive answer.
    • Histology: broad ribbon-like pauciseptate hyphae with irregular branching, necrosis and angioinvasion. Artefacts occur; mixed infections happen.
    • Imaging supports but does not confirm. Sinus CT with bony erosion, orbital involvement; chest CT with nodules or reverse halo; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of orbits and brain when central nervous system (CNS) spread is suspected. Early normal imaging does not exclude disease.
    • Adjuncts are limited. Serum galactomannan and 1,3-beta-D-glucan are typically negative and, used alone, mislead. Molecular assays on tissue or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid are promising but not yet standardised.

    Alert pathology, microbiology, ear-nose-throat (ENT), and radiology: label specimens “suspected mucormycosis — urgent” and avoid grinding tissue, which destroys fragile hyphae.

    Pearl

    Tissue is the issue. Negative culture never excludes disease. Treat on histology and re-biopsy if first sample is non-diagnostic but suspicion stays high.

    Background

    Mucorales hyphae are fragile; grinding for culture shears them. Mincing or slicing preserves yield — warn the lab when mucormycosis is suspected.

    Sinus biopsy shows broad pauciseptate hyphae consistent with Mucorales, but cultures are negative at 48 hours. What next?

    Histology is more sensitive than culture. Tissue necrosis with characteristic hyphae should be treated as mucormycosis even when cultures are sterile; voriconazole is inactive and waiting loses time.

    Treatment pillar one — antifungal therapy

    Start early, use an agent active against Mucorales, and know what is not active.

    Liposomal amphotericin B is first-line. Guidance endorses 5–10 mg/kg intravenously once daily as the backbone, with higher doses in that range favoured for central nervous system (CNS) disease when risk of under-treatment outweighs nephrotoxicity. Use the lipid formulation, not deoxycholate. Hours matter more than milligrams — delays of even a day worsen survival.

    Step-down and salvage: posaconazole (delayed-release tablet or intravenous) and isavuconazole (isavuconazonium sulfate) have activity and are used after initial control or when amphotericin is intolerable. They are not preferred as initial monotherapy for rapidly progressive disease, although isavuconazole has been studied as primary where amphotericin cannot be given. Posaconazole may need therapeutic drug monitoring; the delayed-release tablet is preferred over the suspension.

    What does not work: voriconazole, fluconazole, and echinocandins alone have no reliable activity. Combination of amphotericin plus an echinocandin or azole has been reported but is not routine standard — involve infectious diseases and pharmacy.

    Duration is weeks to months, individualised to surgical control, imaging, and host recovery. Typically intravenous liposomal amphotericin B until clear improvement and debridement, then oral azole consolidation for months, sometimes longer if immunosuppression persists.

    Common pitfall

    Do not cover suspected mould with voriconazole alone. If mucormycosis enters the differential while on voriconazole, add an agent active against Mucorales — typically liposomal amphotericin B — immediately.

    Pearl

    Time to amphotericin is survival. If you suspect mucormycosis, give the first dose the same day you biopsy — do not wait for pathology.

    Treatment pillars two and three — surgery and reversing the host defect

    Antifungals rarely cure mucormycosis when necrotic tissue persists or the host defect is uncorrected. Start surgery and host correction in parallel with drugs, not after.

    Aggressive surgical debridement. Necrotic tissue is avascular and will not be sterilised by drug. Remove all devitalised tissue to bleeding margins, often with repeated looks. Sinus disease may need endoscopic debridement and, when the eye is non-viable and brain is threatened, orbital exenteration. Pulmonary disease may need lobectomy for localised lesions; cutaneous disease needs wide excision. Call ear-nose-throat (ENT), ophthalmology, neurosurgery, or thoracic surgery on the night of admission.

    Reverse the host defect fast:

    • Correct diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperglycaemia with insulin and fluids.
    • Reduce immunosuppression where feasible — taper steroids, coordinate with haematology or transplant teams; neutrophil recovery via granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) when appropriate is a turning point.
    • Stop deferoxamine; do not give iron.

    Manage as a team with daily reassessment: Is the lesion advancing? Are margins clear? Is the host recovering? Repeat imaging early if no improvement — progression can occur within hours.

    Pearl

    Drugs buy time; surgery plus host recovery cures. If you are debating whether to consult surgery, you have waited too long.

    Rhino-orbital disease on liposomal amphotericin B with initial debridement now shows orbital apex involvement, no light perception, and persistent hyperglycaemia. What is the best next step?

    Necrotic tissue requires ongoing surgical control plus host correction alongside active antifungal therapy. Voriconazole and echinocandin monotherapy are ineffective; progression after one debridement signals need for repeat surgery.

    The five-minute version

    • Suspect mucormycosis in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) with sinus or orbital necrosis, neutropenia with nodules or reverse halo, and necrotic wounds — act the same day.
    • Biopsy centre and edge of necrosis, warn the lab not to grind tissue, and treat histology as actionable even when cultures are negative.
    • Start liposomal amphotericin B 5–10 mg/kg daily immediately; voriconazole and echinocandins alone do not work.
    • Debride all necrotic tissue early and be prepared to return — drug cannot sterilise dead tissue.
    • Simultaneously reverse the host defect — correct diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperglycaemia, reduce steroids, stop deferoxamine, and support neutrophil recovery.
