    Tick ID of the Midwest

    From erythema migrans to Ehrlichia — what a Midwest resident needs in their pocket

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA Chalk Talk faculty (transcript corrupted — reconstructed from guidelines) Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 58-year-old man from northwestern Wisconsin presents in mid-June with 5 days of fever, headache, and myalgias. He removed an engorged tick from his thigh 10 days ago. No rash. Complete blood count (CBC) shows leukopenia 2.8 × 109/L and thrombocytopenia 98 × 109/L. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is 88 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 76 U/L. Lyme enzyme immunoassay (EIA) is negative.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • List the three medically important ticks of the Midwest and what each transmits.
    • Recognize Lyme disease, anaplasmosis, and ehrlichiosis and know when to think of babesiosis and spotted fever.
    • Choose testing wisely — when polymerase chain reaction (PCR), smear, and serology help and when they mislead.
    • Start empiric doxycycline without waiting when the syndrome is high risk.

    Why the Midwest is tick country now

    Ixodes scapularis, the black-legged tick, has expanded across Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa, Michigan, Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio over the last two decades. Wisconsin and Minnesota have the highest Lyme incidence nationally, and the same tick carries Anaplasma phagocytophilum and Babesia microti. Southward, Amblyomma americanum (lone star) has moved through Missouri, Kansas, and southern Iowa and Illinois with Ehrlichia chaffeensis and E. ewingii. The upper Midwest also has Ehrlichia muris subsp. eauclairensis, first described in Wisconsin and Minnesota and carried by Ixodes scapularis.

    Most transmission is April through October, peaking for nymphal Ixodes late May through July. Nymphs are poppy-seed sized and cause most Lyme, anaplasma, and babesia infections because they are missed. Adults are larger and more often noticed. Co-infection with Borrelia burgdorferi and Anaplasma or Babesia occurs but is uncommon.

    Background

    Ticks feed 36 to 72 hours. Borrelia burgdorferi needs at least 36 hours; Anaplasma, Ehrlichia, and Babesia can transmit sooner, Powassan within minutes.

    Only about one-third of patients with proven tick-borne infection recall a bite. A febrile, cytopenic, or rash illness in tick season should prompt a tick history even without a bite report.

    Pearl

    In northern Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Michigan think Ixodes scapularis first. In Missouri and southern Iowa or Illinois, put Amblyomma americanum and ehrlichiosis at the top.

    Common pitfall

    Do not send ticks for pathogen testing and do not order broad tick panels because a tick was found. Tick testing does not predict infection and panels drive false positives.

    Lyme disease — most common and most overcalled

    Lyme disease from Borrelia burgdorferi (rarely Borrelia mayonii in Minnesota and Wisconsin) is the most common tick-borne infection in the region.

    Erythema migrans is a clinical diagnosis. It appears 3 to 30 days after the bite, expands over days, and is usually warm but not painful. Central clearing is uncommon; many lesions are uniformly erythematous, often in axilla, groin, or popliteal fossa. Fever may be present. In an endemic area, treat erythema migrans without serology — antibodies are absent early and a negative test does not rule it out.

    Early disseminated disease includes multiple erythema migrans lesions, carditis with fluctuating atrioventricular block, facial nerve palsy and other cranial neuropathies, and lymphocytic meningitis. Late disease is most often large-joint oligoarthritis of the knee.

    Pearl

    Erythema migrans in June in Wisconsin is Lyme disease. Do not swab it, do not wait for serology, and do not call it cellulitis or spider bite.

    Beyond erythema migrans, use two-tier serology: enzyme immunoassay (EIA) then immunoblot if positive. Sensitivity is low early. Immunoglobulin M (IgM) after 30 days is usually false. Do not order blood polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Borrelia.

    A 34-year-old woman in Iowa has a 10-cm annular expanding rash 10 days after a tick bite. No fever. What next?

    Erythema migrans in an endemic area is clinical. Serology is negative early and waiting delays care. Culture is not practical and blood PCR for Borrelia is insensitive.

    Anaplasmosis and ehrlichiosis — the fever and cytopenia syndromes

    These are the fever-with-labs illnesses. Five to 14 days after the bite: fever, headache, malaise, myalgias. Rash is uncommon in anaplasmosis and variable in ehrlichiosis — absence does not exclude.

    The clue is the laboratory triad: leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated transaminases. This pattern in tick season points to Anaplasma phagocytophilum (upper Midwest, Ixodes) or Ehrlichia chaffeensis, E. ewingii, or Ehrlichia muris eauclairensis (more southern Amblyomma or Ixodes). Severe disease includes sepsis-like illness and respiratory failure; delay harms older and immunocompromised patients.

    Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) on whole blood is most sensitive in the first week before antibiotics. Blood smear may show morulae in neutrophils (anaplasmosis) or monocytes (ehrlichiosis) but sensitivity is low. Serology is retrospective — a single acute titre is often negative. Do not wait for serology to treat.

    Pearl

    Fever plus leukopenia plus thrombocytopenia in June–September is anaplasmosis or ehrlichiosis until proven otherwise. Send PCR and smear, but start doxycycline now.

    The Wisconsin man has fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and hepatitis 10 days after a tick bite with negative Lyme EIA. Best next step?

    This fits anaplasmosis. Lyme serology is negative because the illness is not Lyme and it is too early. PCR is sensitive early but treatment should not wait. Doxycycline covers Anaplasma, Ehrlichia, and Borrelia; amoxicillin does not cover Anaplasma or Ehrlichia.

    Common pitfall

    Do not wait for confirmation to treat suspected anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, or spotted fever. If epidemiology and syndrome fit, start doxycycline while tests are pending.

    Babesiosis, spotted fever, and tularemia — less common but high stakes

    Babesiosis from Babesia microti in Wisconsin and Minnesota causes fever, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and transaminase elevation. Hemolysis with low haptoglobin, high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and indirect bilirubinemia distinguishes it. Asplenic and immunocompromised patients can exceed 10% parasitemia. Diagnose by Babesia PCR and thin smear (tetrads). Doxycycline does not treat babesiosis.

    Rocky Mountain spotted fever from Rickettsia rickettsii presents with fever, headache, myalgias; triad is uncommon early. Rash, when present, is maculopapular to petechial on wrists and ankles. Serology is negative the first week — treat empirically with doxycycline at any age.

    Tularemia from Francisella tularensis causes ulceroglandular disease — ulcer with tender adenopathy — and the lab must be alerted before culture.

    Background

    Heartland, Bourbon (Amblyomma, Missouri/Kansas) and Powassan (Ixodes, Great Lakes) viruses cause similar cytopenias without doxycycline response; Powassan transmits within minutes.

    Pearl

    Febrile hemolytic anemia after Ixodes exposure, especially without a spleen, is babesiosis until proven otherwise.

    Testing wisely — what helps and what misleads

    • Asymptomatic bite or erythema migrans — no serology. Counsel or treat clinically; early serology misleads.
    • Anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, babesiosis — send whole-blood PCR early plus smear for babesia. Sensitivity falls after antibiotics.
    • Serology is retrospective. Paired titres 2–4 weeks apart confirm later, not on admission.
    • Avoid broad panels and test of cure. Panels yield false positives; Lyme antibodies persist for years.
    Pearl

    PCR tells you what the patient has now; serology tells you what they had weeks ago. For the acutely ill, PCR and smear matter — but a negative result never delays empiric doxycycline when the story fits.

    A 72-year-old asplenic man in Minnesota has fever, hemoglobin 8.1 g/dL, platelets 68 × 109/L, LDH high, haptoglobin undetectable, tick bite 2 weeks ago, Lyme IgG positive. Best test for the acute illness?

    Hemolysis after Ixodes exposure in an asplenic patient is classic for babesiosis. Lyme IgG reflects past exposure, common in endemic areas. Blood PCR for Borrelia is insensitive.

    Treatment and prevention

    Doxycycline covers what can kill quickly. Start it when anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, or spotted fever is possible.

    Common pitfall

    Do not use amoxicillin, cefuroxime, or azithromycin alone when anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, or spotted fever is in the differential. Only doxycycline covers them reliably.

    Prevention: daily tick checks (axilla, groin, scalp), prompt removal with fine-tipped tweezers, shower after exposure, permethrin clothing, and diethyltoluamide (DEET) or picaridin. Save the tick with the date.

    Background

    Single-dose doxycycline prophylaxis after a bite helps only when all are true: engorged Ixodes scapularis attached at least 36 hours, given within 72 hours, local Borrelia infection rate exceeds 20%, and no contraindication. It does not prevent anaplasmosis, babesiosis, or ehrlichiosis and most bites do not qualify.

    A pregnant patient in the first trimester has erythema migrans and penicillin allergy (tolerates cephalosporins). Best therapy?

    Doxycycline is avoided in pregnancy. Amoxicillin and cefuroxime are effective for erythema migrans. Azithromycin is less effective and observation delays care. Clarify the allergy and confirm with obstetrics and pharmacy.

    Pearl

    Counsel every patient with a tick exposure on what to watch for in the next 30 days and when to return — the bite is the exposure, not the diagnosis.

    The five-minute version

    • Ixodes scapularis (Lyme, anaplasmosis, babesiosis), Amblyomma americanum (ehrlichiosis, emerging viruses), and Dermacentor variabilis (spotted fever) define Midwest risk — geography and season narrow the list.
    • Erythema migrans is clinical in an endemic area — treat without serology.
    • Fever with leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and hepatitis in tick season is anaplasmosis or ehrlichiosis until proven otherwise — send PCR but start doxycycline now.
    • Febrile hemolytic anemia after Ixodes exposure, especially without a spleen, is babesiosis — order smear and Babesia PCR; doxycycline alone fails.
    • Do not test ticks, do not order broad panels, and do not let negative acute serology delay therapy when the syndrome fits.
