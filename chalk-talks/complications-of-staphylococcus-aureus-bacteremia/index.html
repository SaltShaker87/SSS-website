    Bacteremia and endocarditis

    Complications of Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia

    Why one positive bottle changes everything — and how to find endocarditis, vertebral infection and other metastatic seeding before it finds you

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 62-year-old man on haemodialysis via a tunneled catheter presents with fever and chills. One of two blood culture sets grows Staphylococcus aureus that is methicillin-susceptible (MSSA — methicillin-susceptible S. aureus). The catheter is removed, cefazolin is started, and he defervesces. On day 4 he reports new dull lower back pain, worse on rolling. He is afebrile, white cells are falling and repeat cultures at 72 hours are negative. The team wonders whether bed rest explains the pain.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Explain why a single culture positive for Staphylococcus aureus is never a contaminant.
    • Distinguish uncomplicated from complicated Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB — Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia) using repeat cultures, fever curve and imaging.
    • List common metastatic complications and the bedside clues that trigger evaluation.
    • Outline the standard workup for every SAB, including repeat cultures and echocardiography.
    • Describe source-control principles and why duration is counted from clearance.

    Why a single bottle of Staphylococcus aureus is never a contaminant

    Coagulase-negative staphylococci in one bottle may be skin flora. Staphylococcus aureus is not. It is rarely a contaminant because when it reaches blood it brings adhesins and toxins built to stick and seed.

    Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) carries measurable mortality and roughly one in three patients develops a secondary focus, even if only one set is positive or the presumed source looks obvious. The bloodstream is the highway, not the destination. Treat the first positive bottle as true bacteremia, draw repeat cultures promptly, and never document “likely contaminant” without infectious diseases (ID — infectious diseases) input.

    Pearl

    One bottle positive for Staphylococcus aureus equals bacteremia. Do not wait for the second set and do not dismiss it because the line site looks clean.

    Risk for complications is higher with community onset, persistent positive cultures, fever beyond 72 hours, prosthetic material, haemodialysis, injection drug use and an unknown primary source. Hospital-onset line-associated bacteremia can still complicate, but community acquisition should sharpen vigilance.

    One of two admission sets grows S. aureus. The patient is afebrile the next morning and the line site is clean. What next?

    Even a single set is bacteremia. Delay wastes time during which seeding occurs, and a clean insertion site does not exclude endovascular or vertebral infection.

    What makes Staphylococcus aureus so good at spreading

    A few virulence themes explain the clinical pattern:

    • Adherence. Fibronectin- and fibrinogen-binding proteins let the organism cling to damaged endothelium, thrombi and prosthetic surfaces within minutes. A structurally normal valve can still be seeded.
    • Evasion. Protein A, capsule and intracellular survival blunt opsonisation and killing, allowing persistence despite antibiotics.
    • Tissue destruction. Alpha-haemolysin, leukocidins and coagulase create necrosis, abscess and clot — the nidus for epidural or psoas collections.
    • Biofilm. On catheters, valves and devices, biofilm resists immunity and drugs, so retained hardware often means persistent bacteremia.

    Hence SAB is rarely transient. Positive cultures or fever at 48 to 72 hours despite appropriate antibiotics are not just treatment failure — they signal an undrained focus, retained hardware or endocarditis.

    Pearl

    Still positive at 48 to 72 hours despite appropriate therapy? Assume an undrained focus, retained hardware or endocarditis until proven otherwise.

    Background

    Methicillin-susceptible and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MSSA and MRSA — methicillin-susceptible and methicillin-resistant S. aureus) share this propensity. Resistance affects drug choice, not the risk of metastasis. Do not treat MSSA as “less worrying.”

    Uncomplicated versus complicated — sorting it out on day three

    Classification is made after 72 hours and drives duration.

    Classifying Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia after initial evaluation
    FeatureUncomplicatedComplicated (any one qualifies)
    Follow-up cultures at 48–72 hoursNegativePositive
    FeverResolves within 72 hoursPersists beyond 72 hours
    Metastatic focus or endocarditisNoneAny metastatic site, endocarditis or septic thrombophlebitis
    SourceIdentified and removableUnknown, retained prosthesis or non-removable
    EchocardiographyNo vegetation (when performed)Vegetation or perivalvular complication

    Only about one-quarter of SAB meets all uncomplicated criteria. When uncertain, treat as complicated and look harder. You cannot label uncomplicated at the first positive culture. You need repeat cultures every 48 hours until negative, documentation of fever and exam, and an echo interpreted in context. Duration is counted from the first negative culture, not admission, and not because the patient “feels fine.” Uncomplicated disease needs at least two weeks from clearance; complicated, endocarditis or deep foci need four to six weeks or longer.

    Common pitfall

    Stopping at two weeks without repeat cultures or an echo. If follow-up cultures were never drawn or the echo was deferred “because the line was removed,” you have not earned the uncomplicated label.

    A 45-year-old woman with MSSA cellulitis has negative cultures at 48 hours, defervesces by day 2, transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE — transoesophageal echocardiogram) is negative and the source is debrided. No prosthesis. How to classify and how long?

    This meets uncomplicated criteria: cleared bacteremia, rapid defervescence, removable focus, no metastases and negative TEE without prosthesis. At least 14 days from clearance is standard; seven days is too short and routine MRI without symptoms is not required.

    Where it goes: complications you must look for

    Think by territory and ask about each daily. Three buckets overlap: endovascular, musculoskeletal and visceral or pulmonary embolic.

    Common metastatic complications and bedside clues
    TerritoryComplicationClue
    Heart and vesselsEndocarditis, device infection, septic thrombophlebitis, mycotic aneurysmNew murmur, embolic lesions, persistent bacteremia, line-vein tenderness
    Spine and jointsVertebral osteomyelitis or discitis, epidural abscess, septic arthritisNew back or neck pain, radicular pain, joint swelling, weakness or sphincter change
    LungSeptic emboli, cavitary lesions, empyemaPleuritic pain, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, nodular infiltrates
    Central nervous systemEmbolic stroke, brain abscessHeadache, focal deficit, confusion
    VisceraPsoas, splenic or renal abscessFlank or hip pain, persistent fever, abdominal pain

    Vertebral and epidural disease is especially easy to miss in a supine inpatient and carries risk of paralysis. New mechanical, focal back pain with point tenderness in SAB is vertebral infection until proven otherwise — do not attribute it to bed rest. S. aureus is a leading cause of infective endocarditis (IE — infective endocarditis). It may present only as persistent bacteremia or stroke. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE — transthoracic echocardiogram) misses many vegetations, especially with prosthetic valves or devices, so threshold for TEE is low.

    Pearl

    Back pain in SAB is vertebral osteomyelitis or epidural abscess until proven otherwise. Examine for tenderness, radicular features, weakness and sphincter dysfunction, and image urgently with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI — magnetic resonance imaging) if present.

    Pearl

    Examine hands, eyes and skin daily. Janeway lesions, splinter haemorrhages, conjunctival petechiae and a new murmur upgrade you to complicated and push you toward TEE.

    The haemodialysis patient reports dull lumbar pain on day 4. Repeat cultures are negative and he is afebrile. Next step?

    Negative cultures do not exclude vertebral seeding; pain may appear days after clearance. Examination plus MRI is the diagnostic step. Plain films are insensitive early and empiric extension misses epidural disease that may need drainage.

    The workup every patient with Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia needs

    1. Repeat blood cultures every 48 hours until negative. Document the first negative date — that starts the duration clock.
    2. Daily history and exam. Ask about back or neck pain, joint pain, headache, chest or flank pain and dyspnoea. Look at line sites, joints, skin, and listen for a new murmur. Screen neurologically.
    3. Source identification and control. Remove infected catheters promptly and involve surgery or radiology for drainable collections. SAB is rarely truly from urine without seeding, so look beyond a positive urine culture.
    4. Echocardiography in almost everyone. TEE is favoured with prosthetic valve, prior endocarditis, device, persistent bacteremia or fever, community onset, injection drug use or emboli. TTE alone is insufficient in those settings.
    5. Targeted imaging for clues. MRI spine for back pain or deficits, joint aspiration for a hot joint, computed tomography (CT — computed tomography) chest for nodular infiltrates, abdominal imaging for flank or hip pain.
    6. Infectious diseases consultation. Associated with lower mortality and more appropriate workup and duration — recommended for every SAB.

    Definitive antibiotic choice follows susceptibility, with anti-staphylococcal beta-lactams preferred for MSSA and vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA under pharmacy guidance. The complication hunt is independent of the drug — source control matters more than escalating antibiotics alone.

    Pearl

    Count duration from the first negative blood culture, not the first dose. Without documented clearance you cannot start the clock.

    Common pitfall

    Pulling a line and calling the job done. Line removal does not exclude endocarditis or vertebral seeding that occurred before removal. Complete repeat cultures and echo even when the source seems obvious.

    A 70-year-old woman with MRSA bacteremia, prosthetic aortic valve and persistent bacteremia has a negative TTE on day 2. Next cardiac imaging?

    Prosthetic material plus persistent bacteremia demands TEE sensitivity. TTE misses prosthetic vegetations and perivalvular abscess where early surgery may be needed.

    Treatment, source control and how long to treat

    Two principles: remove or drain what you can, and give enough duration to sterilise what remains.

    Source control first

    Review every piece of hardware: peripheral and central catheters, dialysis catheters, pacemaker leads, prosthetic joints and grafts. Remove infected lines; do not rely on cultures drawn through the line. Epidural, psoas and splenic abscesses need drainage when feasible. Involve surgery or interventional radiology early.

    Antibiotic principles

Use a definitive beta-lactam for MSSA when possible and reserve vancomycin or daptomycin for MRSA with pharmacy-guided monitoring. Early oral switch or truncating intravenous therapy because the patient “looks well” is inappropriate for complicated SAB. Rifampicin adjuncts and combination therapy are not routine for bacteremia alone.

    Duration framework

    • Uncomplicated: at least two weeks intravenous from first negative culture, provided all criteria are met.
    • Complicated, endocarditis or deep focus: four to six weeks or longer, dictated by site — endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis with or without epidural abscess and undrained prosthetic infection need the longer end.
    • Persistent bacteremia or undrained focus: restart the clock after drainage or hardware removal.
    Background

    Older teaching sometimes called line-related SAB that cleared quickly “uncomplicated” and gave short courses. Contemporary data raise the bar: catheter-related bacteremia without adequate echo or documented clearance should not be labelled uncomplicated to justify two weeks.

    Pearl

    Do not count days before clearance toward the course. Five days of antibiotics before cultures cleared is not “five days of a two-week course.”

    When to worry again: persistent bacteremia, recurrence and follow-up

    Complications can declare themselves after discharge. Counsel patients that new back or joint pain, chest pain, headache, focal weakness or skin lesions in the weeks after SAB need urgent re-evaluation. Ensure handoffs include the clearance date, echo result and planned duration so truncation does not occur.

    Recurrence within weeks should trigger re-assessment for occult endocarditis, vertebral infection or retained hardware. Haemodialysis, injection drug use and prosthetic material raise recurrence risk and lower the threshold for repeat imaging and ID re-consultation.

    Document what you excluded: “MSSA bacteremia, line removed, TEE negative, repeat cultures negative, serial spine exams without tenderness, 14 days from clearance” communicates more safety than “SAB — improved.”

    The five-minute version

    • Every Staphylococcus aureus positive blood culture is true bacteremia — one bottle triggers a full workup.
    • Classify only after 48 to 72 hours: uncomplicated requires negative repeat cultures, defervescence, no metastases, a removable source and a negative echo; otherwise it is complicated.
    • Examine daily by territory: heart (murmur, emboli), spine (focal back pain, weakness, sphincter change), joints, lung, brain and viscera — back pain is vertebral infection until proven otherwise.
    • Bundle for every SAB: repeat cultures until negative, source control and line removal, low-threshold TEE, targeted imaging for clues and ID consultation.
    • Count duration from first negative culture: at least two weeks for truly uncomplicated, four to six weeks or longer for complicated or deep focus; do not truncate because the patient looks well.
