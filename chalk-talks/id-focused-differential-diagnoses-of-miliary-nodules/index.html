    ID-focused differential diagnoses of miliary nodules

    Sorting hematogenous spread from its mimics at the bedside

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 34-year-old man with untreated human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection presents with 6 weeks of fever, night sweats, 5-kg weight loss, and dry cough. Chest radiograph and computed tomography (CT) show innumerable 1- to 3-mm nodules in a random distribution. He worked two years on a construction site in Arizona and grew up in the Ohio River valley. CD4 count is 38 cells per cubic mm. The night team asks: is this miliary tuberculosis (TB), disseminated histoplasmosis, or something else — and what do you do before morning?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Define miliary nodules and distinguish a random distribution from perilymphatic or centrilobular patterns on chest CT.
    • Generate an infectious diseases (ID)-focused differential for miliary nodules weighted by host immune status and epidemiologic exposure.
    • Recognise clinical and radiographic clues that push toward miliary TB, endemic fungi, Nocardia, or viral causes.
    • Outline a practical inpatient workup that does not miss airborne isolation, HIV testing, and tissue diagnosis.

    What miliary actually means

    Miliary comes from the Latin for millet seed. On imaging it means innumerable 1- to 3-mm nodules scattered so evenly that no lung zone is spared. The pattern can be subtle on plain radiograph; high-resolution CT makes it obvious and lets you read the distribution, which is the most useful clue.

    Pathologists and radiologists describe three distributions:

    • Random nodules touch the pleura, fissures, and bronchovascular bundles without preference — they sit along pleural surfaces and inside the lobule. This is the hematogenous pattern and the one we mean by miliary.
    • Perilymphatic nodules cluster along fissures, pleura, and bronchovascular bundles, sparing parts of the lobule. Think sarcoidosis and lymphangitic carcinomatosis.
    • Centrilobular nodules spare the pleura and sit a few millimetres away from it, often with tree-in-bud branching. Think endobronchial spread of infection such as active TB or bacterial bronchiolitis, or hypersensitivity pneumonitis.

    If the nodules truly touch the pleura everywhere you look, assume hematogenous dissemination until proven otherwise. That single observation narrows a long pulmonary nodule list to a much shorter ID-focused list.

    Pearl

    Size and distribution matter more than counting nodules. Random 1- to 3-mm nodules that abut the pleura in all zones equal hematogenous spread, even before you know the organism.

    Why the infectious differential is so broad

    Any organism that can enter the bloodstream and seed the pulmonary capillaries can produce a miliary pattern. The alveolar capillary bed is a filter for hematogenous traffic, so a shower of organisms lodges diffusely and forms granulomas or microabscesses that appear as identical nodules.

    The same CT pattern appears in a traveller with miliary TB, a transplant recipient with disseminated Histoplasma capsulatum, a young adult on a tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitor with coccidioidomycosis, and an older adult with Nocardia bacteraemia. The radiograph does not name the organism — the host, exposure, and syndrome do.

    A useful mental model is host plus route plus tempo: immune status, exposure, and tempo. Weeks of wasting favours TB and fungi; days of rapid hypoxaemia favours viral or bacterial seeding.

    Background

    Classic miliary TB was described before CT existed, on chest radiographs of children with hematogenous spread. The term stuck because the nodules really do look like millet seeds scattered on a plate. Modern high-resolution CT simply makes the distribution easier to read.

    Pearl

    If you remember only three organisms after midnight, remember Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Histoplasma capsulatum, and Coccidioides. In a United States hospital they cover most infectious miliary cases and they are the ones with specific public-health and treatment consequences.

    A 28-year-old on adalimumab (a TNF inhibitor) for Crohn disease develops fever and diffuse 2-mm random nodules after a winter trip to Arizona. Sputum acid-fast smear is negative. What best explains the pattern?

    Hematogenous infections produce random nodules. A negative smear does not exclude miliary TB, but TNF inhibition plus desert Southwest exposure strongly favours Coccidioides. Sarcoidosis is perilymphatic, not random, and malignancy rarely gives uniform 1- to 3-mm random nodules.

    History and host factors that re-weight the list

    At the bedside, ask three questions before ordering:

    1. Where have you lived and travelled? Midwest or cave/bird exposure pushes Histoplasma; Arizona/California dust pushes Coccidioides; birth in a high-TB-burden country or shelter work pushes TB.
    2. What is the immune status? HIV/CD4, transplant, biologics, steroids, pregnancy. Advanced HIV favours histoplasmosis and cryptococcosis; TNF inhibitors favour TB and fungi.
    3. What is outside the lung? Headache suggests meningitis or brain abscess; skin lesions suggest Nocardia or Cryptococcus; eye symptoms suggest choroidal tubercles or CMV retinitis.

    Do not forget dust, farming, spelunking, mulch or bird droppings, and chicken coops. Ask about TB contacts, prior latent TB testing and treatment, and vaccination and sick contacts for measles and VZV.

    Pearl

    Geography is the best rapid test for endemic fungi. A one-sentence travel and residence history narrows the differential more than a second CT scan.

    What makes you worry for miliary tuberculosis tonight

    Miliary TB is the diagnosis you cannot miss at 2 a.m. because it is transmissible, treatable, and lethal if delayed. It occurs when a primary or reactivated focus seeds the bloodstream and lungs simultaneously.

    Favouring features include weeks of fever, night sweats, weight loss, and cough, often with hepatosplenomegaly, choroidal tubercles, and hints of disseminated disease such as pancytopenia, hyponatraemia, or sterile pyuria.

    The classic error is waiting for sputum to be positive. Smear sensitivity is low in miliary disease; a negative smear or even a negative Xpert Ultra does not exclude TB.

    Common pitfall

    Do not wait for confirmation to isolate. If miliary TB is plausible, place the patient in an airborne infection isolation room, require N95 respirators, and notify infection control and the health department before you send the third sputum. Document why you isolated or why you did not.

    Pearl

    Examine the eyes and the cerebrospinal fluid in miliary TB. Choroidal tubercles and TB meningitis coexist with miliary nodules more often than trainees expect; both change duration of therapy and urgency of steroids and consultation.

    A 62-year-old man with miliary nodules and fever has three negative sputum acid-fast smears. No isolation was ordered. What is the safest next step?

    Miliary TB is paucibacillary in sputum. Smear negativity is common and does not exclude it. Isolation, nucleic acid amplification testing, cultures, HIV testing, and evaluation for meningitis or dissemination should proceed in parallel with testing for fungi and other causes.

    When miliary is not infection — the mimics to rule out

    Not every miliary pattern is infection. Residents are consulted on miliary nodules that turn out to be sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, or metastases. The ID contribution is distinguishing hematogenous infection from these mimics so the patient is not treated incorrectly.

    Clues that point away from infection:

    • Sarcoidosis usually shows perilymphatic nodules along fissures, bilateral hilar adenopathy, and often hypercalcaemia or uveitis. Nodules are not uniformly random to the pleura everywhere.
    • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis shows centrilobular ground-glass nodules that spare the pleura, often with mid-lung predominance and exposure to birds, mould, or hot tubs. Hypoxaemia and inspiratory crackles dominate over fever.
    • Miliary metastases (thyroid, renal, melanoma, and others) can look random but nodules are often larger, vary more in size, and may be positron emission tomography (PET)-avid; history of known malignancy or thyroid mass helps.
    • Langerhans cell histiocytosis and pneumoconioses have upper-lobe or occupational signatures and very different demographics.

    Biopsy often settles the question. Non-caseating granulomas favour sarcoidosis and caseating granulomas favour infection, but neither is specific. Never start steroids for presumed sarcoidosis before infection is reasonably excluded — steroids can worsen TB or disseminated fungi.

    Common pitfall

    Giving high-dose steroids for presumed sarcoidosis or hypersensitivity before excluding TB and endemic fungi. The patient may improve briefly and then deteriorate as infection disseminates. Obtain HIV status, sputum and fungal studies, and tissue before immunosuppression if you can.

    A 48-year-old woman with diffuse nodules has perilymphatic distribution along fissures, bilateral hilar nodes, and hypercalcaemia. No fever. Most likely category?

    Distribution solves the case. Perilymphatic nodules along fissures and pleura plus hilar adenopathy favour sarcoidosis. Miliary infection is random and febrile; hypersensitivity spares the pleura.

    A practical workup you can order on call

    Build the workup in layers: isolate and test for TB, check immune status, pursue fungi and Nocardia in parallel, and plan for tissue.

    First hour

    • Place in airborne isolation if TB is on the differential and document it. Notify infection prevention.
    • Offer HIV antigen/antibody testing if status unknown, and CD4 count if positive. This changes pretest probabilities immediately.
    • Obtain sputum for acid-fast smear, mycobacterial nucleic acid amplification (e.g., Xpert Ultra), and mycobacterial culture — three sputa are often recommended by guidelines, but do not delay isolation for the second or third. Add bacterial and fungal cultures of sputum and blood.
    • Check basic labs with attention to cytopenias, liver enzymes, and electrolytes; these are clues to disseminated disease rather than diagnostic tests.

    Before morning

    • High-resolution chest CT to confirm random distribution and to guide bronchoscopy. Compare old imaging if available.
    • For endemic fungi: Histoplasma urine antigen and serology; Coccidioides serology and antigen; cryptococcal antigen where appropriate. Cross-reactivity is common — interpret with ID.
    • For Nocardia: alert the lab to hold cultures longer, request modified acid-fast staining, and consider brain imaging.
    • For viral causes: review vaccination, consider CMV PCR and eye exam in immunosuppression.
    • Plan for bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and transbronchial biopsy if sputum is non-diagnostic. BAL yield is higher than sputum for TB and fungal testing. A skin or node biopsy is faster if a lesion exists.

    No single test excludes disseminated infection. Cultures take weeks; antigens and nucleic acid tests are faster but each has blind spots. Running the panel in parallel avoids days of delay.

    Pearl

    Send the fungal and mycobacterial tests together. Histoplasma urine antigen can return in a day and change management before cultures or biopsy. Waiting for one result before sending the next adds days you cannot afford.

    A 41-year-old with miliary nodules, fever, and headache has advanced HIV. Chest CT is random miliary. What studies belong in the first bundle besides TB workup?

    In advanced HIV, miliary pattern strongly overlaps disseminated histoplasmosis and cryptococcosis, both of which involve the central nervous system. CD4 count, fungal antigens, and evaluation for meningitis belong with TB testing, not after it.

    Which feature best separates miliary infection from hypersensitivity pneumonitis on CT?

    Distribution is the discriminator. Random nodules abut pleura everywhere in hematogenous infection; hypersensitivity nodules are centrilobular and keep a few millimetres distance from the pleura, often with ground-glass opacity.

    The five-minute version

    • Miliary means hematogenous: 1- to 3-mm nodules in a random distribution that touch the pleura everywhere. Check distribution before chasing organisms.
    • Carry a four-group ID short list: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, endemic fungi (Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Blastomyces), Nocardia, and disseminated viral or cryptococcal disease — re-weighted by immune status.
    • Geography and host are the fastest tests. A sentence about residence, dust or cave or bird exposure, HIV and TNF inhibitor status, and extrapulmonary symptoms re-orders the list immediately.
    • If miliary TB is plausible, isolate on arrival, send sputum smear, nucleic acid amplification, and culture, offer HIV testing, and look for extrapulmonary TB — a negative smear never excludes it.
    • Work up fungi and Nocardia in parallel, not in sequence, and plan early for BAL or biopsy. Do not start high-dose steroids for presumed sarcoidosis before infection is reasonably excluded.
