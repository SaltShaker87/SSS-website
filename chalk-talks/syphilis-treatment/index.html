    Sexually transmitted infections

    Syphilis treatment

    Pick the penicillin, get the dose and duration right, and do not miss the nervous system

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: Dr S. Reddy, IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 9 minutes
    Case

    A 29-year-old man presents with a diffuse rash involving the palms and soles, sore throat, and a healed painless penile ulcer. He reports a new male partner in the past three months. Rapid plasma reagin (RPR) is 1:64 and treponemal test is positive. He has no headache, vision changes, hearing loss, or focal deficits. He is not pregnant, has no penicillin allergy, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status is pending. You need to choose treatment, counsel partners, and plan serologic follow-up.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Stage syphilis correctly to choose the right penicillin regimen and duration.
    • Select and dose benzathine penicillin G, aqueous crystalline penicillin G, and alternatives for neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, and otosyphilis.
    • Manage syphilis in pregnancy, penicillin allergy, the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, and partner and serologic follow-up.

    Why staging determines everything

    Syphilis is caused by Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum, a spirochete that remains fully sensitive to penicillin after 70 years. No clinically relevant penicillin resistance exists, so penicillin G is the drug for every stage. What changes is where the organism lives and how fast it divides. Early infection has abundant, rapidly dividing treponemes, so a single depot injection that keeps levels above the treponemicidal threshold for 7 to 10 days cures it. Late infection has sparse organisms behind tissue and central nervous system (CNS) barriers and needs weeks of exposure.

    The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines divide syphilis into primary (painless chancre), secondary (disseminated rash, mucous patches, condylomata lata), early latent (asymptomatic, acquired within 12 months), late latent (acquired more than 12 months ago or unknown), tertiary, and neurosyphilis/ocular/otosyphilis, which can occur at any stage. If you cannot confidently prove acquisition within 12 months from history, prior serology, or exam, treat as late latent.

    Background

    Treponema pallidum divides every 30 to 33 hours. Benzathine penicillin G (BPG) releases penicillin slowly from muscle for 2 to 3 weeks, which is why one dose covers early disease but not late latent disease.

    Pearl

    Treatment is stage, not titer. A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) of 1:128 does not need more penicillin than 1:4. Stage first, then choose the regimen.

    Early syphilis — primary, secondary, and early latent

    For adults with primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis, give benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units intramuscular as a single dose, as two 1.2-million-unit injections at separate sites. Extra doses do not improve outcome or prevent neurosyphilis.

    On the day of treatment, confirm no penicillin allergy and screen for neurologic, ocular, or otologic symptoms — headache, vision change, eye pain or redness, hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, facial droop, or gait change. If any are present, you are no longer in the early syphilis pathway. For uncomplicated early disease you do not need lumbar puncture or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination. Draw a baseline nontreponemal test — RPR or Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) — using the same assay and laboratory you will use for follow-up.

    Counsel about the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction: fever, myalgia, headache, and transient rash flare within 2 to 12 hours of the first dose as treponemes lyse. It is not allergy, resolves within 24 hours, and does not preclude future penicillin. In pregnancy it can trigger uterine contractions, but do not delay treatment.

    For nonpregnant patients without CNS involvement who truly cannot receive penicillin, doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 14 days is the preferred alternative. Do not use azithromycin because resistance is widespread.

    Pearl

    Benzathine, procaine, and aqueous crystalline penicillin G are not interchangeable. Benzathine does not reach treponemicidal levels in CSF and must never be used alone for neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, or otosyphilis.

    The vignette patient has secondary syphilis with palmoplantar rash and no neurologic, ocular, or otologic symptoms. No allergy. What is correct today?

    Primary, secondary, and early latent need a single 2.4-million-unit benzathine dose. The intravenous regimen is for neurosyphilis/ocular disease. Three weekly doses are for late latent. Doxycycline 28 days is the late latent alternative, not early disease.

    Late latent, tertiary, and syphilis of unknown duration

    If the patient is asymptomatic with a positive treponemal test and reactive nontreponemal test, and you cannot prove acquisition within 12 months, treat as late latent: benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units intramuscular weekly for 3 weeks (7.2 million units total). The same 3-dose regimen applies to tertiary syphilis without CNS involvement.

    Give doses at 7-day intervals, document dates, and recall the patient. If more than 9 days elapse between doses, most clinicians restart the series. Do not assume a negative RPR excludes late syphilis — nontreponemal tests can wane to nonreactive while treponemal tests stay positive. Counsel that serologic decline is slow in late disease; a fourfold drop may take 12 to 24 months, and tertiary damage such as gumma or aortitis may not reverse even though treatment halts progression.

    Common pitfall

    Calling every asymptomatic patient early latent to give one dose instead of three. Early latent needs affirmative evidence of acquisition within 12 months. When timing is uncertain, treat as late latent and document why.

    Pearl

    A treponemal test usually stays positive for life. Use the nontreponemal test (RPR or VDRL) to judge response, and compare to a baseline drawn the day you treat.

    A 62-year-old woman is asymptomatic, treponemal test positive, RPR 1:8, last syphilis test 10 years ago. No neurologic or ocular symptoms. What regimen?

    Without proof of acquisition within 12 months this is late latent of unknown duration, needing three weekly doses. A single dose is insufficient and adding doxycycline is not indicated when penicillin is tolerated.

    Neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, and otosyphilis

    Neurosyphilis can occur at any stage. Consider it with headache, meningismus, stroke in a young adult, cognitive change, or cranial nerve deficits — and always when syphilis comes with eye or ear symptoms. Ocular syphilis (uveitis, retinitis, optic neuritis) and otosyphilis (hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo) are treated as neurosyphilis even with normal CSF.

    The adult regimen is aqueous crystalline penicillin G 18 to 24 million units per day intravenously as 3 to 4 million units every 4 hours or continuous infusion for 10 to 14 days. When intravenous therapy is not feasible, give procaine penicillin G 2.4 million units intramuscular daily plus probenecid 500 mg orally four times daily for 10 to 14 days. Some experts add one benzathine dose after the intravenous course, but it does not replace it.

    Cerebrospinal fluid examination is important, but do not delay ocular or otologic treatment for the tap. A reactive CSF-VDRL is specific but insensitive — a negative result does not exclude neurosyphilis. Interpret pleocytosis (greater than 5 white cells per microliter often used in HIV-negative patients), protein, and VDRL together with serum serology; in persons living with HIV interpretation is more nuanced and infectious disease input helps. All patients need urgent ophthalmology or audiology evaluation, HIV testing if unknown, and repeat CSF if initially abnormal.

    Pearl

    Ask every patient with syphilis about vision change, eye pain, hearing loss, tinnitus, and vertigo. A yes moves you from benzathine in clinic to intravenous penicillin in hospital.

    Common pitfall

    A nonreactive CSF-VDRL does not rule out neurosyphilis. Use the full CSF profile and treat ocular or otologic disease as neurosyphilis even with bland CSF.

    A 38-year-old man with secondary syphilis has acute unilateral eye pain, photophobia, and blurred vision, RPR 1:32. No allergy. Correct next step?

    Ocular syphilis is neurosyphilis regardless of CSF. Benzathine does not reach the eye or CSF, and doxycycline is inadequate. Delay risks permanent vision loss.

    Syphilis in pregnancy

    Untreated maternal syphilis can cause stillbirth, neonatal death, preterm birth, and congenital syphilis. The maternal regimen is the same as for nonpregnant patients by stage — one benzathine dose for early, three weekly doses for late latent — and intravenous aqueous crystalline for neurosyphilis or ocular disease. No alternative reliably treats the fetus.

    If a pregnant patient reports penicillin allergy, do not substitute. Stratify the history and, if true allergy is possible, refer for penicillin skin testing and desensitization in a monitored setting. Once desensitized, penicillin must continue without interruption.

    Screen all pregnant persons at the first prenatal visit and, in high-prevalence settings or with risk factors, repeat at 28 weeks and at delivery. Treat immediately — days matter — and arrange neonatal evaluation even after maternal treatment, especially when treatment was late in pregnancy and may not have had time to cure fetal infection.

    Background

    Congenital syphilis has risen sharply in the United States. Most cases follow missed screening or non-penicillin therapy. Early benzathine penicillin G in the birthing parent is the most effective prevention.

    Pearl

    In pregnancy, benzathine penicillin G is the only recommended therapy for any stage. Allergy is an indication for desensitization, not substitution.

    If penicillin cannot be used, and how to follow up

    Outside pregnancy and outside the CNS, doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily is the best studied alternative: 14 days for early syphilis, 28 days for late latent. Data are thinner than for penicillin, so follow-up must be close, and any neurologic, ocular, or otologic sign should prompt desensitization instead.

    Take a precise allergy history — timing, hives, angioedema, wheeze, anaphylaxis, and exposures since. Many labeled allergies are remote rashes, not true hypersensitivity. For neurosyphilis, ocular/otosyphilis, or pregnancy, have a low threshold to involve allergy and pharmacy for desensitization.

    Follow nontreponemal titers with the same assay and laboratory. For early syphilis without HIV, check RPR or VDRL at 6 and 12 months; a fourfold decline (for example, 1:64 to 1:16) within 12 months signals response. For late latent, follow at 6, 12, and 24 months. A sustained fourfold rise, failure to fall fourfold, or recurrent symptoms needs evaluation for reinfection versus failure, repeat HIV testing, CSF examination if indicated, and retreatment. Treponemal tests stay positive and are not for follow-up.

    Partner management is treatment. For primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis, treat partners exposed within 90 days presumptively even if seronegative — they may be incubating. For exposures beyond 90 days, test and treat if positive or if follow-up is uncertain. Advise no sex until patient and partners are treated and symptoms resolved. HIV coinfection does not change the penicillin dose, but it raises vigilance for atypical presentation and slower titer decline — screen neurologically and ophthalmologically with a low threshold for CSF examination.

    Background

    The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction is most common in secondary syphilis within 12 hours of the first dose — fever, rigors, and rash flare that mimics sepsis. It is self-limited, managed with antipyretics, and more common with high starting titers. In pregnancy monitor for uterine activity but do not delay therapy.

    Common pitfall

    Judging cure from a single titer. One RPR means nothing without a baseline and a repeat with the same assay. Draw the baseline the day you treat and schedule the repeat before the patient leaves.

    A nonpregnant 45-year-old with late latent syphilis and documented anaphylaxis to penicillin cannot be desensitized tonight. No CNS or eye/ear symptoms. Best alternative?

    For late latent without CNS or ocular disease when penicillin cannot be used, doxycycline 28 days is preferred. Azithromycin fails due to resistance and ceftriaxone single dose is inadequate.

    The five-minute version

    • Stage first: primary, secondary, and early latent (within 12 months) get benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units intramuscular single dose; late latent, unknown duration, and tertiary without neurosyphilis get 2.4 million units weekly for 3 weeks.
    • Any neurologic, ocular, or otologic symptom is neurosyphilis — use aqueous crystalline penicillin G 18 to 24 million units per day intravenously for 10 to 14 days (or procaine penicillin plus probenecid). Benzathine alone never suffices for CNS disease.
    • In pregnancy use the same benzathine regimen by stage. It is the only effective fetal therapy; if allergic, desensitize and treat with penicillin.
    • Counsel about the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction within 12 hours, treat partners from the past 90 days presumptively, and follow RPR or VDRL with the same assay — a fourfold decline indicates response.
    • Penicillin allergy outside pregnancy and outside the CNS: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 14 days (early) or 28 days (late latent) with close follow-up; when the CNS or eye/ear is involved, desensitize.
