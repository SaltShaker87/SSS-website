    Opportunistic infections in HIV/AIDS: screening and prevention

    A CD4-guided approach to screening, prophylaxis, and everyday prevention

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 34-year-old man is newly diagnosed with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. He feels well and has no fever, cough, headache, or visual changes. Labs show a CD4 count of 42 cells/µL and an HIV viral load of 210,000 copies/mL. He asks what infections he needs to worry about, and whether he should start antibiotics to prevent them. You wonder what to screen for now, what prophylaxis to start today, and what can wait until antiretroviral therapy (ART) takes hold.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Use the CD4 count to predict which opportunistic infections (OIs) to screen for.
    • Order the correct screening tests at entry to HIV care and when CD4 falls below key thresholds.
    • Choose and stop primary prophylaxis appropriately, with ART as the central prevention strategy.
    • Counsel patients on vaccines and everyday measures that prevent OIs beyond pills.

    Why opportunistic infections still matter when ART works so well

    An opportunistic infection is an infection that causes disease mainly when the immune system is impaired — the classic acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)-defining illnesses. In people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), the risk is driven by progressive CD4 T-lymphocyte depletion. When antiretroviral therapy (ART) restores immunity, OI rates fall dramatically. But OIs have not disappeared. They appear in three groups you will meet on call: people newly diagnosed with advanced HIV, people who have fallen out of care, and people whose ART is failing.

    The teaching point from this talk is not a list to memorise organism by organism. It is a system: know the CD4 thresholds where specific OIs emerge, screen for latent infections before they reactivate, use short-term primary prophylaxis only where it helps, and treat ART itself as the most effective OI prevention. Everything else — serologies, antigens, and prophylactic antibiotics — follows that logic.

    Two principles will keep you out of trouble. First, most OIs are reactivations or exposures you can anticipate. A thorough history — country of origin and travel, prior tuberculosis exposure, sexual history, injection drug use, housing, animal and soil exposure, and prior OIs and vaccines — is screening. Second, a test is only useful if you know what you will do with the result. Send screening tests at entry to care and repeat only when the CD4 falls to a new threshold, not at every visit.

    Pearl

    ART is the best opportunistic infection prophylaxis. No antibiotic prevents as many OIs as suppressing HIV replication and raising the CD4 count.

    Background

    Before effective ART, Pneumocystis pneumonia and disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex disease were leading causes of hospitalisation in AIDS. In the ART era the absolute number of OIs has fallen, but the conditions still define AIDS and drive mortality when diagnosis is late — which is why screening at entry to care matters even when the patient looks well.

    The CD4 count is still your compass

    The CD4 count predicts which OIs to anticipate, even though the absolute number is not a wall — infections can occur above and below any cutoff. Use thresholds as a compass, not a rule.

    • Less than 200 cells/µL: risk rises for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, with higher risk below 100 cells/µL.
    • Less than 100 cells/µL: risk rises for Toxoplasma gondii encephalitis and disseminated cryptococcosis caused by Cryptococcus neoformans.
    • Less than 50 cells/µL: risk rises for disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) disease and cytomegalovirus (CMV) end-organ disease.
    • Any CD4 count: tuberculosis caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis can occur, with higher risk and more atypical presentation at lower CD4 counts.

    Geography adds risk. Endemic fungi — Histoplasma capsulatum in the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys and Coccidioides immitis or Coccidioides posadasii in the southwestern United States — cause reactivation-like illness at low CD4 counts. Ask where the patient has lived and travelled.

    You will hear two CD4-related mistakes on rounds. One is relying on the percentage or viral load to decide screening; use the absolute CD4 count. The other is forgetting that ART changes everything quickly — once ART raises the CD4 count durably above thresholds, many prophylactic drugs can be stopped.

    Pearl

    Learn four CD4 landmarks: 200, 100, 50, and any count for tuberculosis. They tell you which prophylaxis and screening tests to pull off the shelf.

    Common pitfall

    Do not delay ART to finish an opportunistic infection work-up in a patient without active OI symptoms. Screening tests and ART can start together. The exception is when you suspect cryptococcal meningitis or tuberculous meningitis — evaluate before starting ART and get expert input on timing.

    What to screen for at entry to care — and what not to chase

    Every person newly diagnosed with HIV needs a baseline OI screen regardless of CD4 count, because you often do not know the CD4 when you first meet them. Repeat targeted screening when the CD4 falls below a new threshold or a new exposure occurs.

    Pearl

    Screen once well at entry to care, then screen again only when the CD4 crosses a new threshold. A negative Toxoplasma IgG at CD4 350 cells/µL does not need rechecking next month — it matters when the count approaches 100 cells/µL.

    Your new patient has a CD4 count of 42 cells/µL and no headache, fever, or visual changes. Which screening step is most important today?

    Cryptococcal antigen screening is recommended when the CD4 count is less than 100 cells/µL, and Toxoplasma serology and tuberculosis screening belong at entry to care. CMV PCR is not routine screening in asymptomatic patients, and waiting for viral suppression misses the window where screening prevents disease. A positive cryptococcal antigen needs meningitis evaluation before ART timing is set.

    Primary prophylaxis: who gets it, what to give, and when to stop

    Primary prophylaxis means giving an antimicrobial to prevent a first episode of an OI. In the ART era its role is narrow and temporary: protect the patient while ART reconstitutes immunity, then stop when the CD4 recovers.

    The table deliberately keeps doses out of short-term memory. Doses for TMP-SMX, dapsone, and atovaquone have not changed often, but the decision to start and stop matters more than the milligram number — and the milligram number should be verified in a current guideline or pharmacy reference before you prescribe. What you can remember is: TMP-SMX does double duty for Pneumocystis and Toxoplasma when the patient is seropositive, and atovaquone does not reliably prevent toxoplasmosis.

    Common pitfall

    Keeping prophylaxis forever “to be safe.” Continuing TMP-SMX or azithromycin after the CD4 count has been greater than 200 cells/µL for months on suppressive ART adds adverse effects, pill burden, and resistance without benefit. Put a stop date and a CD4 checkpoint in the plan.

    A patient with CD4 35 cells/µL, Toxoplasma IgG positive, starts ART today and tolerates TMP-SMX. What is the best prophylaxis plan?

    TMP-SMX covers both Pneumocystis (at CD4 less than 200) and Toxoplasma (seropositive and CD4 less than 100) and is stopped after durable CD4 recovery. Routine MAC prophylaxis is not added when ART is started promptly, and primary fluconazole for cryptococcosis is not recommended as CD4-based prophylaxis. ART alone on day one does not yet protect — prophylaxis bridges the gap.

    Pearl

    If the patient is on TMP-SMX and is Toxoplasma seropositive with CD4 less than 100 cells/µL, you already have toxoplasmosis prophylaxis — do not add a second agent for the same indication.

    Antiretroviral therapy first, but mind the timing and immune reconstitution

    For patients without an active opportunistic infection, start ART as soon as possible, ideally on the day of diagnosis or at the first follow-up, while screening tests are pending. You do not need to wait for Toxoplasma IgG or cryptococcal antigen results if the patient is asymptomatic. The faster the viral load falls and the CD4 rises, the sooner prophylaxis becomes unnecessary.

    When an active OI is present, timing matters and you should involve infectious diseases early. Two patterns to recognise:

    • For most OIs, including Pneumocystis pneumonia, early ART (within about 2 weeks of OI treatment) improves survival without overwhelming immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS).
    • For cryptococcal meningitis and tuberculous meningitis, immediate ART can worsen outcomes. ART is deliberately deferred for several weeks while antifungal or antituberculous therapy takes effect — the exact interval depends on the infection and on current guidelines. Do not start ART reflexively in a patient with headache, fever, and CD4 less than 100 cells/µL until meningitis has been excluded.

    IRIS is the clinical worsening of a known or subclinical OI as immunity recovers. It is not ART failure. It typically appears in the first weeks to months after ART initiation, most often in patients who started with very low CD4 counts. Warn patients about new fever, enlarging lymph nodes, worsening chest imaging, or eye symptoms after ART starts, and have a low threshold to re-evaluate. Management is treatment of the underlying OI, sometimes with anti-inflammatory therapy guided by specialists — not stopping ART.

    Background

    IRIS is most familiar with mycobacterial disease, cryptococcosis, and CMV retinitis, but it can occur with any OI. The risk is a reason to screen for cryptococcal antigen and tuberculosis before ART when CD4 is very low, so you are not surprised by a subclinical infection that declares itself on ART.

    A patient with CD4 28 cells/µL is diagnosed with cryptococcal meningitis today. When should ART start?

    Cryptococcal meningitis is the classic reason to defer ART. Early ART during active cryptococcal meningitis increases mortality and IRIS risk. Treat the meningitis first, then start ART after the recommended deferral interval — not immediately and not indefinitely.

    Vaccines and everyday prevention that also prevent opportunistic infections

    Prophylaxis is not only pills. Vaccines and simple counselling prevent OIs and other severe infections in people with HIV, and they are easy to miss when the CD4 count dominates the conversation.

    • Vaccines: ensure pneumococcal, influenza, hepatitis A and B, human papillomavirus (HPV), and COVID-19 vaccines are up to date per current adult immunisation schedules; recombinant zoster vaccine is recommended according to age and guidelines. Live vaccines are generally avoided when CD4 is less than 200 cells/µL — check before giving measles-mumps-rubella or varicella vaccines.
    • Tuberculosis: ask about exposure and symptoms at every visit when CD4 is low; treat latent infection and counsel on airborne precautions where relevant.
    • Toxoplasma prevention if seronegative: counsel to avoid raw or undercooked meat, wash fruits and vegetables, and avoid handling cat litter or use gloves and hand hygiene; no need for cat removal.
    • Cryptococcosis and endemic fungi: counsel to avoid bird droppings and, in endemic areas, disturbing soil or construction dust without precautions when CD4 is very low.
    • CMV: at CD4 less than 50 cells/µL, counsel on prompt ophthalmology evaluation for floaters, scotomata, or visual changes — CMV retinitis still blinds when missed. Routine fundoscopic screening without symptoms is no longer universally recommended where ART is available, but eye symptoms are urgent.
    • Sexual health and substance use: screen and treat sexually transmitted infections, offer harm reduction, and ensure ART adherence support — these reduce hospitalisation as much as any prophylaxis.

    Document vaccines and counselling in the problem list so the next resident does not reorder serologies that are already done. A line that reads “Toxoplasma IgG negative 08/2026, counselled; cryptococcal antigen negative at CD4 42” saves the next night team a repeat work-up.

    Pearl

    Counselling is prophylaxis. A 30-second conversation about food, litter boxes, and eye symptoms prevents the OI that no antibiotic could have covered.

    Which vaccine approach is correct for a patient with newly diagnosed HIV and CD4 42 cells/µL?

    Inactivated vaccines can and should be given when indicated even at low CD4 counts, though responses may be blunted and boosters may be needed after ART. Live vaccines are generally deferred when CD4 is less than 200 cells/µL because of replication risk. Deferring all vaccines leaves the patient unprotected against pneumococcus and influenza, which kill more often than the rare OIs we prophylax against.

    The five-minute version

    • Think CD4 thresholds: less than 200 for Pneumocystis, less than 100 for Toxoplasma and cryptococcal screening, less than 50 for MAC and CMV risk, and any count for tuberculosis.
    • Screen at entry to care: Toxoplasma IgG, cryptococcal antigen if CD4 less than 100 cells/µL, tuberculosis screening, plus hepatitis and syphilis work-up. Repeat only when CD4 falls to a new threshold.
    • Start primary prophylaxis to bridge to ART: TMP-SMX for Pneumocystis (and toxoplasmosis if seropositive and CD4 less than 100); do not add routine MAC or cryptococcal prophylaxis when ART is started promptly.
    • Start ART promptly if no active OI; defer for cryptococcal or tuberculous meningitis and involve infectious diseases. Warn about immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) in the first months.
    • Stop prophylaxis when the CD4 count is durably recovered on ART — typically greater than 200 cells/µL for at least 3 months. Put the stop condition in the plan on day one.
    • Vaccinate, counsel on food and animal exposures, and arrange urgent eye assessment for visual symptoms when CD4 less than 50 cells/µL.
