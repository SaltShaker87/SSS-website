    Pounding out penicillin allergies

    Sorting true allergy from lingering label — and why fixing the label changes outcomes

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 34-year-old woman is admitted with methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia from cellulitis. Her chart lists "penicillin allergy — rash as child." The overnight team avoids cefazolin and orders vancomycin plus aztreonam. She has no record of what the rash looked like, when it occurred, or whether she has taken amoxicillin since. You wonder: is this label helping her, or harming her?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Explain why an unverified penicillin allergy label leads to worse clinical outcomes.
    • Take a focused allergy history that distinguishes immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated, delayed, and non-allergic reactions.
    • Apply risk stratification to decide who can be delabelled by direct oral challenge versus who needs specialty referral or strict avoidance.
    • Counsel patients on cephalosporin and carbapenem cross-reactivity and document delabelling so the label stays off.

    Why the label matters more than you think

    About one in ten people in the United States reports a penicillin allergy, yet when formally tested, more than 90% can tolerate penicillins. Most labels date to childhood and were never clarified. The label itself behaves like a chronic exposure: every future prescription is filtered through it.

    Patients with the label receive more fluoroquinolones, clindamycin, vancomycin, and aztreonam and fewer narrow beta-lactams. Cohort studies show higher rates of Clostridioides difficile infection, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) colonisation or infection, longer stay, higher costs, and more surgical site infections when cefazolin is avoided.

    For the woman in the vignette the difference is concrete: cefazolin is first-line for methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteremia, while vancomycin is less rapidly bactericidal and aztreonam has no gram-positive activity. The allergy history is now the decisive data point.

    Pearl

    Treat the allergy label as a vital sign. Reconcile it like you reconcile medications — because it determines which medications the patient will actually receive.

    Background

    After ten years without exposure, about 80% of patients with prior IgE-mediated penicillin allergy lose sensitivity, so a childhood rash 20 years ago that was never rechallenged is weak evidence of current allergy.

    What counts as an allergy — and what does not

    Not every adverse drug reaction is an allergy. Sorting the phenotype is the first step, because each phenotype leads to a different action.

    Two confounders are frequently mislabelled: a maculopapular rash during mononucleosis treated with ampicillin, or a rash during a childhood viral illness. Gastrointestinal upset alone is an intolerance, not an allergy, yet often restricts therapy for decades when coded as allergy.

    Expand every abbreviation the first time you use it, and use the same discipline when you document. "Nausea" is not "allergy" — write "adverse effect: nausea" so the next team is not misled.

    Common pitfall

    Do not accept "penicillin allergy" without details. A chart entry that says only "allergy" with no reaction, date, or severity guarantees that every future prescriber will choose the more toxic alternative.

    How to take a penicillin allergy history that actually helps

    The allergy history is a procedure. In two minutes you can collect what an allergist needs if you use the same order every time.

    1. When? Age at reaction and how many years ago. "As a child" 25 years ago is very different from "last month."
    2. What drug and route? Penicillin G, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, piperacillin-tazobactam — and whether it was oral or intravenous. Ask specifically about amoxicillin, the most common culprit in outpatient rashes.
    3. What exactly happened? Let the patient describe in their own words, then clarify: hives or flat red rash? Itch? Swelling of lips or tongue? Wheeze or shortness of breath? Mucosal sores or blistering? Fever, joint pain, or jaundice?
    4. How quickly after the dose? Minutes to hours suggests immediate hypersensitivity; days later suggests delayed exanthem. A reaction on day 8 of a 10-day course is rarely IgE-mediated.
    5. How was it treated and how long did it last? Emergency department visit, epinephrine (adrenaline), antihistamines, steroids, hospitalisation? Did hives resolve in hours or rash last for days after stopping?
    6. What happened on re-exposure, if any? Many patients have taken a penicillin or cephalosporin since and forgotten. "Have you taken amoxicillin, Augmentin, Keflex, or any antibiotic for dental work since then?" is more effective than "have you had penicillin again?"
    7. Other drug allergies and atopy? Document separately; multiple labelled allergies increase suspicion for broadly labelled intolerances rather than true multiple allergies.

    Write the history as a sentence anyone can act on: "Age 4, amoxicillin for otitis, diffuse non-itchy macules on day 7, no hives or angioedema, no breathing difficulty, resolved in 3 days without treatment, no re-exposure, now age 34." That one sentence is enough to stratify.

    Pearl

    If the patient cannot remember, say so and note the uncertainty. "Patient unable to recall rash morphology; history reported as childhood rash 30 years ago, no medical records, no known re-exposure" is more useful than "penicillin allergy — unknown."

    A 28-year-old man reports penicillin allergy: at age 6 he had 2 days of loose stools while taking amoxicillin, no rash, no swelling, no breathing difficulty, and has not taken penicillin since. How should this history be classified?

    Isolated gastrointestinal upset without rash, hives, angioedema, or respiratory symptoms is an intolerance, not an immune-mediated allergy. Labelling it as allergy would deny the patient first-line therapy for no benefit. Document it as an adverse effect and remove the allergy label after counseling.

    Risk stratification: who is low risk and what it unlocks

    Once you have a history, stratify. The purpose is not to assign a perfect probability but to choose a safe next step: direct oral challenge under observation, specialist referral for skin testing, or strict avoidance.

    Risk strata for reported penicillin allergy
    StratumTypical historiesUsual next step
    Low riskRemote (>10 years) mild maculopapular rash without hives, angioedema, or systemic symptoms; isolated childhood rash with no recollection of features; remote itching without rash; mild delayed rash without mucosal involvement, blistering, or organ injury; gastrointestinal or other non-immune symptoms only; family history onlyDirect oral challenge after shared decision-making and informed consent, in a monitored setting where anaphylaxis can be treated; no skin testing required for very low risk
    Moderate riskRecent (within 5–10 years) hives or angioedema without airway compromise; immediate hypersensitivity where timing suggests IgE-mediated but without severe features or hemodynamic instabilityReferral for penicillin skin testing (prick followed by intradermal testing with major and minor determinants) followed by oral challenge if skin testing is negative and no contraindication exists
    High risk or contraindication to challengeHistory of anaphylaxis with hypotension, laryngeal edema, or bronchospasm; SJS, TEN, DRESS, AGEP, or other severe cutaneous adverse reaction; organ injury such as hemolytic anemia, nephritis, or hepatitis attributed to penicillin; any severe delayed hypersensitivity requiring hospitalisationDo not challenge or skin test. Permanent avoidance, detailed documentation, evaluate safe alternatives, and refer to allergy or dermatology for counseling and labeling guidance.

    One validated tool that operationalises this judgment is PEN-FAST (reported penicillin allergy, five years or less since reaction, anaphylaxis or angioedema, severe cutaneous reaction, and treatment required for reaction). In derivation and validation cohorts, a PEN-FAST score of 0 identified very low risk with a high negative predictive value; scores of 1–2 were low risk, and scores of 3 or higher were moderate to high risk. It does not replace clinical judgment, but it gives you a common language with allergy colleagues and a threshold for direct challenge.

    Background

    PEN-FAST was studied in adults in whom an oral penicillin challenge was performed under observation. Like any prediction rule, it performs best when the history you feed it is accurate — which is why the structured history matters more than the score itself.

    Pearl

    Recency and severity drive risk. A mild rash 30 years ago and hives 3 weeks ago are not the same, even if both are coded as "penicillin allergy" in the electronic health record (EHR).

    A 45-year-old woman reports a diffuse, mildly itchy maculopapular rash on day 5 of amoxicillin at age 12, no hives, no angioedema, no mucosal lesions, no breathing difficulty, no hospitalisation, no re-exposure since. PEN-FAST score is 0. What is the most appropriate next step in a monitored inpatient setting after counseling?

    This is a classic low-risk delayed exanthem. Current practice parameters support direct oral challenge without prior skin testing for very low-risk histories when performed where anaphylaxis can be managed and after shared decision-making. Skin testing is reserved for moderate-risk immediate hypersensitivity, not for every label. Desensitization is for patients who must receive the drug despite true allergy, not for delabelling.

    Delabelling in practice: challenge, skin testing, and making it stick

    Delabelling is not a favour to the chart; it restores the best therapy. For inpatients, the opportunity is timely: you have monitoring, intravenous access, and time to observe.

    Direct oral challenge. For low-risk patients, many programmes offer a supervised oral amoxicillin challenge with 60 minutes of observation for immediate symptoms and instructions to report delayed rash in the next 24–72 hours. Follow your institutional protocol for dosing and observation. Requirements are the same everywhere: informed consent, a setting where anaphylaxis can be treated with epinephrine and airway support, clear stopping criteria, and follow-up.

    Penicillin skin testing. For moderate-risk immediate hypersensitivity, skin testing uses prick then intradermal testing with the major determinant (penicilloyl polylysine) and minor determinants, followed by oral challenge if negative. Access is limited and sensitivity imperfect, but it allows delabelling when direct challenge is not appropriate. Do not use it for severe delayed reactions.

    Making it stick. The most common failure is not medical but administrative: the challenge is negative, but the electronic health record (EHR) still says "penicillin allergy." Complete these steps before the patient leaves:

    • Remove or update the allergy entry. Many systems distinguish "allergy" from "intolerance / adverse effect." Use the correct category.
    • Add a dated note describing the history, the test performed, the result, and that the patient tolerated a therapeutic dose without reaction.
    • Counsel the patient plainly: "You took amoxicillin under observation without reaction. You are not penicillin-allergic. Show this note to future clinicians." Provide a wallet card or summary when available.
    • Communicate with the primary team and pharmacist so prophylaxis and empiric choices change the same day.
    Common pitfall

    Never challenge a patient with a history of SJS, TEN, DRESS, AGEP, or other severe delayed hypersensitivity, even if the reaction was years ago. These are T-cell–mediated and can recur more severely. That label stays.

    Pearl

    Consent for delabelling is a conversation, not a form. Explain why you are doing it, what you will watch for, and that the goal is to make the next infection easier — and safer — to treat.

    A 62-year-old man with reported penicillin-induced anaphylaxis 2 years ago (hypotension, wheeze, epinephrine in the emergency department) now needs evaluation for possible penicillin allergy. What is the appropriate approach?

    Recent anaphylaxis with hypotension and bronchospasm is high risk. Direct oral challenge without specialist evaluation would be unsafe. The correct step is avoidance and referral; allergy may consider skin testing with subsequent challenge in a highly controlled setting if indicated, but this is not a bedside delabelling case.

    What about cephalosporins, carbapenems, and the cross-reactivity myth

    Many clinicians avoid all beta-lactams when the chart says "penicillin allergy," but the data no longer support that reflex.

    Cross-reactivity is driven by similar R1 side chains, not the beta-lactam ring itself. Early 10% estimates reflected contaminated early cephalosporin preparations. Contemporary risk is around 1–2% for cephalosporins and below 1% for carbapenems. The monobactam aztreonam does not cross-react except with ceftazidime, which shares its R1 side chain.

    For the vignette this matters now: vancomycin plus aztreonam leaves MSSA bacteremia inadequately treated. A structured history plus a plan for same-admission delabelling — or a supervised challenge where cefazolin is clearly indicated — is higher-value than defaulting to broader, less effective therapy.

    Pearl

    Cefazolin is not "a penicillin." Know its side chain. In many hospitals, a low-risk penicillin allergy is not a reason to withhold cefazolin for prophylaxis or MSSA therapy when the alternative is vancomycin.

    Background

    When a patient with true IgE-mediated allergy must receive the same beta-lactam (for example, penicillin G for neurosyphilis or enterococcal endocarditis with no alternative), desensitization in a monitored setting — not a delabelling challenge — is the pathway. Delabelling asks "are you allergic?"; desensitization creates temporary tolerance because the drug is required.

    A 58-year-old man with a remote childhood maculopapular rash to amoxicillin (no hives, no angioedema, 40 years ago) needs cefazolin for total knee arthroplasty prophylaxis. No re-exposure since. What is the best counsel for the surgical team?

    This remote, mild delayed rash is low risk. Cefazolin does not share an R1 side chain with amoxicillin and is the first-line prophylactic agent for MSSA coverage. Aztreonam has no meaningful gram-positive activity and vancomycin is inferior for MSSA prophylaxis when cefazolin can be given. Formal delabelling can follow, but prophylaxis should not default to inferior therapy on the basis of this label.

    The five-minute version

    • About 10% of patients report penicillin allergy, but more than 90% can tolerate penicillins when tested; the label drives higher rates of Clostridioides difficile, MRSA, VRE, longer stay, and surgical site infection when cefazolin is withheld.
    • Classify the phenotype first: immediate IgE-mediated (minutes to hours, hives, angioedema, anaphylaxis), delayed mild exanthem versus severe cutaneous adverse reaction (SJS, TEN, DRESS, AGEP), or non-allergic intolerance (nausea, diarrhea, family history alone).
    • Take a structured history every time — when, which drug, what happened, how quickly, how treated, and what happened on re-exposure — and document it as a sentence the next clinician can act on.
    • Stratify: low-risk histories (remote mild rash, childhood rash with no severe features, intolerance) are candidates for direct oral challenge under observation; moderate-risk recent hives warrants skin testing then challenge if negative; high-risk anaphylaxis or any SJS, TEN, DRESS means never challenge and permanent avoidance.
    • Cross-reactivity is side-chain specific and much lower than traditionally taught — about 1–2% for cephalosporins and below 1% for carbapenems; cefazolin is uniquely suited for MSSA and prophylaxis and is often appropriate even in low-risk penicillin-labelled patients.
    • Delabelling only counts if you close the loop: remove or correct the allergy entry in the electronic health record (EHR), add a dated note of what was done and tolerated, counsel the patient, and give them documentation to carry forward.
