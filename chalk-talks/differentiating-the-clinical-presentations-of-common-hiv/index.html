    Differentiating the clinical presentations of common HIV-associated pulmonary infections

    How tempo, CD4 count, and chest imaging separate Pneumocystis, bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, and fungal disease

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 38-year-old man with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), off antiretroviral therapy (ART) for 18 months, presents with 3 weeks of progressive dyspnoea, dry cough, and low-grade fever. Last cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) T-cell count was 42 cells/mm3 two years ago. He is tachypnoeic, oxygen saturation 88% on room air, and chest radiograph shows diffuse bilateral interstitial infiltrates without focal consolidation. He asks if antibiotics will treat this.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Use CD4 T-cell count, tempo, and antiretroviral therapy status to rank human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-associated pulmonary infections.
    • Distinguish the patterns of bacterial pneumonia, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, tuberculosis, and disseminated fungal disease.
    • Choose an evaluation and isolation strategy that does not miss tuberculosis while pursuing the most likely pathogen.

    Start with two questions: CD4 count and tempo

    Two questions narrow the differential faster than any test: what is the CD4 T-cell count, and how quickly did the syndrome evolve? The count tells you which opportunistic infections are plausible; tempo tells you which behave this way. Add ART status, prophylaxis, and prior opportunistic infection history and you have a working differential at the bedside.

    Risk stratifies by CD4 count. Above 500 cells/mm3 the spectrum resembles HIV-negative adults and bacterial pneumonia predominates. At 200–500 cells/mm3 bacterial pneumonia and tuberculosis (TB) remain common and Pneumocystis jirovecii enters when prophylaxis lapses. Below 200 cells/mm3, especially below 100 cells/mm3, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), cryptococcal disease, and endemic fungi appear. Below 50 cells/mm3 cytomegalovirus (CMV) and disseminated infection become plausible, often as co-pathogens.

    Tempo is the second axis: 1–5 days with productive sputum and pleuritic pain favours bacterial pneumonia; 2–4 weeks of progressive dyspnoea and dry cough favours PJP; weeks to months of fever, night sweats, and weight loss favours TB or fungal disease. Anchor on tempo and CD4 count before the image.

    Ask three contextual questions every time: ART adherence and HIV viral load, prophylaxis including trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), and exposures — smoking, injection use, incarceration, shelter, birthplace, travel, cave or bird-roost exposure, and residence for Histoplasma capsulatum or Coccidioides immitis.

    Pearl

    Lead with CD4 count and tempo, not the radiograph. The same bilateral infiltrate means something different at CD4 600 over 2 days than at CD4 30 over 3 weeks.

    Bacterial pneumonia: acute and lobar at any CD4 count

    Bacterial pneumonia is the most common pulmonary infection at every CD4 count, and risk rises as CD4 count falls. Recurrent bacterial pneumonia — two episodes in 12 months — is an acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)-defining condition. Usual pathogens are Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae; Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are more likely with health-care exposure or injection drug use.

    Expect 1–5 days of productive purulent sputum, fever, tachycardia, pleuritic pain, and focal crackles. Leukocytosis is typical but may be blunted at low CD4 counts. Chest radiograph (CXR) usually shows focal lobar or segmental consolidation, sometimes with effusion; diffuse disease is uncommon unless severe. Computed tomography (CT) is reserved for complications or alternative diagnoses.

    Improvement within 48–72 hours of appropriate antibiotics supports the diagnosis. Persistent fever, hypoxaemia, or infiltrates should prompt reconsideration of PJP, TB, or a non-infectious mimic such as pulmonary oedema or Kaposi sarcoma. Sputum and blood cultures and pneumococcal antigen help when positive; negative results do not exclude pneumonia after antibiotics.

    Pearl

    Bacterial pneumonia remains commonest at every CD4 stratum. Exclude it honestly before leaping to a rare opportunist.

    A 45-year-old woman with HIV, CD4 380 cells/mm3 on ART, develops 2 days of productive cough, fever 38.9 °C, and right lower lobe consolidation while taking TMP-SMX. Most likely?

    Bacterial pneumonia occurs at any CD4 count, even on prophylaxis. Two days of productive sputum with lobar consolidation is classic bacterial. PJP is subacute with diffuse ground glass; MAC is subacute at CD4 below 50 cells/mm3.

    Pneumocystis pneumonia: the subacute diffuse interstitial syndrome

    Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia classically occurs with CD4 below 200 cells/mm3, most often below 100 cells/mm3, when ART and prophylaxis have lapsed. Illness evolves over 2–4 weeks with progressive exertional dyspnoea, dry nonproductive cough, low-grade fever, and fatigue. Tachypnoea and hypoxaemia appear early; patients may look well at rest and desaturate only on exertion.

    The chest may be clear relative to hypoxaemia. CXR often shows diffuse bilateral perihilar interstitial infiltrates, sometimes with ground glass; pneumothorax is more common than in bacterial pneumonia. High-resolution CT shows diffuse ground-glass opacities with septal thickening, often sparing the periphery, sometimes with thin-walled cysts. A normal CXR does not exclude PJP — consider arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis and CT.

    Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and alveolar-arterial gradient are often increased but non-specific. Serum 1,3-beta-D-glucan is frequently positive in PJP and other fungi but is not diagnostic alone. Diagnosis requires polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) testing on induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid. Spontaneous pneumothorax at this CD4 count should trigger thought of PJP.

    Common pitfall

    Do not wait for haemoptysis or purulent sputum. The cough in PJP is usually dry; anchoring on 'no sputum, no pneumonia' delays testing.

    Pearl

    Measure oxygenation at rest and on exertion. A widening A-a gradient after a short walk in a patient with 3 weeks of dry cough is PJP until proven otherwise.

    A 34-year-old man with HIV, CD4 28 cells/mm3, 3 weeks of dry cough, dyspnoea, saturation 89% on room air, and diffuse interstitial infiltrates. What favours PJP over bacterial pneumonia?

    PJP is subacute, dry, with progressive hypoxaemia and diffuse ground glass; examination is often less dramatic than the oxygen deficit. Bacterial pneumonia is acute, productive, and focal.

    Tuberculosis: presentation shifts with immunity

    Tuberculosis caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis occurs at any CD4 count, but appearance changes as immunity wanes. Above about 350 cells/mm3 TB resembles HIV-negative disease: productive cough over weeks, fevers, night sweats, haemoptysis, and classic posteroapical cavities or upper-lobe infiltrates, often with positive acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smears.

    Below 200 cells/mm3, especially below 100 cells/mm3, cavitation becomes less common because it requires intact granulomas. Instead you see lower-lobe infiltrates, miliary nodules, intrathoracic lymphadenopathy, effusions, and more smear-negative and extrapulmonary disease. Fever and weight loss may dominate. A normal CXR does not exclude pulmonary TB in advanced HIV.

    Keep TB in the differential for any chronic respiratory syndrome until deliberately excluded, and think about infectiousness early: obtain sputum AFB smear, nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), and mycobacterial culture, and use airborne isolation when TB is plausible. An interferon-gamma release assay does not test for active TB. Prior exposure, birth in a high-prevalence region, homelessness, incarceration, and contacts raise probability.

    Pearl

    TB without cavities is still TB. At low CD4 expect non-cavitary, miliary, nodal, or effusive disease with more false-negative smears.

    Background

    As CD4 function falls, granulomas are fewer and poorly organised. Caseating cavities give way to diffuse infiltrates and haematogenous spread, so smear yield falls and NAAT plus culture become decisive.

    A 29-year-old man with HIV, CD4 45 cells/mm3, 6 weeks of fever and weight loss with miliary nodules and mediastinal nodes, no cavities, smear negative. Next step?

    At CD4 below 100 cells/mm3 TB is often smear-negative and non-cavitary. NAAT and culture are essential; isolation continues during evaluation. Interferon-gamma release assays do not diagnose active TB.

    Disseminated opportunists and regional fungi

    When infiltrates accompany prolonged fever, weight loss, anaemia, and pancytopenia at CD4 below 100 cells/mm3, think disseminated disease. Pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex, usually below 50 cells/mm3, presents as disseminated infection — fever, night sweats, anaemia, raised alkaline phosphatase, with nodular or interstitial infiltrates or adenopathy. The lung is rarely the only site; mycobacterial blood cultures are more useful than sputum alone.

    Cryptococcal disease caused by Cryptococcus neoformans at CD4 below 100 cells/mm3 may show nodules or interstitial infiltrates, but clues are often extrathoracic — headache, altered mentation, or positive serum cryptococcal antigen. Consider lumbar puncture when neurologic symptoms exist.

    Endemic fungi depend on geography. Histoplasma capsulatum in the Ohio-Mississippi valleys and Coccidioides immitis in the southwest can cause reticulonodular, cavitary, miliary, or solitary nodules with adenopathy at CD4 below 150 cells/mm3. Travel, residence, cave or bird-roost, and desert-dust exposure are the key questions. Antigen testing supports but does not replace culture and histology.

    Cytomegalovirus pneumonitis at CD4 below 50 cells/mm3 is rarely an isolated lung disease and often accompanies retinitis, colitis, or viraemia; CMV in BAL fluid alone often reflects shedding.

    Background

    Not every nodule is infection. Pulmonary Kaposi sarcoma (human herpesvirus 8) and lymphoma can mimic pneumonia with nodules, peribronchovascular thickening, and effusions. Violaceous skin or oral lesions, painless adenopathy, and haemorrhagic effusions broaden the differential.

LDH and beta-D-glucan support PJP but are non-specific; serum cryptococcal antigen is sensitive for disseminated cryptococcosis; urine Histoplasma antigen helps in endemic areas.     Pearl

    Hypoxaemia plus diffuse ground glass at CD4 below 200 is PJP until proven otherwise — but obtain TB NAAT in the same pass, because both thrive in that stratum.

    A 40-year-old man with HIV, CD4 18 cells/mm3, 4 weeks of fever and cough has diffuse ground glass with septal thickening. LDH is high, no pleuritic pain. Best statement?

    Ground glass favours PJP at very low CD4, but TB, fungi, and bacterial co-infection overlap. Imaging and LDH are clues, not confirmations; microbiology and isolation still apply.

    Evaluation, isolation, and empiric decisions while you wait

    Examine and monitor continuously: respiratory rate, oximetry at rest and on exertion, and skin, mouth, and nodes for Kaposi lesions, thrush, or adenopathy. Draw current CD4 count and viral load. Check ABG when saturation is below 94% or PJP is considered; an increased A-a gradient quantifies severity and informs steroid decisions for PJP.

    For subacute syndromes obtain sputum for bacterial culture, AFB smear, NAAT for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and mycobacterial culture. Add induced sputum or BAL for Pneumocystis PCR or DFA when PJP is considered. Send blood cultures including mycobacterial cultures when CD4 is below 50 cells/mm3, cryptococcal antigen when below 100 cells/mm3, and urine Histoplasma antigen in endemic areas. Obtain CXR; reserve CT for ambiguous films, suspected nodules or cavities, or mismatch between syndrome and film.

    Isolation is a safety decision. If TB is plausible — weeks of cough, fever, weight loss, cavitary, miliary, nodal, or effusive pattern, or epidemiologic risk — place in airborne isolation until NAAT and smears argue against TB. Stopping isolation prematurely risks ward exposure.

    Empiric therapy follows the leading syndrome and local protocols. Focal consolidation favours prompt antibacterials per community-acquired pneumonia guidance. Classic PJP favours prompt PJP therapy while securing sputum or BAL and involving respiratory and infectious diseases. Suspected TB favours isolation and mycobacterial diagnostics before routine bronchoscopy. Arrange HIV follow-up, ART counselling, and prophylaxis review; pulmonary disease is often the moment ART is restarted, with attention to immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome with TB or cryptococcal disease.

    Common pitfall

    A single negative NAAT or smear does not lift TB isolation when suspicion remains high. Smear-negative TB is common in advanced HIV.

    The five-minute version

    • Ask CD4 count now and tempo now: days favours bacterial pneumonia; weeks favours Pneumocystis, TB, or fungal disease; months favours TB or disseminated opportunists.
    • Bacterial pneumonia is commonest at every CD4 count — acute productive cough, focal consolidation, rapid antibiotic response.
    • Consider PJP when CD4 is below 200 cells/mm3 with weeks of dry cough, exertional hypoxaemia, and diffuse ground glass; assess oxygenation at rest and on exertion.
    • TB can present without cavities and with negative smears at low CD4 — miliary, nodal, or effusive patterns and extrapulmonary spread are clues; keep TB in the differential always.
    • Below 50–100 cells/mm3 think disseminated MAC, cryptococcosis, and endemic fungi such as Histoplasma or Coccidioides, and broaden to Kaposi sarcoma or lymphoma for nodules and effusions.
    • Isolate for TB when plausible, obtain NAAT plus PJP diagnostics in the same pass, and use imaging as pattern confirmation — not a replacement for microbiology and current CD4 count.
