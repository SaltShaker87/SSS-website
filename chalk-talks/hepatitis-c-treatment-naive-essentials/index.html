    Hepatitis C treatment-naïve essentials

    Who to treat, what to check, and how to choose and monitor pangenotypic therapy

    Case

    A 32-year-old man is referred after routine screening showed hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody positive. He has never taken HCV therapy, injects drugs occasionally, drinks 2–3 beers on weekends, and takes no regular medicines. Labs: complete blood count and creatinine normal, alanine aminotransferase 68, aspartate aminotransferase 42, bilirubin and international normalised ratio normal. He asks if he needs a biopsy, genotype, and how long treatment lasts.

    Why treatment is offered to every adult with hepatitis C

    Chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection — detectable HCV ribonucleic acid (RNA) beyond 6 months — is now curable in more than 95% of treatment-naïve adults with 8 to 12 weeks of oral direct-acting antivirals (DAAs). Cure, or sustained virologic response (SVR), means undetectable RNA 12 weeks after therapy and lowers cirrhosis progression, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver death, and transmission. AASLD/IDSA guidance therefore recommends treatment for virtually all adults except those with a very limited life expectancy not improved by cure.

    Watchful waiting for mild disease is obsolete. Treatment is warranted even with minimal fibrosis to prevent hepatic and extrahepatic complications such as cryoglobulinaemia. For residents the question is no longer “does this patient need treatment?” but “is there a modifiable reason to defer today?” Reasons to defer or refer are pregnancy, decompensated cirrhosis, active hepatocellular carcinoma, and unmanageable drug interactions. Active injection drug use alone is not a reason to withhold therapy if the person can adhere.

    Pearl

    Treat chronic hepatitis C in almost everyone. Active drug use, mild transaminase elevation, or a normal physical exam are not reasons to delay — decompensated cirrhosis, pregnancy, and untreated hepatocellular carcinoma are.

    Confirm active infection and stage the liver

    Antibody alone does not prove current infection — about one quarter clear hepatitis C spontaneously and remain antibody positive. Confirm active infection with a quantitative HCV RNA. If undetectable, no treatment now; if detectable, stage without a routine biopsy.

    Staging asks if cirrhosis is present and if it is compensated. Calculate a fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) from age, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and platelets; a low FIB-4 makes advanced fibrosis unlikely, a high value triggers elastography or ultrasound. Ascites, oedema, jaundice, encephalopathy, low platelets or albumin, high bilirubin, or prolonged international normalised ratio suggest advanced disease. Transient elastography gives a rapid noninvasive estimate when available.

    Complete the panel at the same visit: hepatitis B virus surface antigen, surface antibody and core antibody; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antigen/antibody; hepatitis A virus antibody for vaccination; pregnancy test when relevant; complete blood count, metabolic panel with estimated glomerular filtration rate, bilirubin; and a full medication and supplement list including proton pump inhibitors, statins, hormonal contraception, and anti-epileptics. Genotype is not needed for pangenotypic choice in the simplified pathway — follow local protocol.

    Pearl

    Antibody proves exposure, RNA proves active infection. Stage the liver with FIB-4, platelets, exam, and elastography — not a routine biopsy — and get hepatitis B virus serologies, HIV, pregnancy testing, and a full drug list before you prescribe.

    The man in the vignette has positive HCV antibody. What is the essential next test before any treatment plan?

    HCV antibody persists after spontaneous clearance. You must demonstrate active viraemia with a quantitative HCV RNA before treatment. Genotype is not required for a pangenotypic simplified regimen, and biopsy is unnecessary for most treatment-naïve adults.

    Who fits the simplified treatment pathway

    The simplified pathway lets primary care cure most treatment-naïve adults without referral. An adult qualifies if 18 years or older, HCV RNA positive, never treated for hepatitis C, and without decompensated cirrhosis — no ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, encephalopathy, or variceal bleed, and no Child-Pugh B or C. There should be no known hepatocellular carcinoma, liver transplant, or hepatitis B surface antigen requiring active therapy, and no unmanageable drug interactions. Both non-cirrhotic and compensated cirrhosis (Child-Pugh A) fit, though some centres co-manage compensated cirrhosis — follow local rules.

    Who does not fit: decompensated cirrhosis or portal hypertension, indeterminate fibrosis needing workup, active hepatocellular carcinoma, prior direct-acting antiviral exposure, pregnancy, and complex HIV coinfection on interacting antiretrovirals. For these, refer to hepatology or infectious diseases rather than forcing a pangenotypic course. If the vignette patient has normal bilirubin, albumin, platelets and no stigmata, his FIB-4 will likely be low and he fits the simplified track — document that you checked for red flags.

    Common pitfall

    Do not start glecaprevir/pibrentasvir in decompensated cirrhosis — protease inhibitors are contraindicated in Child-Pugh B and C and can precipitate liver failure. If you suspect decompensation, stop, stage carefully, and refer; sofosbuvir/velpatasvir is the usual backbone when treatment is indicated in decompensation, often with specialist input and sometimes with ribavirin.

    Which patient belongs in the simplified pangenotypic pathway?

    Simplified treatment is for treatment-naïve adults without decompensated cirrhosis, without prior DAA failure, and without active hepatocellular carcinoma or pregnancy. Prior treatment failure, decompensation, and pregnancy all require specialty management.

    Choosing between the two pangenotypic regimens

    For treatment-naïve adults who fit the simplified pathway, guidance highlights two pangenotypic options that do not require genotype testing: glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (G/P) and sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL).     Pearl

    In the simplified pathway, genotype does not drive the choice — tolerability, drug interactions, renal function, and cirrhosis status do. Glecaprevir/pibrentasvir is 8 weeks with food; sofosbuvir/velpatasvir is 12 weeks once daily without a food requirement.

    Background

    Hepatitis C was once treated with 24–48 weeks of pegylated interferon and ribavirin; direct-acting antivirals targeting NS3/4A, NS5A and NS5B now cure more than 95% in weeks.

    Drug interactions and contraindications you must check

    Both regimens have major interactions via cytochrome P450 and transporters.

    With glecaprevir/pibrentasvir avoid strong inducers — rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, St John’s wort — which render therapy ineffective, and avoid atazanavir and ethinyl estradiol combinations because of alanine aminotransferase risk. Statins need action: hold rosuvastatin, limit atorvastatin, or switch to pravastatin with monitoring. Review anticoagulants with pharmacy.

    With sofosbuvir/velpatasvir, acid suppression lowers velpatasvir. If a proton pump inhibitor is essential, give sofosbuvir/velpatasvir with food 4 hours before the inhibitor at the lowest effective dose, or use an H2-receptor antagonist separated in time. Separate antacids by 4 hours. Strong inducers are contraindicated. Amiodarone with sofosbuvir can cause severe bradycardia — avoid. Two standalone contraindications: protease inhibitors are contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis, and hepatitis B virus reactivation can occur — obtain surface antigen, surface antibody and core antibody before therapy and manage positives before the first dose.

    Common pitfall

    Starting hepatitis C treatment without checking hepatitis B virus serologies. Reactivation has caused fulminant failure. Obtain surface antigen, surface antibody, and core antibody in every person before direct-acting antivirals, and act on a positive surface antigen or isolated core antibody before the first dose.

    A patient on carbamazepine for epilepsy and omeprazole daily is evaluated for treatment-naïve hepatitis C. What is the safest next step?

    Carbamazepine and other strong inducers are contraindicated with both glecaprevir/pibrentasvir and sofosbuvir/velpatasvir due to subtherapeutic levels. Proton pump inhibitors lower velpatasvir; timing, dose adjustment, or switch is needed. Discuss alternatives with neurology and pharmacy rather than pushing through.

    Monitoring on treatment and confirming cure

    No on-treatment RNA is needed to set duration. The essential test is HCV RNA 12 weeks after the last dose — SVR at 12 weeks (SVR12): undetectable means cured, detectable means refer for resistance and retreatment, not empiric extension.

    During therapy focus on adherence and side effects, take glecaprevir/pibrentasvir with food and separate sofosbuvir/velpatasvir from antacids, and recheck labs only if clinically indicated. Marked alanine aminotransferase rise with symptoms needs urgent review. With compensated cirrhosis, continue hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance with ultrasound with or without alpha-fetoprotein every 6 months after SVR — cure reduces but does not abolish risk. Non-cirrhotic patients need no routine elastography after SVR. Document start, end and SVR12 dates, and if more than 7 days are missed seek specialty input. Schedule SVR12 before starting so follow-up is not lost.

    Pearl

    Cure is defined by undetectable HCV RNA at 12 weeks after the last dose. Do not use end-of-treatment RNA to declare success, and do not add extra weeks because you are anxious — schedule SVR12 before you start.

    Counseling, vaccination, and preventing reinfection

    DAAs do not confer immunity — reinfection occurs with ongoing injection use or high-risk sexual exposure. Discuss harm reduction without stigma, offer clean equipment where legal, medication for opioid use disorder, and partner testing. Antibody stays positive for life, so only RNA can detect reinfection.

    Advise minimal or no alcohol and offer support. Vaccinate for hepatitis A and B if non-immune and complete the series even after SVR. Adherence best predicts cure: link dosing to a daily habit, explain food and antacid rules, give a written plan and a missed-dose action, and start prior authorisation early because access, not biology, most often delays cure.

    Pearl

    Antibody stays positive after cure — you cannot use it to check for reinfection. Tell patients to return for HCV RNA testing if they have a new exposure, and keep hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance every 6 months if they had cirrhosis before treatment.

    Background

    Liver biopsy is rarely needed now — FIB-4, elastography and exam stage most treatment-naïve adults.

    A patient completes 8 weeks of glecaprevir/pibrentasvir and asks when you can confirm he is cured. What do you tell him?

    SVR is defined by undetectable HCV RNA at 12 weeks after therapy ends. End-of-treatment RNA and antibody do not define cure, and FIB-4 monitors fibrosis, not virologic response.

    The five-minute version

    • Treat almost every treatment-naïve adult with chronic hepatitis C (detectable HCV RNA); confirm RNA, do not treat antibody alone, and defer only for pregnancy, decompensated cirrhosis, active hepatocellular carcinoma, or unmanageable interactions.
    • Complete a focused pre-treatment panel at the first visit: HCV RNA, hepatitis B virus surface antigen/core antibody/surface antibody, HIV, pregnancy test, hepatitis A immunity, metabolic panel, complete blood count, FIB-4 with exam and elastography if needed, and a full drug list reviewed with pharmacy.
    • Use the simplified pangenotypic pathway for treatment-naïve adults without decompensated cirrhosis: glecaprevir/pibrentasvir 8 weeks with food or sofosbuvir/velpatasvir 12 weeks once daily — both guideline-derived doses, pangenotypic without routine genotype, and verified locally.
    • Check interactions and contraindications every time: never use glecaprevir/pibrentasvir in decompensated disease, avoid strong inducers with both regimens, manage acid reducers and antacids with sofosbuvir/velpatasvir, and evaluate hepatitis B virus status for reactivation risk before the first dose.
    • Define cure by undetectable HCV RNA 12 weeks after the last dose (SVR12), continue hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance every 6 months if cirrhosis was present before treatment, vaccinate for hepatitis A and B if non-immune, and counsel on adherence and harm reduction because antibody remains positive and reinfection is possible.
