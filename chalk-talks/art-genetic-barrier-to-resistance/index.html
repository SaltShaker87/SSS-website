    Case

    A 26-year-old man with newly diagnosed HIV-1 infection is starting antiretroviral therapy (ART — medicines that block HIV replication). His genotype is pending, viral load 78,000 copies/mL, CD4 count 410 cells/mm3 (cluster of differentiation 4 positive T cells). He works nights and asks, "If I miss a dose, will the virus become resistant?"

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Define genetic barrier to resistance and distinguish it from potency and half-life.
    • Rank common ART classes and drugs as low, intermediate, or high barrier and name the key mutations that break them.
    • Choose an initial high-barrier regimen and protect it when adherence falters or virologic failure occurs.

    What we mean by genetic barrier

    Potency tells you how much virus a drug suppresses when the virus is susceptible. Genetic barrier to resistance tells you how hard it is for the virus to stop being susceptible. A high barrier means the virus must acquire several mutations together and usually loses replicative fitness to do so. A low barrier means a single base change is enough.

    Three things shape the barrier: how many mutations are required, whether one mutation alone causes high-level resistance, and how much those mutations cripple the virus. Barrier is a property of the drug and its target; the patient determines whether it is tested. Every day of HIV replication under drug pressure is a chance for a resistant variant to be selected and archived as proviral DNA in the latent reservoir for years.

    Pearl

    Low barrier is a one-step escape; high barrier is a three- or four-step puzzle the virus must solve at once while losing fitness at each step.

    Do not confuse barrier with forgiveness. Forgiveness is how late a dose can be before levels fall below the threshold that allows replication; it depends on half-life and barrier together. A long half-life with a low barrier can be harmful — drug lingers at subtherapeutic levels that select resistance, as with historic single-dose nevirapine for perinatal prevention.

    Which best describes genetic barrier to resistance?

    Viral load decay measures potency; time above threshold measures half-life and forgiveness. Barrier measures the mutational hurdle — why two equally potent drugs differ when doses are late.

    Why HIV is built to mutate under pressure

    Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) copies itself with reverse transcriptase, which has no proofreading function. It makes about one error per genome per cycle, and untreated infection produces billions of new virions daily. Without drugs, most mutants are less fit and are outcompeted by wild type.

    When ART is present but incomplete — missed doses, interactions, or adding one active drug to a failing regimen — the dynamic flips. Wild type is suppressed, but a pre-existing resistant variant that can replicate despite the drug gains a selective advantage, expands, and is archived in long-lived CD4 cells. Future perfect adherence will not erase it.

    Background

    We describe selection, not creation. The mutant often exists at low frequency before drug exposure. Suppressive levels prevent it from outgrowing wild type.

    Pearl

    Resistance signals replication under pressure. Undetectable viral load prevents it; detectable virus on therapy demands action.

    A regimen's barrier is the sum of its parts. Two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors plus a low barrier NNRTI is a low barrier regimen; the same backbone with dolutegravir or boosted darunavir is high barrier because the anchor dictates the outcome.

    Low-barrier drugs you will see fail first

    The familiar story is suppression for weeks, then a gap — hospitalisation, lost prescription, interacting antacid — and return with detectable viremia and a new genotype.

    Efavirenz falls to K103N: one base change removes binding with little fitness cost and confers cross-resistance to nevirapine. Rilpivirine falls to E138K, often with M184I when paired with emtricitabine because the two mutations compensate for each other's fitness effects. Either leaves the NNRTI class unusable and often compromises the cytosine analogue.

    Raltegravir and elvitegravir taught the same lesson via integrase: N155H or Q148H/K/R alone meaningfully reduces susceptibility, and a second change drives high-level cross-resistance. Elvitegravir's need for cobicistat boosting adds vulnerability — a cytochrome P450 inducer or missed dose exposes elvitegravir monotherapy, which a low barrier cannot tolerate.

    Common pitfall

    Restarting the same low barrier regimen after a gap without a fresh genotype. If resistance was selected during the gap, you are resuming a regimen the virus already knows how to beat. Pause, get advice, and do not add one new drug to "intensify" a failing regimen.

    Pearl

    M184V is the exception: it appears within weeks on lamivudine or emtricitabine yet it cripples replication and hyper-sensitises virus to tenofovir, which is why the backbone is often continued after M184V.

    Patient on efavirenz, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine misses doses three weeks, restarts, viral load 12,000 copies/mL. Most likely resistance?

    Efavirenz needs only K103N; emtricitabine selects M184V quickly. Protease mutations need protease exposure; Q148H affects integrase, not NNRTI. This regimen is low barrier.

    What makes a high barrier — dolutegravir, bictegravir and boosted darunavir

    Second generation INSTIs bind the integrase active site longer and make more contacts with viral DNA. One mutation around a single contact still leaves the drug bound at the others. In vitro they require serial passage with multiple substitutions to escape; in initial therapy trials, emergent integrase resistance was rare — zero to a handful across thousands of participants — when paired with two NRTIs in people without prior integrase exposure.

    Boosted darunavir works differently: the protease active site is highly constrained. Single changes are poorly tolerated because they impair recognition of the natural substrate. Darunavir retains binding despite common polymorphisms, so several mutations must accumulate. It remains active against virus resistant to older PIs, and its path to resistance is slow and stepwise.

    Pearl

    High barrier does not rescue functional monotherapy. Taking dolutegravir while stopping the backbone for side effects is dolutegravir monotherapy under replication — the barrier is undermined.

    Why do dolutegravir and bictegravir have higher barrier than raltegravir?

    Half-life helps adherence but does not change mutational requirement. Structural binding explains the difference: one integrase change impairs raltegravir substantially but dolutegravir or bictegravir only modestly.

    Choosing and protecting a regimen

    For most people starting without prior resistance, guidelines favour two NRTIs — tenofovir alafenamide or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate with emtricitabine (or lamivudine), or abacavir with lamivudine when human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701 is negative — plus a high barrier anchor: dolutegravir or bictegravir for most, or boosted darunavir when integrase resistance or interactions are a concern.

    Three actions on day one protect the regimen before you need forgiveness:

    • Check what you face: send baseline genotype including reverse transcriptase, protease, and integrase, and HLA-B*5701 if considering abacavir. Start promptly when ready, but adjust when results return — transmitted NNRTI or INSTI resistance, though uncommon, changes the plan.
    • Remove selection pressure gaps: counsel on the long tail of injectable cabotegravir plus rilpivirine and on interactions that lower levels — rifamycins, certain anticonvulsants, antacids or cations that chelate INSTIs. Space dosing from polyvalent cations and review medicines each visit.
    • Make adherence feasible: choose a regimen the patient can take. High barrier taken intermittently is still intermittent therapy. If missed doses are predicted, add supports — pillboxes, reminders, case management — rather than choosing a low barrier drug that seems simpler.
    Common pitfall

    Adding one new drug to a failing regimen. Adding dolutegravir alone to a failing backbone is functional dolutegravir monotherapy under replication — the fastest way to erode a high barrier. Keep the regimen, get a genotype on therapy, consult specialists, and rebuild with at least two fully active agents.

    When viral load becomes detectable on therapy, assess adherence without judgment, repeat the measurement, check for interactions or malabsorption, and obtain genotype while still on the failing regimen or shortly after stopping.

    Pearl

    Treat the first regimen as your best chance. Early genotype, high barrier anchor, and adherence support protect future options more than any salvage strategy.

    Virologically suppressed on bictegravir, tenofovir alafenamide and emtricitabine, patient asks to simplify. Old genotype two years ago shows no resistance. Safest step?

    Rilpivirine is low barrier and prior NNRTI failure may be archived and missed on old genotype. Tenofovir protects against hepatitis B flare. Bictegravir alone is inadequate. Simplification is reasonable when suppression is durable and prior failure excluded.

    When even a high barrier breaks

    No barrier is absolute. Failure follows prolonged replication under suboptimal pressure — often because one component was stopped while another continued, or levels stayed low due to interactions or malabsorption.

    Two-drug switch regimens illustrate the point. Dolutegravir plus lamivudine and dolutegravir plus rilpivirine work as switch strategies when viral load is already undetectable and prior resistance is excluded; they were not started during failure and not offered to those with known M184V or NNRTI mutations without input. Two drugs can be enough when replication is already controlled, not when the virus is actively replicating.

    Dolutegravir monotherapy maintenance failed more often than combination therapy, with emergent integrase resistance in some, because the virus needed to overcome one drug, not three. Any period of functional monotherapy carries the same risk.

    Background

    Archived resistance outlives plasma genotypes. A standard genotype sequences circulating virus and misses proviral DNA. Prior K103N will re-emerge under renewed NNRTI pressure even if current genotype appears wild type.

    Pearl

    If therapy must pause — for procedure or toxicity — stop all agents together and restart together when feasible. Staggered stops create sequential monotherapy tails that select resistance.

    The five-minute version

    • Genetic barrier is the number and fitness cost of mutations needed for escape — low is one step, high is several.
    • HIV makes errors constantly; resistance expands only when replication continues under drug pressure and is then archived.
    • NNRTIs, lamivudine or emtricitabine alone, and first generation INSTIs (raltegravir, elvitegravir) are low barrier; second generation INSTIs (dolutegravir, bictegravir) and boosted darunavir are high barrier.
    • Start two NRTIs plus a high barrier anchor for most, check baseline genotype including integrase, and address interactions and adherence barriers on day one.
    • Never add one active drug to a failing regimen, never continue single-drug monotherapy, and when failure occurs keep the regimen, obtain genotype promptly, and rebuild with at least two fully active agents.
