    Diagnostic reasoning

    Fungal biomarkers

    When galactomannan, beta-D-glucan, and fungal antigens help — and when they mislead

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 58-year-old man with acute myeloid leukaemia, day 14 after induction chemotherapy, remains neutropenic and febrile despite 72 hours of cefepime. He is on fluconazole prophylaxis. Chest computed tomography (CT) shows a new 1.8-cm nodule with surrounding ground glass in the right upper lobe. Serum galactomannan (GM) returns at 0.7 (optical density index), and (1→3)-β-D-glucan (BDG) at 95 pg/mL. The team asks whether this is proven aspergillosis and whether to change antifungals.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Describe what galactomannan (GM), (1→3)-β-D-glucan (BDG), cryptococcal antigen (CrAg), and Aspergillus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) actually measure.
    • Select the right biomarker for the right host and interpret results in the context of pretest probability.
    • List common causes of false-positive and false-negative biomarker results and how to avoid misinterpretation.
    • Apply a stewardship framework for when to order, when not to order, and when to repeat fungal biomarkers.

    Why we need fungal biomarkers at all

    Invasive fungal disease is hard to prove quickly. Blood cultures are insensitive for Aspergillus and other moulds, tissue biopsy is often unsafe in thrombocytopenic or critically ill patients, and cultures can take days to weeks. Meanwhile, empiric antifungals are toxic, expensive, and drive resistance. Biomarkers — circulating fungal cell-wall components, antigens, or nucleic acid — were developed to close that diagnostic gap, not to replace clinical judgement.

    All of them share the same problem: their performance depends almost entirely on pretest probability. In a high-risk host with a compatible syndrome, a positive result raises confidence meaningfully. In a low-risk host without a compatible syndrome, the same result is far more likely to be noise. The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSGERC) definitions make this explicit: biomarkers can support a diagnosis of probable invasive fungal disease only when host, clinical, and mycologic criteria align. A positive biomarker alone never makes a proven case.

    Keep three principles in mind throughout this talk. First, no biomarker diagnoses infection by itself. Second, sensitivity and specificity quoted from studies in haematology patients do not travel well to intensive care unit (ICU) or general medical patients. Third, drugs that suppress fungal growth will suppress biomarker release — mould-active prophylaxis or early therapy blunts galactomannan and BDG sensitivity.

    Background

    The EORTC/MSGERC definitions classify invasive fungal disease as proven (histopathology or culture from sterile site), probable (host factor plus clinical feature plus mycologic evidence, including biomarkers), or possible (host plus clinical without mycologic support). Biomarkers live in that middle, mycologic lane.

    Galactomannan — the aspergillus signal

    Galactomannan (GM) is a polysaccharide in the cell wall of Aspergillus species that is released during hyphal growth. The assay is an enzyme immunoassay reported as an optical density index (ODI). It does not detect Mucorales, Fusarium inconsistently, and Cryptococcus not at all.

    Interpretation depends on specimen. Serum GM is most studied in neutropenic patients with haematologic malignancy or haematopoietic stem-cell transplant where angioinvasion releases antigen into blood. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid GM is more sensitive than serum in non-neutropenic hosts and in patients already on mould-active agents, because antigen is concentrated at the site of pulmonary disease. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) GM has been used for suspected Aspergillus meningitis but is not standardised.

    Cutoffs commonly used in practice and in trial definitions are an ODI of 0.5 for serum positivity (some centres require two consecutive positives or 0.7 on a single sample) and 1.0 for BAL fluid positivity. Sensitivity in high-risk haematology cohorts is roughly 70 to 80 percent for proven or probable invasive aspergillosis, higher in BAL than serum, and specificity around 85 to 90 percent when prevalence is high. In low-risk populations, the positive predictive value collapses because false positives dominate.

    False positives still occur, although the classic piperacillin-tazobactam problem has largely resolved with modern formulations. Other causes include severe mucositis with gut translocation, receipt of certain nutritional or electrolyte solutions, and laboratory handling. False negatives are more common and more dangerous: concurrent mould-active prophylaxis with posaconazole, isavuconazole, or voriconazole, early sampling before angioinvasion, and non-Aspergillus moulds that simply do not make galactomannan.

    Pearl

    Galactomannan is not a screen for everyone with fever. It performs in neutropenic hosts with pulmonary disease and little else — outside that group, order it only to support a specific clinical question.

    Your neutropenic patient on posaconazole prophylaxis has a negative serum GM of 0.2 ODI. What best explains the result?

    Mould-active azoles suppress galactomannan release, so serum sensitivity falls from the 70 to 80 percent seen in untreated haematology patients to much lower. A single negative serum GM on prophylaxis does not exclude disease. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid GM retains higher sensitivity. There is no validated lower cutoff on prophylaxis, and daily serum repeats without a clinical change add noise without benefit.

    (1→3)-β-D-glucan — a broad but noisy fungal screen

    (1→3)-β-D-glucan (BDG) is a cell-wall polysaccharide found in most pathogenic fungi — Candida, Aspergillus, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and others — but notably absent in Cryptococcus and Mucorales, which have little BDG in their walls. The most widely used assay in the United States is the Fungitell assay, with a common positivity threshold around 80 pg/mL (with an indeterminate zone around 60 to 79 pg/mL). Thresholds vary by laboratory and assay, so check your own report.

    BDG is best understood as a pan-fungal marker with good negative predictive value and poor positive predictive value. Pooled sensitivity for invasive fungal disease is roughly 75 to 80 percent and specificity roughly 80 percent in high-risk cohorts, but specificity drops sharply in ICU patients. In other words, a negative BDG in a high-risk, untreated patient makes invasive candidiasis or aspergillosis less likely, while a single positive BDG should not by itself trigger therapy.

    The list of false positives is long and clinically relevant. Intravenous immunoglobulin, albumin, certain beta-lactams and haemodialysis membranes, surgical gauze exposure, and severe bacterial infection can all elevate BDG. Blood sampling through gauze or filters can contaminate specimens. Conversely, BDG is insensitive for cryptococcosis and mucormycosis by biology, not by assay failure, and can be low in early or localised disease.

    Common pitfall

    Ordering BDG as a daily screen in every ICU patient with fever. The pretest probability for invasive fungal disease in most ICU patients is low, so even a specificity of 80 to 85 percent generates far more false positives than true positives. Use BDG to support discontinuation of empiric therapy in high-risk patients or to bolster suspicion for Pneumocystis pneumonia, not as a fever workup panel.

    Pearl

    A negative BDG helps you stop. In high-risk patients started on empiric antifungals, two consecutive negative BDG results combined with negative cultures and stable imaging strengthen a decision to de-escalate — but only within the full clinical picture.

    Background

    Pneumocystis jirovecii produces abundant BDG. In human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-negative patients with diffuse ground-glass opacities where bronchoscopy is delayed, a markedly elevated BDG can support early presumptive therapy pending definitive testing. Cryptococcus and Mucorales remain the deliberate blind spots — a negative BDG never excludes them.

    A 64-year-old ICU patient on haemodialysis with bacteraemia due to Staphylococcus aureus has a BDG of 110 pg/mL without any pulmonary nodules or candidemia. What is the most appropriate next step?

    BDG is positive in only 10 to 20 percent of bacteraemic patients in some series, but haemodialysis and albumin are well-described false positives. Galactomannan does not detect Candida and cannot confirm candidiasis. A single elevated BDG without host risk, imaging, or culture support should not drive therapy — repeat testing amplifies the false-positive problem when prevalence is low.

    Cryptococcal antigen and endemic mycosis antigens

    Cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) is in a different league from GM and BDG for accuracy. The lateral flow assay (LFA) for cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide is highly sensitive and specific in both serum and CSF, approaching 99 percent in many studies, and has largely replaced the older latex agglutination test. A positive serum CrAg in a patient with meningitis or pulmonary nodules should prompt lumbar puncture unless contraindicated, and in people living with HIV with CD4 counts below 100 cells per microlitre, serum CrAg screening can detect subclinical cryptococcaemia before meningitis develops.

    Titres matter less than trajectory. CrAg can remain positive for months after successful treatment, so rising titres or persistently high titres do not by themselves define failure — symptoms, culture, and opening pressure matter more. False negatives are rare but occur with a prozone effect at very high antigen burdens (the laboratory must dilute) and with localised pulmonary disease where antigenaemia is limited.

    For endemic mycoses, urine and serum antigen enzyme immunoassays for Histoplasma capsulatum are valuable, particularly for disseminated disease. Sensitivity is highest in disseminated histoplasmosis (often 90 percent or higher in urine) and lower in isolated pulmonary disease. Cross-reactivity is the key caveat: Histoplasma and Blastomyces dermatitidis antigens cross-react substantially, and Coccidioides and Paracoccidioides can cross-react to a lesser degree. A positive Histoplasma urine antigen in the right epidemiology — Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, cavitary or disseminated disease — is actionable, but confirmation with culture, pathology, or serology is still needed. Blastomyces urine antigen has similar caveats, and Coccidioides diagnosis still relies heavily on serology (complement fixation and immunodiffusion) rather than antigen alone.

    Pearl

    Cryptococcal antigen lateral flow assay is the one fungal biomarker you can trust broadly. A positive serum or CSF CrAg deserves urgent action, while a negative BDG or GM without clinical context deserves scepticism.

    A 35-year-old with advanced HIV (CD4 45 cells per microlitre) has a positive serum cryptococcal antigen lateral flow assay but no headache or fever. What is the next step?

    Asymptomatic cryptococcal antigenaemia in advanced HIV predicts progression to meningitis. World Health Organization (WHO) and IDSA guidance recommend lumbar puncture to exclude central nervous system disease even without symptoms when serum CrAg is positive at low CD4, followed by pre-emptive fluconazole if meningitis is excluded. Waiting for culture loses the window where intervention prevents disease.

    Aspergillus polymerase chain reaction and emerging assays

    Aspergillus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detects fungal deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) in blood (serum, plasma, or whole blood) or BAL fluid. The EORTC/MSGERC definitions now include PCR as mycologic support when performed in a standardised laboratory, requiring two consecutive positive blood PCR results or a single BAL PCR with appropriate controls. In practice, outside dedicated centres, assay heterogeneity remains the barrier — extraction methods, targets, and thresholds differ — so a positive PCR from an unvalidated laboratory should be interpreted with caution.

    Where PCR shines is combination testing. In high-risk haematology patients, combining GM plus PCR raises sensitivity and, critically, raises negative predictive value: two negative biomarkers off therapy make invasive aspergillosis considerably less likely. PCR can also remain positive slightly longer than GM after therapy has started, though the clinical use of that persistence is still being defined.

    Beyond PCR, Pneumocystis PCR on BAL fluid is now central for Pneumocystis pneumonia, and T2 magnetic resonance (T2MR) Candida panels and targeted next-generation sequencing are entering clinical use in some centres. None has yet replaced culture and histology as the reference, and none should be ordered without a plan for what a positive or negative result will change.

    Pearl

    Combine, do not stack. Galactomannan plus Aspergillus PCR in the right host is a sensible pair with complementary strengths; ordering galactomannan, BDG, and PCR together in a low-risk patient is a recipe for conflicting false positives.

    Background

    The 2020 EORTC/MSGERC update formalised Aspergillus PCR criteria precisely because earlier studies were undermined by non-standardised assays. If your laboratory does not report assay validation and controls, treat PCR as investigational and confirm with a second specimen type or reference laboratory.

    Putting it together — pretest probability, pitfalls, and stewardship

    Think Bayesian, not binary. Estimate pretest probability from host (prolonged neutropenia, transplant, high-dose steroids, HIV with low CD4), syndrome (pulmonary nodule with halo sign, diffuse ground glass, meningitis with high opening pressure, disseminated granulomatous disease), and exposures (geography, construction, prior prophylaxis). Then choose the narrowest biomarker that tests your hypothesis.

    Stewardship matters. Do not use GM or BDG as a screening test in patients without risk factors, do not repeat a positive BDG daily to watch it fall (it does not track reliably with therapeutic response), and do not treat a single low-level positive without corroboration — repeat the same assay, send a second specimen type, and reassess the clinical syndrome.

    For monitoring, GM has the best evidence for trajectory: falling serum GM in a treated patient with invasive aspergillosis correlates with response, while persistently positive GM predicts worse outcomes. BDG trajectories are inconsistent and should not guide duration of therapy. CrAg titres linger and are poor markers of response; follow symptoms, culture clearance, and imaging instead.

    Pearl

    Use biomarkers to answer one question at a time. Write down what you will do if the result is positive and what you will do if it is negative before you order — if the answer is the same either way, skip the test.

    Common pitfall

    Chasing a positive BDG in a patient on haemodialysis, albumin, or with recent gauze exposure. The test was not built for that host, and a single value above 80 pg/mL without a compatible syndrome is far more likely to be analytic noise than invasive fungal disease.

    Returning to the opening vignette — neutropenic leukaemia, pulmonary nodule, on fluconazole, serum GM 0.7 and BDG 95 pg/mL. What is the best interpretation?

    Proven disease requires histology or sterile culture — biomarkers alone never qualify. Fluconazole has no mould activity, so it would not blunt GM, unlike posaconazole. A GM of 0.7 exceeds the common serum threshold of 0.5 and in a compatible high-risk pulmonary syndrome supports probable disease, but BDG near 80 pg/mL is borderline and non-specific for Aspergillus. Mucorales do not make galactomannan. The right move is BAL-based confirmation where safe and mould-active coverage while awaiting results.

    The five-minute version

    • All fungal biomarkers depend on pretest probability — the same number means opposite things in a high-risk neutropenic host and a low-risk ICU patient with fever.
    • Galactomannan detects Aspergillus (not Mucorales or Cryptococcus); use serum cutoff around 0.5 ODI and BAL around 1.0 ODI, and expect false negatives on mould-active prophylaxis — BAL GM rescues sensitivity.
    • (1→3)-β-D-glucan is pan-fungal but noisy; threshold around 80 pg/mL varies by assay, negative results help de-escalate while single positives without a syndrome mislead — haemodialysis, albumin, and gauze are classic false positives, and Cryptococcus and Mucorales are true negatives by biology.
    • Cryptococcal antigen lateral flow assay is highly accurate; a positive serum CrAg in advanced HIV deserves lumbar puncture and pre-emptive therapy even without meningismus, and titres linger long after cure.
    • Aspergillus PCR is now EORTC/MSGERC-accepted when standardised, and pairs well with GM; non-standardised PCR, T2MR, and sequencing should not be ordered without a plan for the result.
    • Order one hypothesis-driven biomarker at a time, do not treat a single borderline BDG or GM without repeat or second-specimen confirmation, follow GM trajectories not BDG trajectories, and never use biomarkers to diagnose or exclude mucormycosis.
