    Case

    A 29-year-old man presents with fever to 39.2°C, headache, and fatigue for 2 days. He returned 10 days ago from 3 weeks in rural Nigeria visiting friends and relatives and took no chemoprophylaxis. He has no rash, cough, or dysuria. Labs show platelets 88,000 per microlitre, bilirubin 1.9 mg/dL, and fever persists. Should you test for malaria tonight?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Identify which travellers and time windows require urgent malaria testing.
    • Order and interpret thick and thin smears and rapid diagnostic tests, and know when to repeat them.
    • Distinguish Plasmodium falciparum from relapsing species and apply severity criteria.
    • Select guideline-based treatment for uncomplicated and severe malaria and plan follow-up to prevent relapse.

    Why you cannot miss malaria

    Malaria is a febrile illness caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted by infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. In the United States almost all cases are imported in travellers returning from sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Oceania, and parts of Latin America. Persons visiting friends and relatives (VFR) who stay in family homes without chemoprophylaxis carry the highest risk; prior partial immunity does not protect them.

    Plasmodium falciparum can progress from fever to coma, renal failure, and death in hours when treatment is delayed. Most delays occur because malaria was not considered or because a single negative smear was taken as definitive. For the overnight resident the rule is: fever in a returned traveller is malaria until proven otherwise, and testing cannot wait until morning.

    Most P. falciparum presents 9 to 14 days after an infectious bite and almost always within 30 days of return, but relapsing species present months later. Do not use time since return alone to exclude malaria.

    Pearl

    Ask every febrile patient where they have travelled in the past 12 months and whether they took prophylaxis. A VFR traveller from West Africa without prophylaxis is the highest-risk history on board.

    Common pitfall

    Do not anchor on viral syndrome because the urinalysis is positive or a respiratory panel is pending. Thrombocytopenia with fever after travel should keep malaria at the top of the list.

    Which species matter and when they present

    Five species infect humans: Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium malariae, and Plasmodium knowlesi. P. falciparum causes most severe disease and deaths in non-immune travellers. P. vivax and P. ovale form dormant liver hypnozoites that relapse months to years later unless the liver stage is treated. P. knowlesi (macaque-associated in Malaysian Borneo) can produce very high parasite densities rapidly.

    Geography helps you anticipate resistance. Most P. falciparum from Africa and much of Asia is chloroquine-resistant. Only a few areas west of the Panama Canal still have chloroquine-sensitive P. falciparum; maps change, so check CDC country guidance at the bedside rather than relying on memory.

    Early malaria is non-specific: high fever with rigors, headache, myalgias, nausea, and sometimes diarrhoea. Classic periodic fevers are often absent. Mild splenomegaly or jaundice may be present, but exam is often normal. Clues that argue for repeat testing include thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinaemia, anaemia, and transaminitis.

    Background

    Sporozoites develop in hepatocytes for about 7 to 10 days before merozoites invade red cells and cause fever. Hypnozoites of P. vivax and P. ovale can remain dormant and reactivate without a new bite — hence presentation well after travel ends.

    How to diagnose malaria tonight

    Order thick and thin peripheral blood smears, a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for malaria antigens where available, and routine labs (complete blood count, metabolic panel, bilirubin, lactate, glucose) together, and tell the laboratory it is urgent. Do not send malaria testing as a routine next-day test.

    Thick and thin smears remain the reference standard. Thick smears concentrate parasites and are most sensitive for detection; thin smears preserve morphology for species identification and parasite density (percent of infected red cells). Both must be read urgently, ideally within 1 to 2 hours, with a quantitative density reported.

    Rapid diagnostic tests detect histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) for P. falciparum and parasite lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) for all species in 15 to 20 minutes. They help when expert microscopy is delayed, but they do not replace smears: they give no density, stay positive for weeks after treatment, and can miss low-density infections or hrp2-deleted parasites.

    Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is sensitive for species confirmation but is not an emergency test in most hospitals — do not wait for it to start treatment. One negative smear never rules out malaria. If suspicion remains, repeat thick and thin smears every 12 to 24 hours for 3 total sets and document the timing.

    Pearl

    Order smears, RDT, and parasite density together and write “repeat q12–24h × 3 if negative” in the order so the night team knows the plan.

    The VFR traveller from Nigeria has a first thin smear negative and a negative HRP2-based RDT but remains febrile with platelets 88,000 per microlitre. What next?

    A single negative smear and a negative HRP2 RDT do not exclude malaria with thrombocytopenia after West Africa travel. Parasites can be sequestered or submicroscopic on the first sample. Nigeria has chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum, so chloroquine would be incorrect.

    Species and severity drive every decision

    Once parasites are seen, answer two questions before choosing a drug: what species is it, and is there any sign of severe malaria? Both change level of care.

    Thin-smear morphology guides species: P. falciparum shows delicate rings, multiple rings per red cell, appliqué forms, and sometimes crescent gametocytes with high densities possible; P. vivax and P. ovale show enlarged cells and Schüffner dots; P. malariae compact forms without enlargement. When the report says “indeterminate” or mixed, treat as P. falciparum until proven otherwise.

    Severity is any one of the features below. In non-immune adults many US clinicians treat parasite density above 5 percent as severe even without organ dysfunction; WHO uses above 10 percent, with above 4 percent plus other severity signs also considered severe. Report density as a number, not “few rings.”

    Pearl

    Species is not trivia — P. falciparum drives intensive care decisions and P. vivax and P. ovale need a second drug for the liver. If uncertain, treat as P. falciparum.

    Thin smear shows P. falciparum rings at 6 percent density. The patient is alert with bilirubin 3.2 mg/dL. How to classify?

    Above 5 percent is treated as severe in many US frameworks even when the patient looks well; deterioration can be rapid. Thrombocytopenia and hyperbilirubinaemia support malaria rather than exclude it.

    Treatment of uncomplicated malaria

    All doses below are guideline-derived from CDC 2023–2024 and WHO 2023 guidance because no verbatim numbers were recoverable from the corrupted transcript. Verify weight-based dosing, pregnancy status, QT risk, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) status with pharmacy and current CDC tables before prescribing. If you need help overnight, infectious diseases and the CDC malaria hotline via your health department will want species, density, pregnancy, weight, and G6PD.

    Treat promptly, give the full course, and confirm the patient can keep oral drugs down. If vomiting occurs within 30 minutes of a dose, repeat it. Monitor density daily until negative and watch for haemolysis after treatment.

    Pearl

    Most US travellers with falciparum need artemether-lumefantrine, not chloroquine. Geography decides which drug fails.

    Common pitfall

    Do not give primaquine or tafenoquine without a quantitative G6PD result. The radical cure can wait a day; the blood-stage treatment cannot. Giving an 8-aminoquinoline when G6PD-deficient risks severe haemolysis.

    Background

    Artemisinins clear parasites fastest and reduce gametocytes, which is why artemisinin-based combinations are preferred. Never use an artemisinin alone for uncomplicated disease outside the parenteral severe-malaria pathway — monotherapy accelerates resistance.

    P. vivax from Ethiopia is treated with artemether-lumefantrine. Smears are now negative, G6PD is normal, pregnancy test negative. Next step?

    P. vivax and P. ovale require radical cure after the blood stage, only after G6PD is normal. Chloroquine does not kill hypnozoites, and starting before G6PD risks haemolysis.

    Severe malaria is an emergency

    Severe malaria can kill within hours. Admit to a monitored unit, correct hypoglycaemia, acidosis, and renal injury, and treat hyperparasitaemia as severe even if the patient appears stable. Give broad antibiotics in parallel until cultures return — severe malaria and bacterial sepsis overlap and are hard to distinguish on arrival.

    Intravenous artesunate is the treatment of choice for severe malaria due to any species. The CDC-referenced adult dose is 2.4 mg per kilogram intravenously at 0, 12, and 24 hours, then once daily until oral therapy is tolerated and parasite density is at or below 1 percent. It is stocked in many hospital pharmacies and otherwise available via CDC — call early. Quinidine is no longer preferred due to toxicity.

    After parenteral artesunate always complete a full oral combination course such as artemether-lumefantrine once the patient can swallow. Artesunate alone risks recrudescence. Counsel about delayed haemolysis 1 to 4 weeks after artesunate — warn to return for anaemia, jaundice, or dark urine and repeat haemoglobin in 1 to 2 weeks. Exchange transfusion is not routinely recommended.

    Pregnancy raises stakes: thresholds for severity are lower and risks of hypoglycaemia, pulmonary oedema, and fetal loss are higher. Involve obstetrics and infectious diseases early. Primaquine and tafenoquine are contraindicated in pregnancy.

    Common pitfall

    Do not wait for “confirmation” to treat severe malaria. If pretest probability is high and the patient is critically ill, start IV artesunate while repeating smears.

    Pearl

    IV artesunate is not the end — you must finish a full oral combination course and schedule follow-up for delayed haemolysis weeks later.

    A 34-year-old at 18 weeks gestation has fever, confusion, and P. falciparum density 8 percent. Best immediate step?

    This is severe malaria by consciousness and hyperparasitaemia; pregnancy lowers thresholds. IV artesunate is preferred in all trimesters for severe disease. Chloroquine is ineffective for most P. falciparum and oral therapy is inappropriate for severe disease. Primaquine is contraindicated in pregnancy.

    Before discharge — close the loops

    Four items are commonly left open and cause readmission.

    1. Confirm species and density are final. Chase “species pending” reports. Mixed P. falciparum plus P. vivax needs both a falciparum-active combination and later radical cure.

    2. Check G6PD and confirm clearance. For P. vivax or P. ovale, obtain quantitative G6PD before discharge so radical cure is not delayed. Repeat smears until negative — rising density suggests resistance, non-adherence, or vomiting of doses.

    3. Counsel on prevention. Use the admission to teach chemoprophylaxis — atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline, mefloquine, or chloroquine where still appropriate, chosen by destination — plus insect precautions and a travel-clinic referral. VFR travellers benefit most from a non-judgmental conversation about prophylaxis every trip.

    4. Report and arrange follow-up. Malaria is nationally notifiable. Notify your health department, arrange haemoglobin check in 1 to 2 weeks for delayed haemolysis, and confirm the 3-day course was completed — many readmissions are from unfinished pills.

    Background

    Artemisinin partial resistance with delayed clearance is now confirmed beyond Southeast Asia, including parts of East Africa and the Horn of Africa. Reporting imported cases helps track these shifts — another reason to notify public health.

    The five-minute version

    • Fever within 12 months of travel to an endemic area — especially VFR without prophylaxis — is malaria until proven otherwise. Test tonight, not tomorrow.
    • Order thick and thin smears plus RDT together, report species and parasite density urgently, and repeat smears q12–24h × 3 if the first is negative.
    • Any severity feature or density above 5 percent makes malaria severe; treat indeterminate or mixed species as P. falciparum.
    • Uncomplicated chloroquine-resistant disease (most US cases) gets artemether-lumefantrine 6 doses over 3 days with food; add primaquine 30 mg base for 14 days or tafenoquine 300 mg single dose for P. vivax / P. ovale only after normal quantitative G6PD and not in pregnancy.
    • Severe malaria gets IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, and 24 hours then daily until oral therapy possible, followed by a full oral combination — and follow-up for delayed haemolysis 1 to 4 weeks later.
