    HIV and opportunistic infections

    Human herpesvirus-8 disease in people living with HIV

    Kaposi sarcoma and the rest of the KSHV spectrum — what to recognise, what to biopsy, and when to call for help

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 34-year-old man with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, diagnosed 2 years ago but not consistently in care, presents with 6 weeks of fever, night sweats, and fatigue. CD4 count is 68 cells per mm3 and HIV RNA is 180,000 copies per mL. Examination shows violaceous plaques on the shin and palate, diffuse lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and a small right pleural effusion. Labs show anaemia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminaemia, hyponatraemia, and C-reactive protein (CRP) 142 mg per L. Cultures are negative. He asks whether this is lymphoma, infection, or something else.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Name the four major human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8)–associated diseases seen in people living with HIV.
    • Distinguish Kaposi sarcoma (KS), multicentric Castleman disease (MCD), KSHV inflammatory cytokine syndrome (KICS), and primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) at the bedside.
    • Choose the right tissue and test to confirm a KSHV disease, and know what not to rely on.
    • Explain why antiretroviral therapy (ART) is central and when to involve oncology or haematology urgently.

    Why HHV-8 still matters in the ART era

    Human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8), also called Kaposi sarcoma–associated herpesvirus (KSHV), is a gammaherpesvirus that establishes lifelong latency. Most infections are asymptomatic; disease appears when cellular immunity fails — classically in PLWH with low CD4, in older Mediterranean men, in sub-Saharan Africa where seroprevalence is high, and in transplant recipients.

    After effective ART, epidemic KS incidence fell by more than 80%, yet it has not disappeared. Cases now cluster in people with undiagnosed or untreated HIV, delayed ART, or poor retention in care, and less often in people with well-controlled HIV years later.

    If you care for hospitalised PLWH you will still see KSHV disease. It mimics lymphoma and disseminated infection, and a patient can have KS, MCD, and KICS together.

    Background

    There is no recommended HHV-8 screening. Serology is not diagnostic, and a positive HHV-8 DNA PCR in blood does not by itself establish disease — many with advanced HIV have viraemia without organ disease. Diagnosis requires tissue or fluid plus histopathology.

    The virus in brief

    After acquisition — most often via saliva in endemic regions and sexual transmission among men who have sex with men in non-endemic regions — HHV-8 infects endothelial cells and B cells. It encodes latency-associated nuclear antigen (LANA) that tethers viral episomes to host chromosomes, a viral interleukin-6 (vIL-6) that drives human interleukin-6 (IL-6) inflammation, and other proteins that promote angiogenesis and cell survival.

    Two programmes matter. In latency, LANA is detectable by immunohistochemistry — the diagnostic anchor. In lytic replication, virions and cytokines surge. HIV Tat can reactivate HHV-8, which is why uncontrolled HIV amplifies disease. There is no vaccine or HHV-8 prophylaxis; control rests on HIV suppression.

    Pearl

    HHV-8 serology and blood PCR do not make the diagnosis. If you suspect KSHV disease, get tissue and ask pathology for HHV-8 LANA immunohistochemistry every time.

    Kaposi sarcoma — the disease you will see most

    Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a vascular tumour, not a conventional sarcoma. Epidemic (HIV-associated) KS is the form residents see most, but histology is the same across forms: spindle cells, slit-like channels, extravasated erythrocytes, and HHV-8 LANA–positive nuclei.

    Look on skin and mouth first. Lesions are non-blanching violaceous or brown patches, plaques, or nodules — often on lower extremities, face, or genitalia. Oral lesions favour the palate. Oedema can be striking without many lesions because lymphatic obstruction is common. Visceral disease often lacks skin clues: gut KS can bleed, pulmonary KS causes dyspnoea, infiltrates, or effusions.

    KS is a clinical diagnosis confirmed by biopsy. A 4-mm punch usually suffices for skin. Gut or bronchoscopic biopsies are often non-diagnostic because lesions are submucosal — take multiple bites and request LANA. Never give empiric steroids when pulmonary KS is suspected; they worsen disease. After ART, IRIS may enlarge lesions within weeks, even as CD4 rises — not ART failure, but it can threaten the airway.

    Pearl

    Violaceous does not always mean KS, but treat violaceous lesions as KS until proven otherwise — biopsy and specifically request HHV-8 LANA. “Vascular proliferation, not diagnostic” without LANA is unfinished.

    Common pitfall

    Never give systemic corticosteroids to “treat inflammation” when KS, MCD, or KICS is on the differential. Steroids can flare KS and convert indolent skin disease into life-threatening visceral disease, and in MCD they mask features without treating the underlying vIL-6 drive.

    A 29-year-old man with HIV (CD4 45 cells per mm3) has new violaceous plaques on the lower leg with oedema. Which step best establishes the diagnosis?

    KS requires histology with HHV-8 LANA. Serology and blood PCR show exposure or viraemia but do not prove organ disease; imaging stages but does not diagnose; and corticosteroids worsen KS.

    Multicentric Castleman disease and KICS — the febrile, inflamed patient

    If KS is a tumour you can see, multicentric Castleman disease (MCD) is a cytokine storm you can measure. HHV-8–associated MCD is a polyclonal B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder driven by vIL-6 and human IL-6, with flares and remissions.

    During a flare the patient looks septic but cultures stay negative: fever, diffuse nodes, splenomegaly, oedema or effusions, anaemia, thrombocytopenia, hypoalbuminaemia, hyponatraemia, and markedly high CRP. Viral load may be high or suppressed; MCD can flare on ART and can be fatal without therapy.

    Diagnosis requires excisional node biopsy showing HHV-8–positive plasmablasts in the mantle zone plus viraemia. Cores often miss it. Choose the largest, most PET-avid node and involve haematology early.

    KSHV inflammatory cytokine syndrome (KICS) is the clinical twin without pathology: the same fever, cytopenias, and high CRP and HHV-8 load, but without Castleman histology. Many view it as the same spectrum. If syndrome persists without a diagnostic node, KICS is the working label — management overlaps with MCD and often coexists with KS.

    Pearl

    Think MCD or KICS when fever, cytopenias, and very high CRP persist with negative cultures — especially with splenomegaly or concurrent KS. Do not label it “culture-negative sepsis” and move on.

    A man with HIV on ART with suppressed viral load develops recurrent fevers to 39 °C, diffuse adenopathy, splenomegaly, platelets 45,000 per µL, albumin 2.4 g per dL, and CRP 165 mg per L. Cultures are negative and two Kaposi sarcoma plaques are present. What is the best next step for suspected MCD?

    This is classic MCD: inflammatory labs, adenopathy, and coexistent KS. Diagnosis needs excisional tissue with LANA. Serology is not diagnostic, and steroids alone can worsen KS. First-line is rituximab-based therapy, not antibiotics.

    Primary effusion lymphoma and other rarer KSHV diseases

    Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) is an HHV-8–driven large B-cell lymphoma that grows in body cavities without a mass. Most also carry Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). Patients present with rapidly reaccumulating pleural, pericardial, or peritoneal effusions.

    Diagnosis rests on fluid: HHV-8 LANA–positive malignant cells on cytology and flow cytometry, usually with EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) positivity. An extracavitary variant presents as a solid mass with the same phenotype. Effusions are not “hypoalbuminaemia” in this context.

    Other entities — KSHV-associated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma arising in MCD and germinotropic lymphoproliferative disorder — are less likely on call. Recognise that HHV-8 plus EBV in an effusion or mass is PEL until proven otherwise and needs urgent haematology referral.

    Background

    HHV-8 does not cause the common HIV-associated lymphomas such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma or Burkitt lymphoma unless HHV-8 is demonstrated in the tumour. If a report describes high-grade B-cell lymphoma without HHV-8 staining, do not assume a KSHV disease.

    Pearl

    A large exudative effusion without a mass in very low CD4 should trigger fluid cytology with HHV-8 LANA and flow cytometry. Do not drain and discard — send for cytology, cell block, and flow every time.

    How to evaluate a suspected KSHV disease

    Start with HIV history, ART adherence, recent CD4 and HIV RNA, and prior KS or MCD flares. Examine all skin, palate and gums, every nodal basin, liver and spleen, and assess for oedema, effusions, and bleeding.

    Labs frame the syndrome: blood count, CRP, albumin, sodium, renal and liver function, CD4, HIV RNA, and HHV-8 DNA PCR if available. Use HHV-8 DNA as supportive only — high in MCD, KICS, and PEL but can be low in KS and detectable in asymptomatic viraemia. Imaging follows symptoms: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET-CT to choose the best node, chest CT for pulmonary KS.

    Tissue is the decision point. Skin: punch biopsy with LANA. Nodes: push for excisional biopsy for MCD. Effusions: cytology, flow, cell block with LANA and EBER, plus HHV-8 PCR. Tell pathology you are considering KSHV disease — without that prompt LANA may not be done.

    What not to rely on

    • HHV-8 serology — epidemiologic, not diagnostic.
    • Blood HHV-8 PCR alone — supportive at best; negative does not exclude KS.
    • Fine-needle aspiration for MCD — usually misses mantle-zone plasmablasts.
    • Empiric corticosteroids as a test — obscures histology and worsens KS.

    A 42-year-old woman with HIV (CD4 310, undetectable viral load) has known KS and now fever, CRP 110 mg per L, small pleural and peritoneal effusions, and HHV-8 DNA 55,000 copies per mL. Core node shows reactive changes. What best explains the picture?

    This is the overlap zone: KS plus systemic inflammation with high viraemia and effusions, but non-diagnostic core. KICS is MCD-like syndrome without MCD pathology. It is distinct from sepsis, serology does not exclude disease, and IRIS alone does not usually produce this degree of inflammation and viraemia.

    Management principles and when to urgently refer

    Antiretroviral therapy is the foundation for every KSHV disease. Optimise ART and address adherence barriers promptly, even when chemotherapy is planned. Immune reconstitution reduces HHV-8 replication. Do not delay ART while awaiting oncology.

    Beyond ART, each disease has a different next step — why the biopsy matters.

    • Kaposi sarcoma. Limited disease without oedema or visceral involvement often improves with ART alone. Visceral disease, extensive skin disease, symptomatic oedema, pulmonary involvement, or rapid progression need systemic therapy — usually pegylated liposomal doxorubicin or paclitaxel via oncology. Counsel about IRIS.
    • HHV-8–associated MCD. First-line is rituximab, an anti-CD20 antibody that depletes infected B cells and interrupts IL-6. When KS coexists, add liposomal doxorubicin because rituximab can transiently worsen KS. Steroids alone are not first-line.
    • KICS. No randomised trials; practice mirrors MCD — optimise ART and consider rituximab-based therapy for severe disease in an experienced centre.
    • Primary effusion lymphoma. Urgent haematology referral for anthracycline-based chemotherapy with ART, typically CHOP-based, plus drainage. Prognosis is poor without therapy.

    Involve dermatology, oncology, and haematology early. Screen for concurrent opportunistic infections, but do not let a broad workup delay tissue — the effusion you discard today is the diagnosis you need tomorrow.

    Pearl

    ART first, always — then disease-specific therapy. Limited KS may need only ART; MCD, KICS, and PEL need ART plus additional therapy, so get oncology or haematology to the bedside the same day you get the biopsy.

    Background

    HHV-8 MCD can progress to HHV-8–positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Long-term follow-up with infectious diseases and oncology is the norm, with monitoring of HHV-8 DNA, CRP, and counts during flares, even though these are not validated for routine screening.

    Which best guides therapy for HHV-8–associated MCD with coexistent KS?

    Rituximab targets CD20-positive plasmablasts and is first-line for MCD. With coexistent KS, adding liposomal doxorubicin addresses the sarcoma and mitigates worsening that rituximab can cause. Steroids alone are not first-line and ART is continued.

    The five-minute version

    • HHV-8 (KSHV) causes KS, MCD, KICS, and PEL — most relevant in PLWH with low CD4 or poor retention in care, even though ART has reduced incidence.
    • KS presents as violaceous, non-tender patches, plaques, or nodules with oedema and palate lesions; visceral disease can be invisible — biopsy with HHV-8 LANA and request LANA on every specimen.
    • MCD and KICS present as fever, diffuse adenopathy, splenomegaly, cytopenias, hypoalbuminaemia, hyponatraemia, and very high CRP with high HHV-8 viraemia — think beyond sepsis and avoid steroids alone.
    • PEL presents as a large pleural, pericardial, or peritoneal effusion without a mass in very low CD4 — always send fluid for cytology, flow, and HHV-8 LANA / EBER, and do not discard it.
    • ART is foundational for every KSHV disease; then add disease-specific therapy — observation with ART for limited KS, liposomal doxorubicin or paclitaxel for advanced KS, rituximab-based therapy for MCD (plus doxorubicin if KS coexists), urgent anthracycline-based chemotherapy for PEL — with early oncology or haematology co-management.
