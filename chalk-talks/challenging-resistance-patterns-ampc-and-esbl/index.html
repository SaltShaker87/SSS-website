    Antimicrobial resistance

    Challenging resistance patterns: AmpC and ESBL

    What to do when ceftriaxone looks susceptible but will fail, and when piperacillin-tazobactam is not enough

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 9 minutes
    Case

    A 64-year-old man with a common bile duct stricture and an internal biliary drain is admitted with fever to 38.9°C and hypotension two days after an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Blood cultures grow Enterobacter cloacae complex. The laboratory reports ceftriaxone susceptible at a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 1 mg/L. The overnight team continues ceftriaxone. Across the hall, a 71-year-old woman with pyelonephritis and bacteremia grows Escherichia coli resistant to ceftriaxone and ceftazidime but reported susceptible to piperacillin-tazobactam. The resident asks whether either patient can stay on the current beta-lactam.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Distinguish extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) and AmpC beta-lactamase (AmpC) mechanisms, the organisms that most often carry them, and the antibiotics each enzyme hydrolyses.
    • Explain why a susceptibility report can show ceftriaxone or piperacillin-tazobactam as susceptible and still be unsafe to use.
    • Choose empiric and directed therapy for bloodstream infection and urinary tract infection caused by high-risk AmpC producers and by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, using current Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidance.
    • Apply stewardship principles to avoid induction and selection of AmpC and to reserve carbapenems when narrower options work.

    Why AmpC and ESBL get confused

    Both AmpC and ESBL are beta-lactamases made by Enterobacterales, the gram-negative rods that cause most urinary, intra-abdominal, and health care-associated bloodstream infections. Both make third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone look unreliable. Both often travel with resistance to other classes. But they are different enzymes, coded by different genes, inhibited by different inhibitors, and they fail on different drugs.

    The confusion matters when the report says susceptible. A susceptible ceftriaxone result does not guarantee ceftriaxone will work if the organism carries inducible AmpC, and a susceptible piperacillin-tazobactam result does not guarantee it will work for ESBL bacteremia. Knowing the likely enzyme tells you which result to trust.

    Background

    The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) lowered cephalosporin breakpoints in 2010. Before that, laboratories reflexively performed ESBL confirmatory testing and edited susceptible cephalosporin results to resistant. Since the breakpoint change, many laboratories no longer perform routine ESBL testing at all. They report the MIC as measured. Your job is to apply IDSA guidance to that MIC when the organism and syndrome make ESBL or AmpC likely.

    What ESBL means and where you find it

    An ESBL is most often a plasmid-mediated enzyme of the CTX-M, SHV, or TEM families. It hydrolyses penicillins, first-, second-, and third-generation cephalosporins, and aztreonam. It does not hydrolyse carbapenems or cephamycins (cefoxitin) and it is inhibited in vitro by clavulanate. That last point is a laboratory clue, not a therapeutic one — you do not treat ESBL infection with clavulanate combinations.

    The classic ESBL producers are Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Proteus mirabilis. The plasmid often carries fluoroquinolone and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance as well. Community carriage of E. coli ST131 with CTX-M-15 is now common, so ESBL urinary infection occurs without recent health care exposure.

    ESBL genes are not inducible the way AmpC is. If the isolate tests resistant to ceftriaxone, you should assume the ESBL is present whether the laboratory performs a confirmatory test or not. Current IDSA guidance treats any ceftriaxone-resistant E. coli, K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, or P. mirabilis as a presumptive ESBL producer for treatment purposes.

    Pearl

    Cefoxitin tells them apart. ESBL isolates are typically susceptible to cefoxitin. Resistance to cefoxitin plus resistance to ceftriaxone points toward AmpC rather than ESBL in E. coli or Klebsiella.

    What AmpC means and why it behaves differently

    AmpC enzymes also hydrolyse penicillins and first-, second-, and third-generation cephalosporins, but unlike ESBL they also hydrolyse cefoxitin and they are not inhibited by clavulanate. Two properties make AmpC clinically tricky: induction and derepression.

    Many organisms carry a chromosomal ampC gene that is normally repressed. Certain beta-lactams induce higher production. Cefepime and carbapenems are weak inducers; cefoxitin is a strong inducer but is itself stable to hydrolysis. Ceftriaxone and ceftazidime sit in the middle: they induce AmpC and are also hydrolysed once AmpC is derepressed.

    Derepression is the second problem. During ceftriaxone therapy, a subpopulation with a regulator mutation (ampD or ampR) can lose repression and produce AmpC constitutively. The isolate may test susceptible on day 1 and resistant on day 4. Risk is highest for high-inoculum infection such as bacteremia, cholangitis, or undrained abscess, and lowest for uncomplicated cystitis where urine levels are high.

    Not every gram-negative rod carries this risk equally. IDSA guidance separates organisms by the likelihood that AmpC production is clinically significant.

    Pearl

    Three names carry the most AmpC risk: Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, and Citrobacter freundii. If one of those grows from blood and the report says ceftriaxone susceptible, do not relax.

    Why the susceptibility report is not the whole answer

    Automated testing measures growth at a fixed inoculum over hours. It does not capture selection of a derepressed mutant after several days of ceftriaxone. That is why IDSA guidance does not rely on the ceftriaxone MIC alone for high-risk AmpC organisms with invasive disease.

    For ESBL, laboratories may not flag the mechanism at all. Treat ceftriaxone resistance in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, or P. mirabilis as presumptive ESBL and avoid penicillins, cephalosporins, and aztreonam regardless of other panel results. Cefoxitin susceptibility favours ESBL over AmpC.

    AmpC is not reliably inhibited by tazobactam, so piperacillin-tazobactam is not a workaround for AmpC even if the MIC looks good. Cefepime is a weak inducer and more stable to AmpC hydrolysis than ceftriaxone, so it is preferred for susceptible AmpC isolates, but it is not active against most ESBLs. Carbapenems remain active against both when no carbapenemase is present, but should not be default for every resistant urine isolate.

    Common pitfall

    Do not use ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ceftazidime for bacteremia or other invasive infection caused by E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, or C. freundii even when the laboratory reports susceptible. Clinical failure from derepression occurs after the report has been printed.

    Background

    Cefepime is dosed to keep concentrations above the MIC for longer. In IDSA guidance the recommendation for AmpC bacteremia assumes standard high dosing of cefepime and that the isolate is susceptible to cefepime. Check local thresholds with pharmacy, and verify dosing for renal function and for critical illness where extended infusion of beta-lactams is used.

    A blood culture grows Enterobacter cloacae complex, ceftriaxone MIC 1 mg/L (susceptible), cefepime MIC 2 mg/L (susceptible). The source is cholangitis. What is the safer directed choice?

    Ceftriaxone is a strong AmpC inducer and selects for derepressed mutants during therapy of high-inoculum infection. The susceptible MIC on day 1 does not predict day 4. Piperacillin-tazobactam is not reliably active against AmpC, and clavulanate does not inhibit AmpC. Cefepime is a weak inducer and stable to AmpC hydrolysis; a carbapenem is the alternative when cefepime is not susceptible or the patient is critically ill. Source control still matters.

    AmpC at the bedside: avoiding the ceftriaxone trap

    When you see Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, or Citrobacter freundii from a sterile site or from a high-inoculum source, treat the organism as if AmpC will matter, even before any repeat testing confirms it.

    • For invasive infection such as bacteremia, cholangitis, pneumonia, or undrained intra-abdominal infection, avoid ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and ceftazidime regardless of susceptibility. Prefer cefepime when susceptible; use meropenem or ertapenem when cefepime is non-susceptible, the MIC is borderline, or the patient is critically ill.
    • For uncomplicated cystitis with the organism only in urine, high urinary concentrations change the approach. Nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, or other urinary agents with demonstrated susceptibility can work where the same organism would need a different drug for bacteremia.
    • Do not rely on piperacillin-tazobactam for high-risk AmpC bacteremia even if the MIC is low — it faces the same selection pressure.

    When you use cefepime, check the exact MIC and dosing. IDSA guidance accepts cefepime only when susceptible; borderline MICs or critical illness often push toward a carbapenem. A quick pharmacy check is part of safe prescribing.

    Pearl

    Cefepime survives AmpC when ceftriaxone does not, because it is a weak inducer and is more stable to hydrolysis. That advantage disappears once the organism also carries an ESBL or a carbapenemase.

    A urine culture in a stable, afebrile woman with dysuria grows Enterobacter cloacae complex 10^5 CFU/mL, susceptible to cefepime and nitrofurantoin, ceftriaxone susceptible. Blood cultures are negative. What is reasonable?

    Site matters. Uncomplicated cystitis is low inoculum and urinary concentrations are high, so oral urinary agents with proven susceptibility can work and spare broader therapy. For upper tract or invasive disease, avoid ceftriaxone for high-risk AmpC organisms. Piperacillin-tazobactam is not a reliable AmpC drug.

    ESBL at the bedside: choosing therapy by site and severity

    For ESBL producers, the site of infection drives the choice more than the MIC panel suggests.

    • Uncomplicated cystitis due to ESBL E. coli is often treatable without a carbapenem. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin achieve very high urine levels and are preferred when susceptible and without upper tract signs or bacteremia. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole can be used when susceptible, though resistance is common.
    • Pyelonephritis, bacteremia, pneumonia, or intra-abdominal infection due to ESBL E. coli, K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, or P. mirabilis requires a carbapenem when ceftriaxone-resistant. Ertapenem or meropenem are typical choices. Piperacillin-tazobactam is not a carbapenem-sparing option for bloodstream infection even when susceptible — the MERINO trial showed higher mortality with piperacillin-tazobactam than meropenem.
    • Do not use cefepime or ceftazidime for ESBL bacteremia even if susceptible. Most ESBLs hydrolyse them and failures have been reported. Newer combinations such as ceftolozane-tazobactam or ceftazidime-avibactam are reserved for carbapenem-resistant organisms and specific indications.

    Source control remains part of therapy. Remove infected lines, drain abscesses, relieve obstruction. An ESBL cholangitis that stays obstructed will fail on any drug.

    Pearl

    For ESBL bloodstream infection, piperacillin-tazobactam susceptibility on the panel is not a green light. The randomised MERINO trial is why IDSA guidance favours a carbapenem for invasive ESBL disease.

    Pearl

    Uncomplicated ESBL cystitis is the one place you can often spare a carbapenem. Nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin for susceptible E. coli cystitis treats the patient and protects carbapenems for the next patient with bacteremia.

    A 71-year-old woman with pyelonephritis and E. coli bacteremia grows ceftriaxone-resistant, cefoxitin-susceptible, piperacillin-tazobactam-susceptible E. coli. She is hypotensive. Which is most appropriate?

    This is invasive ESBL E. coli disease — bacteremia plus pyelonephritis with hypotension. Nitrofurantoin does not achieve blood levels. Cefepime is not active against ESBL. Piperacillin-tazobactam showed inferior outcomes to meropenem for ESBL bloodstream infection in MERINO, so a carbapenem is preferred. Cefoxitin susceptibility simply supports that AmpC is not the main mechanism.

    A healthy 24-year-old woman with dysuria, no fever or flank pain, grows ESBL E. coli in urine, susceptible to nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin, resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Blood cultures are negative. What is reasonable?

    Uncomplicated ESBL cystitis without bacteremia or upper tract involvement can be treated with a urinary agent that tests susceptible. Nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin concentrate in urine and spare carbapenems. Ceftriaxone and cefepime are not options for an ESBL isolate with ceftriaxone resistance, and routine carbapenem use for cystitis drives resistance.

    Putting it together on call

    On call, run a short checklist before you accept a susceptible result.

    1. What organism? High-risk AmpC organisms (E. cloacae complex, K. aerogenes, C. freundii) mean no ceftriaxone for invasive infection even if susceptible. Low-risk organisms with ceftriaxone resistance suggest ESBL.
    2. What site and how sick? Bacteremia, pneumonia, cholangitis, and undrained abscess are high-inoculum AmpC failure risks. Uncomplicated cystitis is where oral urinary agents can avoid both the AmpC trap and carbapenem overuse.
    3. What does cefoxitin say? Resistant to both cefoxitin and ceftriaxone suggests AmpC; cefoxitin susceptible with ceftriaxone resistance suggests ESBL.
    4. What is the safest narrow option? High-risk AmpC bacteremia: cefepime if susceptible or a carbapenem. ESBL bacteremia or pyelonephritis: a carbapenem. Uncomplicated ESBL cystitis: nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin when susceptible.

    Document your reasoning and talk to pharmacy early. Local antibiogram rates of ESBL and AmpC matter more than any single table, and IDSA guidance is updated as data evolve.

    Pearl

    When you are not sure whether you are dealing with AmpC or ESBL, let the organism and cefoxitin guide you, and choose the drug that is safe for either until infectious diseases or microbiology can help.

    The five-minute version

    • ESBL (often CTX-M in E. coli or Klebsiella) hydrolyses ceftriaxone and aztreonam, spares cefoxitin, and is inhibited by clavulanate; AmpC hydrolyses ceftriaxone and cefoxitin, is not inhibited by clavulanate, and can be induced or derepressed on therapy.
    • High-risk AmpC organisms are Enterobacter cloacae complex, Klebsiella aerogenes, and Citrobacter freundii. For invasive infection with one of them, avoid ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and ceftazidime even if reported susceptible; use cefepime if susceptible or a carbapenem.
    • Treat ceftriaxone resistance in E. coli, K. pneumoniae, K. oxytoca, or P. mirabilis as presumptive ESBL and avoid penicillins, cephalosporins, and aztreonam regardless of panel results for those agents.
    • For invasive ESBL infection such as bacteremia or pyelonephritis, a carbapenem is preferred. Piperacillin-tazobactam is not a reliable carbapenem-sparing option for bloodstream infection even when susceptible, and cefepime is not reliable for ESBL.
    • For uncomplicated ESBL cystitis without bacteremia, nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin with demonstrated susceptibility can spare a carbapenem. Nitrofurantoin does not treat bacteremia or pyelonephritis.
    • Check cefoxitin, confirm the exact MIC for cefepime, verify dosing with pharmacy, and prioritise source control for any high-inoculum infection.
