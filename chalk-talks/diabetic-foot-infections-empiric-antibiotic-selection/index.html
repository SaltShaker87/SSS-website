    Diabetic foot infections — empiric antibiotic selection

    Grading severity, predicting microbiology, and choosing empiric therapy without over-covering

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA chalk talk faculty — 2023 revision (transcript reconstructed from guideline) Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 9 minutes
    Case

    A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus and neuropathy has a 3-week plantar ulcer under the first metatarsal head. Over 2 days it has become red, swollen and malodorous with yellow purulent drainage. He is afebrile, blood pressure 128/74 mmHg, heart rate 88/min. The ulcer is 2.5 cm with 3 cm of surrounding erythema, warmth and tenderness. He took cephalexin 4 weeks ago for a prior ulcer. Nasal screening last admission grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Pedal pulses are diminished. You are asked to choose empiric antibiotics and what culture to send.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Use Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) criteria to decide if a diabetic foot wound is infected and how severe it is.
    • Predict likely pathogens from chronicity, prior antibiotics, and risk factors for MRSA and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
    • Select an empiric regimen for mild, moderate and severe infection.
    • Obtain a deep tissue culture correctly and recognise when to look for osteomyelitis.

    Is this wound infected at all?

    Most diabetic foot ulcers are colonised. Treat infection, not colonisation. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) define infection as purulent secretions or at least two signs of inflammation: erythema, warmth, swelling or induration, and pain or tenderness. The diabetic foot infection is a host response, not just bacteria on a swab. Callus, venous stasis or Charcot change can mimic inflammation, so look for purulence or expanding erythema. Fever, leukocytosis and hyperglycaemia support infection but are not required. Neuropathy blunts pain and ischaemia blunts redness, so a bland ulcer can still be infected.

    An uninfected ulcer — no purulence and fewer than two inflammatory signs — needs no antibiotics even if a swab would grow organisms. Swabbing uninfected wounds drives overtreatment.

    Pearl

    Colonisation is not infection. No purulence and fewer than two inflammatory signs means no antibiotics and no culture. Debride and offload instead.

    The vignette has purulence plus four inflammatory signs — clearly infected. Next grade severity.

    A 58-year-old woman has a 1-cm plantar ulcer with a dry scab, no erythema, swelling or purulence. Swab grows Staphylococcus aureus. What next?

    Without two inflammatory signs or purulence the ulcer is uninfected. Treating colonisation harms without benefit. Even MRSA colonisation alone is not an indication for antibiotics.

    Severity grading is the decision point

    Once infected, grade severity. Grade determines setting, route and breadth, and reminds you to check perfusion and bone.

    IDSA/IWGDF severity for diabetic foot wounds
    GradeCategoryFeatures
    1UninfectedNo purulence, no inflammation
    2Mild≥2 signs or purulence, erythema ≤2 cm, limited to skin/subcutis, no systemic toxicity
    3ModerateErythema >2 cm, lymphangitis, subfascial spread, abscess, gangrene, or muscle/tendon/joint involvement
    4SevereAny infection with systemic toxicity — fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, leukocytosis — meeting systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria

    Mild disease can often be outpatient oral therapy. Moderate often needs initial intravenous and deeper debridement. Severe needs admission, broad intravenous cover and urgent surgery.

    Pearl

    Grade before you prescribe. Mild is oral and narrow; severe with SIRS is admission, surgery and broad intravenous therapy.

    Background

    With every grade check perfusion — pulses, dependent rubor, prior revascularisation — and screen for osteomyelitis. Probing to bone or an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) around 70 mm per hour should raise suspicion.

    The vignette man has purulence, 3 cm of periwound erythema, swelling and tenderness, but is afebrile with normal vitals. What grade?

    Erythema greater than 2 cm with purulence and local signs but no systemic toxicity is moderate. Mild needs erythema 2 cm or less. Severe needs SIRS.

    What grows and why history matters

    History predicts flora better than appearance. Acute, antibiotic-naive mild infections are usually gram-positive cocci — methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and Streptococcus pyogenes. Chronic wounds, prior antibiotics or hospitalisation bring polymicrobial flora: Enterobacterales such as Escherichia coli and Proteus species plus anaerobes.

    • Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA): prior MRSA, recent hospital or nursing facility, recent antibiotics, high local prevalence, severe or non-healing wound.
    • Pseudomonas aeruginosa: macerated or water-exposed wound, warm climate, prior antibiotics, chronic or severe infection. Not routine without these risks.

    Pearl

    Ask about recent antibiotics, hospitalisation and MRSA history, and whether the wound is macerated. Those three answers decide MRSA and Pseudomonas cover.

    The vignette has recent antibiotics and known MRSA colonisation — add MRSA cover now.

    Empiric therapy for mild infections

    Mild disease without MRSA or Pseudomonas risk needs only narrow oral therapy for MSSA and streptococci. Use one agent, reassess in 48–72 hours, and pair with wound care and offloading.

    Oral options for mild infection — verify dose locally
    ScenarioOptions (guideline-derived; verify locally)
    No MRSA/Pseudomonas riskCephalexin or dicloxacillin; or amoxicillin-clavulanate if gram-negative/anaerobe concern. Clindamycin only if true beta-lactam allergy and susceptibility known.
    MRSA riskTrimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline (each needs added streptococcal cover) or linezolid. Culture and narrow.

    All regimens here are from current IDSA and International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) guidance because the transcript was not recoverable; doses were not preserved in the source. Verify every dose with pharmacy and renal function before prescribing.

    Pearl

    Do not add vancomycin or piperacillin-tazobactam to mild disease without MRSA or Pseudomonas risk. Over-covering mild infection adds harm without benefit.

    Common pitfall

    Clindamycin is unreliable empiric cover where resistance is high. Do not use it alone for moderate or severe infection.

    Empiric therapy for moderate and severe infections

    Moderate disease often starts intravenous and steps to oral once improving. Severe disease starts broad intravenous plus urgent surgical evaluation, then narrows.

    Empiric approach by severity — narrow at 48–72 hours
    SeverityWithout MRSA/Pseudomonas riskWith risk or severe disease
    ModerateAmoxicillin-clavulanate, or ceftriaxone plus metronidazole, or ertapenemAdd vancomycin/daptomycin/linezolid if MRSA risk; use piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime plus metronidazole or meropenem if Pseudomonas risk
    SevereVancomycin plus antipseudomonal beta-lactam — piperacillin-tazobactam, or cefepime plus metronidazole, or meropenem — adjust for renal function. Consult surgery promptly.

    Cefepime lacks anaerobe cover — add metronidazole. Piperacillin-tazobactam and carbapenems already cover anaerobes. Avoid routine double gram-negative or double anaerobe cover. Vancomycin remains default for severe MRSA cover with level monitoring.

    Pearl

    Start broad for moderate and severe, then stop what cultures say you do not need. Severe is vancomycin plus an antipseudomonal beta-lactam, with anaerobe cover if needed.

    Common pitfall

    Vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam raises acute kidney injury risk in some cohorts. Monitor creatinine and levels, hydrate, and de-escalate early.

    Background

    The 2023 IWGDF/IDSA update kept the severity-based strategy but stressed earlier oral step-down when improving. Duration depends on severity and osteomyelitis, not the initial empiric choice.

    A 67-year-old man has severe infection with hypotension, leukocytosis, prior hospitalisation and known MRSA. Which regimen fits?

    Severe disease with MRSA risk needs MRSA plus broad gram-negative and anaerobe cover. Only vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam achieves that; the others are too narrow and would delay source control.

    Getting the culture right

    Empiric cover is a bridge. Narrowing depends on a good culture. Superficial swabs of pus or wound edges mostly grow colonisers. When possible, culture before antibiotics, but do not delay severe disease.

    Clean and debride first, then sample the ulcer base — curettage, punch biopsy of viable tissue, or aspirated pus. If bone is exposed, obtain bone via uninfected skin. Send gram stain and aerobic and anaerobic cultures. Note prior antibiotics so no growth is interpreted correctly. Do not culture uninfected ulcers.

    Pearl

    Clean, debride, then biopsy the base. Deep tissue after debridement tells the truth; a superficial swab tells you surface colonisers.

    For the vignette, debride, obtain deep curettage culture, note cephalexin exposure, then start moderate disease therapy with MRSA cover. Draw blood cultures if severe.

    When to worry about bone

    Osteomyelitis complicates up to a third of infected ulcers and changes duration and surgery. Suspect it with an ulcer over bone, visible bone, non-healing despite perfusion, or persistent erythema.

    The probe-to-bone test — feeling gritty bone with a sterile blunt probe — is specific when positive in a high-risk foot but a negative test does not exclude disease. An erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) markedly high (around 70 mm per hour) and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) raise suspicion; a normal ESR makes osteomyelitis less likely. Plain films are insensitive for 1–2 weeks; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) best shows marrow oedema. Definitive diagnosis needs bone culture and histology via percutaneous biopsy through uninfected skin.

    Common pitfall

    Normal early radiographs do not exclude osteomyelitis. If suspicion is high, offload, involve surgery early and plan bone biopsy rather than prolonging broad antibiotics.

    A 70-year-old man has a chronic calcaneal ulcer, probe-to-bone positive, radiograph normal, ESR 85 mm per hour. What next?

    Positive probe plus high ESR is highly suggestive despite normal early film. Sinus swabs mislead. MRI and bone sampling guide therapy.

    The five-minute version

    • Do not treat colonisation. Purulence or at least two inflammatory signs are required for infection; uninfected ulcers need wound care, not antibiotics.
    • Grade first. Mild is erythema ≤2 cm, moderate is larger or deeper, severe is any infection with SIRS. Grade drives setting and breadth.
    • History predicts bugs. Acute mild is Staphylococcus aureus and streptococci; chronic or previously treated wounds are polymicrobial. Add MRSA cover for prior MRSA or recent antibiotics/hospitalisation; add Pseudomonas cover for macerated or severely ischaemic wounds.
    • Choose by grade: narrow oral (cephalexin, dicloxacillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate) for mild; broader for moderate; vancomycin plus antipseudomonal beta-lactam for severe — then narrow at 48–72 hours.
    • Culture the base, not the drainage. Debride, then send deep tissue or curettage for aerobic and anaerobic culture before antibiotics when feasible.
    • Think bone early. Probe to bone, check ESR and CRP, MRI when needed, and biopsy bone for definitive microbiology.
