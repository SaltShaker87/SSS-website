    Navigating penicillin allergies

    Turning a chart label into a clinical decision

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty — transcript reconstructed from current guideline knowledge Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 68-year-old man is admitted with left leg cellulitis — warm, tender, with a small ankle wound. He is afebrile, no bullae or crepitus. The allergy list says “penicillin — rash, childhood.” He recalls a blotchy rash at age 8 after amoxicillin, no hives, swelling, or hospitalisation, and has avoided penicillins since. Is a beta-lactam safer and more effective?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Explain why a penicillin allergy label causes harm and why most labels do not represent true allergy.
    • Take a focused five-question allergy history and document it clearly.
    • Sort histories into low-risk, moderate-risk, and high-risk for immunoglobulin E (IgE)–mediated or severe reactions.
    • Choose a safe pathway — direct challenge, skin testing, or desensitisation — and apply cross-reactivity principles.

    Why the label matters

    About one in ten hospitalised patients reports penicillin allergy, yet more than nine in ten can tolerate penicillin when tested. Many reports reflect a childhood viral rash, a non-allergic effect such as nausea, or a family member’s allergy copied into the chart.

    The label harms patients. Labelled patients receive broader, less effective, and more toxic alternatives, with higher rates of Clostridioides difficile infection, longer stays, more surgical site infections when cephalosporin prophylaxis is withheld, and greater exposure to vancomycin, fluoroquinolones, and carbapenems. Systematic de-labelling — evaluating and, when safe, removing the label — is a stewardship intervention.

    True allergy still matters. Immunoglobulin E (IgE)–mediated reactions cause urticaria (hives), angioedema, bronchospasm, or anaphylaxis within minutes to hours. Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) are delayed by days to weeks. The goal is to return low-risk patients to beta-lactams and protect high-risk patients.

    Pearl

    “Penicillin — rash” is a story, not a diagnosis. Ask what it looked like, when it appeared, and what else was happening.

    Taking the allergy history in two minutes

    Five questions cover what an allergist needs and let you risk-stratify at the bedside:

    1. What happened? Was it hives, swelling, wheezing, or throat closure versus flat rash, nausea, or diarrhoea? Many “allergies” are intolerances.
    2. Timing after dose? IgE reactions start within minutes to hours. Rash after days or weeks later points away from IgE; SCARs are days to weeks.
    3. How long ago and since? Half of IgE wanes by five years. Age-8 rash 60 years ago differs from hives last month. Ask about any beta-lactam tolerated since.
    4. Treatment and outcome? Antihistamines versus epinephrine or intensive care changes risk; peeling or organ injury suggests SCAR.
    5. Other cause? Mono with ampicillin, viral exanthem, or “my mother said I was allergic as a baby” are common confounders.

    Document the exact story: “generalised hives and lip swelling 30 minutes after amoxicillin at age 24, epinephrine in emergency department” is actionable. “PCN allergy” is not. Use the structured allergy fields so pharmacy and the next team see the detail.

    Pearl

    “I don’t remember, my mother said I was allergic as a baby” with no severe features is typically a low-risk, incomplete history — not an automatic high-risk label.

    Sorting low-risk from high-risk

    Sort the story into three buckets. This reflects current allergy and stewardship principles for triage, not a substitute for allergist judgement. No specific drug doses are given here; all pathways should follow institutional allergy and stewardship protocols.

    Risk stratification by history for triage
    CategoryTypical historiesWhat it suggests
    Low-risk for IgE and for SCARRemote mild non-urticarial rash; maculopapular rash without hives or swelling; isolated gastrointestinal upset, headache, or yeast infection labelled as allergy; vague childhood history with no severe featuresVery low likelihood of true allergy; often eligible for supervised direct challenge after protocol review

    Any history of blistering, peeling, painful skin, mucosal involvement, or fever with facial oedema and eosinophilia weeks into therapy should prompt avoidance and urgent allergy or dermatology input. Do not skin test or challenge SCARs.

    Common pitfall

    Do not re-expose a patient with a credible history of toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) from penicillin to “see if it was really the drug,” even if the chart is vague. Avoid and consult.

    A 74-year-old reports nausea alone after amoxicillin ten years ago, no rash, swelling, or breathing difficulty, and tolerated cephalexin since. How would you categorise this?

    Nausea without skin, respiratory, or cardiovascular features is an intolerance, not allergy. Remote timing and cephalosporin tolerance lower IgE risk, so supervised direct challenge per pathway is typical.

    When the history is low-risk: direct challenge

    When history is low-risk for IgE and SCAR, many centres offer a direct oral challenge — a supervised dose of amoxicillin with observation per protocol — without prior skin testing. Most low-risk patients tolerate it and can be de-labelled.

    Do not improvise challenges. They require a pathway with selection, consent, resuscitation capability, monitoring, and documentation. Allergy or stewardship pharmacy determines eligibility and location.

    After a negative challenge, update the allergy field, pharmacy, and primary care record, and counsel the patient: “You took amoxicillin under observation without reaction. We removed the allergy. Tell every clinic and pharmacy you have taken it since.”

    Pearl

    De-labelling is the treatment. A negative challenge that stays in a progress note but never updates the allergy field helps no one. The allergy list, pharmacy, and patient must all get the same message.

    The man in the vignette (remote blotchy rash at age 8, no severe features) is admitted for cellulitis at a hospital with a pharmacist-supervised challenge pathway. What is the best next step?

    Low-risk histories are best managed through a structured pathway, not reflexive avoidance or unsupervised exposure. Vancomycin is less effective and more toxic for cellulitis; desensitisation is for high-risk patients who must receive the culprit.

    When the history is high-risk: testing and desensitisation

    Recent hives, angioedema, wheezing, or anaphylaxis minutes to hours after penicillin, or any SCAR, should prompt avoidance and referral. Do not challenge on the ward.

    Penicillin skin testing typically uses penicilloyl polylysine and, where formulary allows, minor determinants with histamine and saline controls, followed by observed oral challenge if negative. It requires trained staff and resuscitation equipment and is not a bedside workaround. It is not validated for SCARs.

    Desensitisation induces temporary tolerance by giving escalating doses over hours in a monitored setting, often intensive care. Tolerance lasts only while the drug is continuously infused; interruption requires repeat desensitisation. It is for patients with confirmed high-risk allergy who must receive the culprit agent urgently and have no acceptable alternative, for example penicillin for neurosyphilis with documented anaphylaxis.

    Common pitfall

    Ordering penicillin skin testing for every labelled patient without triage wastes allergist capacity and delays care. Many low-risk patients do not need skin testing at all to be de-labelled.

    Choosing alternatives and cross-reactivity

    When you must avoid penicillin, side chain similarity — not the beta-lactam ring itself — drives risk.

    Cross-reactivity principles — check institutional side chain tables
    SituationWhat to knowPractical point
    Cephalosporins after penicillin historyRisk is low when the cephalosporin does not share the culprit’s side chain; modern risk is much lower than historic 10 percent estimates.Do not assume all cephalosporins are equal. Check side chains with pharmacy.
    Carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem)Cross-reactivity is low, generally around 1 percent or less, and many IgE-allergic patients have safely received carbapenems under observation.For severe infections where a carbapenem is indicated, a remote low-risk rash alone should not automatically deny therapy; consult for high-risk histories.
    Aztreonam (monobactam)Almost no cross-reactivity with penicillin except with ceftazidime, which shares an identical side chain with aztreonam.Usable for gram-negative coverage in penicillin anaphylaxis, but lacks gram-positive activity — add coverage for Staphylococcus or Streptococcus when needed.

    For non-purulent cellulitis, first-generation cephalosporins covering methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus and streptococci are first-line in many pathways when the history is low-risk and side chains differ. Vancomycin and clindamycin are not interchangeable. Purulent cellulitis or suspected MRSA shifts choices regardless of allergy.

    Pearl

    Ask pharmacy: “Does my intended alternative share the side chain of the culprit penicillin?” That is more useful than “Do penicillins cross-react with cephalosporins?”

    A patient with penicillin anaphylaxis (hives, wheeze, hypotension 20 minutes after amoxicillin last year) needs gram-negative therapy for severe pneumonia. Which is most accurate about aztreonam?

    Aztreonam’s monobactam structure gives very low penicillin cross-reactivity; ceftazidime is the exception because of an identical R1 side chain. It does not cover gram-positives, so Streptococcus pneumoniae or S. aureus needs added therapy. Carbapenem cross-reactivity is low but not zero after recent anaphylaxis.

    Documenting and closing the loop

    Before discharge, do three things:

    1. Update the structured allergy field — symptom, timing, severity, treatment, and tolerance since. For de-labelled patients, reclassify as “de-labelled — tolerated amoxicillin on [date] supervised.”
    2. Send it where it will be seen — handoff, discharge summary, and patient instructions.
    3. Counsel the patient. Give explicit permission to stop saying they are allergic and explain what to do if symptoms recur.

    If not de-labelled now, write: “Low-risk remote rash, referral sent; avoid penicillin until evaluated, cephalosporins per side chain guidance pending.”

    Pearl

    The most common failure is administrative: the challenge was negative but the allergy field stayed active, so the next admission reused vancomycin.

    Common pitfall

    Do not copy forward “allergy: penicillins” with no reaction listed as permanent avoidance without asking the five questions. Every medication reconciliation is a chance to refine.

    After a negative supervised amoxicillin challenge via the pathway, what best completes documentation?

    The structured allergy field drives prescribing alerts. A note alone does not suppress future alerts, and the patient needs written instructions to carry forward.

    The five-minute version

    • One in ten reports penicillin allergy; more than nine in ten can tolerate it. The label drives broader, less effective therapy and C. difficile risk.
    • Ask five questions every time: what happened, timing after dose, how long ago and what has been tolerated since, treatment and outcome, and alternative explanations. Document verbatim in the allergy field.
    • Sort histories: low-risk (remote mild rash, isolated gut upset) versus moderate-risk (recent hives) versus high-risk or severe (anaphylaxis, SJS/TEN/DRESS) — only low-risk is considered for direct challenge; never challenge SCARs.
    • Low-risk patients who tolerate supervised challenge should be actively de-labelled across the chart and counselled. High-risk patients avoid the culprit, use side chain–informed alternatives, and are referred; desensitisation is temporary and only when the culprit is indispensable.
    • Cross-reactivity follows side chains: cephalosporin risk low when side chains differ; carbapenem about 1 percent or less; aztreonam negligible except with ceftazidime and lacks gram-positive coverage.
