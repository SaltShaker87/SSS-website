    Streptococci 101

    How to group them, what each group does, and what to do about it

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 24-year-old presents in February with 2 days of sore throat, fever to 38.6°C, tender anterior nodes, and tonsillar exudates, without cough or rhinorrhoea. Rapid antigen is positive and her roommate asks about testing. Down the hall, a 68-year-old man has fever and back pain 2 weeks after a dental cleaning; blood cultures grow gram-positive cocci in chains, later Streptococcus gallolyticus. The team wonders if endocarditis workup and colonoscopy are needed.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Sort streptococci by haemolysis and Lancefield group and predict the clinical syndrome each group tends to cause.
    • Distinguish suppurative group A Streptococcus infections from non-suppurative immune sequelae and know when to test and treat pharyngitis.
    • Recognise the adult syndromes of groups B, C, G, viridans, anginosus, and bovis groups and their sentinel associations.
    • Choose first-line therapy for streptococcal syndromes and know when penicillin still works and when resistance matters.

    Why streptococci deserve a framework, not a list

    On Gram stain they all look alike — gram-positive cocci in chains — but at the bedside they behave like different organisms. Streptococcus pyogenes makes toxins, Streptococcus agalactiae colonises mucosa and invades neonates and older adults, viridans streptococci build biofilm on valves, anginosus group makes abscesses, and Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly Streptococcus bovis) points to the colon. This talk gives you a sorting system, not a list of 20 names.

    The two tools you will use most are haemolysis on blood agar — complete (beta), partial or greening (alpha), or none (gamma) — and Lancefield grouping by cell wall carbohydrate. Group A is almost always Streptococcus pyogenes, group B is Streptococcus agalactiae, and many viridans streptococci are non-groupable. Streptococcus pneumoniae is technically alpha-haemolytic but is managed separately.

    When the lab says “streptococci,” ask how it haemolysed, what group, and where it came from. The same alpha-haemolytic description in sputum versus two blood culture sets after a dental procedure means different things.

    Background

    Rebecca Lancefield grouped streptococci in the 1930s by cell wall carbohydrate; haemolysis was described by James Brown. Neither alone is specific — for example, groups C and G can be Streptococcus pyogenes-like or anginosus group.

    How the lab sorts streptococci before the name arrives

    A beta-haemolytic colony that is bacitracin sensitive and pyrrolidonyl arylamidase (PYR) positive is Streptococcus pyogenes until proven otherwise. Group B streptococci are beta-haemolytic, bacitracin resistant, PYR negative, hippurate positive, and Christie-Atkins-Munch-Petersen (CAMP) positive.

    For alpha-haemolytic isolates, Streptococcus pneumoniae is optochin susceptible and bile soluble with lancet-shaped diplococci. Viridans group are optochin resistant and bile insoluble. Anginosus group (Streptococcus anginosus, Streptococcus constellatus, Streptococcus intermedius) are often minute colony and may be beta-, alpha-, or gamma-haemolytic with a faint caramel odour.

    You rarely order these tests, but they help you read a preliminary report. “Beta-haemolytic streptococcus, not A or B, pending” in a superficial swab is often not a primary pathogen; the same report from blood in a postpartum patient is likely group C or G Streptococcus with invasive potential.

    Pearl

    If the report says “beta-haemolytic streptococcus, not A or B,” look at the source before you treat it as Streptococcus pyogenes. Throat and blood are different stories from a superficial swab.

    A preliminary blood culture report says “alpha-haemolytic streptococcus, optochin resistant.” Which description fits best?

    Streptococcus pneumoniae is optochin sensitive and bile soluble. Optochin resistance points to viridans group, which in blood, especially with a recent dental procedure or valvular disease, should prompt consideration of endocarditis rather than dismissal as a contaminant.

    Group A Streptococcus pyogenes — throat, skin, and what comes after

    Group A Streptococcus (GAS), essentially Streptococcus pyogenes, is the streptococcus with both toxins and immune aftermath. It causes pharyngitis, scarlet fever, impetigo and cellulitis, necrotising soft tissue infection, and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.

    Pharyngitis

    Classic group A Streptococcus (GAS) pharyngitis is abrupt sore throat with fever, tonsillar exudates, tender anterior nodes, and no cough or rhinorrhoea. Cough, coryza, hoarseness, conjunctivitis, or diarrhoea favour viral illness. Use modified Centor criteria to decide who to test, then test. Do not treat adults without testing, and do not test asymptomatic contacts.

    Rapid antigen testing is specific but misses some cases; backup culture is recommended in children when rapid testing is negative. Treatment shortens symptoms modestly and prevents acute rheumatic fever when started within about 9 days. Penicillin and amoxicillin remain first line because Streptococcus pyogenes has never shown penicillin resistance. With immediate penicillin allergy, options per guidelines include a first-generation cephalosporin if tolerated, clindamycin, or a macrolide — but macrolide resistance makes macrolides a last choice.

    Common pitfall

    Do not test “every sore throat.” Testing cough-predominant illness drives unnecessary antibiotics and misses the real diagnosis.

    Skin and invasive disease

    Impetigo is honey-crusted or purulent. GAS cellulitis is rapidly spreading with well-demarcated erythema, and necrotising infection is pain out of proportion with systemic toxicity — a surgical emergency. Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome presents with fever, hypotension, renal or hepatic dysfunction, and coagulopathy, driven by superantigen toxins.

    For mild non-purulent cellulitis where group A Streptococcus and methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus are the target, use an anti-streptococcal beta-lactam. For necrotising infection or toxic shock, add clindamycin for toxin suppression — never alone — and obtain urgent surgical debridement.

    The immune sequelae only GAS causes

    Only GAS causes acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Rheumatic fever follows pharyngitis 1 to 5 weeks later with migratory polyarthritis, carditis, chorea, erythema marginatum, or nodules, and is largely preventable by treating pharyngitis. Glomerulonephritis follows throat or skin infection 1 to 3 weeks later with haematuria, oedema, and hypertension; antibiotics treat the infection but do not prevent it.

    Pearl

    Penicillin still kills Streptococcus pyogenes every time. When treatment fails, think adherence, re-exposure or viral co-infection, or carriage — not penicillin-resistant GAS.

    Pearl

    Glomerulonephritis can follow skin infection; rheumatic fever cannot. If a patient had impetigo 2 weeks ago and now has haematuria, think post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, not rheumatic fever.

    A 19-year-old has sore throat with fever, exudates, tender nodes, and no cough. Rapid antigen is negative. What is the best next step?

    Rapid antigen tests are specific but miss some cases. Guidelines advise backup culture in children when rapid testing is negative because rheumatic fever prevention matters most in that group. ASO titres rise weeks later and do not help acute pharyngitis decisions. Empiric macrolide therapy risks failure and resistance.

    Groups B, C, and G — not just a pregnancy problem

    Group B Streptococcus (GBS), Streptococcus agalactiae, colonises the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tract in 15 to 30 percent of adults. You learned it as a neonatal pathogen causing early-onset sepsis in the first week and late-onset meningitis, with universal rectovaginal screening at 35 to 37 weeks and intrapartum prophylaxis. In adults, most invasive GBS now occurs outside pregnancy.

    In non-pregnant adults GBS causes bacteraemia, skin and soft tissue infection especially with diabetes, urinary infection, pneumonia, arthritis, and meningitis. Management is a beta-lactam, penicillin first line when susceptible. Do not treat asymptomatic colonisation in non-pregnant adults.

    Groups C and G are largely Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis. They cause pharyngitis, cellulitis, bacteraemia, and endocarditis that can mimic GAS but do not cause rheumatic fever. Penicillin susceptibility is the rule.

    Background

    Intrapartum prophylaxis has reduced early-onset neonatal GBS disease since the 1990s, but adult invasive disease has risen — now more often seen on adult wards than obstetric wards.

    Pearl

    GBS bacteremia in an older adult with diabetes and cellulitis is not a contaminant and not “just skin flora.” Look for bacteraemia, joint infection, and endocarditis risk rather than assuming a simple wound culture.

    Viridans, anginosus, and bovis groups — three clues you should not miss

    These are the alpha- or gamma-haemolytic streptococci where the species name changes management even when the Gram stain does not.

    Viridans group — the endocarditis organism

    Viridans group streptococci (VGS) — Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus sanguinis, and others — are oral flora and classic causes of subacute endocarditis on damaged or prosthetic valves after dental trauma. Two positive blood culture sets, persistent growth, a new murmur, or emboli should trigger Duke criteria evaluation and echocardiography; a single positive bottle can be contamination but not always.

    Endocarditis requires prolonged intravenous beta-lactam therapy, often ceftriaxone or penicillin G, sometimes with short gentamicin synergy per guidelines — not a short course. Prophylaxis before dental procedures is now limited to high-risk cardiac conditions; check current American Heart Association guidance.

    Anginosus group — the abscess former

    Anginosus group (Streptococcus anginosus, Streptococcus constellatus, Streptococcus intermedius) forms abscesses — dental, sinus, brain, liver, lung, intra-abdominal — often with anaerobes. When this group grows, expect pus and plan for drainage.

    Bovis or gallolyticus group — the colonoscopy clue

    Streptococcus gallolyticus subsp. gallolyticus (biotype I, formerly Streptococcus bovis type I) bacteremia is associated with colonic neoplasia and endocarditis, often on a normal valve. Subsp. pasteurianus (biotype II/2) is linked more to biliary disease. Finding Streptococcus gallolyticus in blood should prompt colonoscopy and echocardiography even if fever resolves.

    Common pitfall

    Calling viridans group streptococci in blood a contaminant without counting sets and timing, or calling Streptococcus gallolyticus bacteremia “just bacteraemia” without evaluating the colon and heart, misses the diagnosis that changes outcomes.

    A 64-year-old man has Streptococcus gallolyticus bacteremia from two sets. Transthoracic echocardiogram is negative and he is afebrile after 48 hours of ceftriaxone. What is the best next step?

    Streptococcus gallolyticus bacteremia has a well-established association with colonic pathology including cancer and adenoma, and with endocarditis even on native valves. A negative transthoracic study does not exclude endocarditis, and resolution of fever does not remove the need to evaluate the colon. Management is prolonged therapy when endocarditis is found.

    A patient with a dental abscess develops headache, fever, and a rim-enhancing brain lesion. Culture grows Streptococcus intermedius. What is most characteristic of this group?

    Anginosus group organisms (Streptococcus anginosus, Streptococcus constellatus, Streptococcus intermedius) are classic abscess formers in brain, liver, and abdomen, often in mixed infection with anaerobes. Source control via drainage is often essential.

    Streptococcus pneumoniae — the streptococcus with its own rules

    Streptococcus pneumoniae is the alpha-haemolytic outlier — lancet-shaped diplococci with a capsule. It causes pneumonia, otitis media, sinusitis, and meningitis, especially with asplenia or after viral infection.

    Unlike Streptococcus pyogenes, it can be penicillin non-susceptible and macrolide resistant. Empiric therapy depends on syndrome and local susceptibility. Check current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention vaccine schedules for adults 65 years or older, immunocompromise, asplenia, cerebrospinal fluid leak, or cochlear implant.

    Pearl

    When you see “gram-positive diplococci” in sputum, think Streptococcus pneumoniae, but do not call sputum culture proof of pneumonia. When you see them in cerebrospinal fluid, treat as pneumococcal meningitis until proven otherwise and add dexamethasone where indicated before or with the first antibiotic dose.

    Choosing antibiotics — principles rather than a memorised table

    For almost all beta-haemolytic streptococci except pneumococcus, penicillin or amoxicillin is the backbone when susceptible — which for GAS, GBS, and groups C/G it almost always is. Broader is not better; vancomycin is not stronger for a penicillin-susceptible streptococcus.

    Syndrome sets the duration and setting: pharyngitis needs a short oral course, endocarditis needs weeks intravenously, abscess needs drainage. For anginosus group, the drug without drainage fails.

    Allergy history matters more than the label — most reported penicillin allergies are not true immunoglobulin E-mediated allergies.     Pearl

    For penicillin-susceptible streptococci, penicillin is the most active drug. Reaching for vancomycin “to be safe” is less safe and less effective.

    The five-minute version

    • Sort by haemolysis and Lancefield, then by site — that predicts syndrome better than memorizing every species name.
    • Group A Streptococcus pyogenes is the toxin- and immune-sequelae organism: pharyngitis, impetigo, cellulitis, necrotising infection, toxic shock, and uniquely rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
    • Group B Streptococcus agalactiae colonises many adults and causes neonatal early- and late-onset disease plus adult bacteraemia, cellulitis, and meningitis — treat infection, not colonisation.
    • Viridans group signals endocarditis risk in blood; anginosus group signals abscess and need for drainage; Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) in blood triggers echocardiography and colonoscopy.
    • Streptococcus pneumoniae is the capsular, vaccine-preventable outlier with distinct penicillin and macrolide resistance — test susceptibility and check current vaccine schedules.
    • Penicillin remains first line for most streptococci except pneumococcus; add clindamycin for toxin-mediated GAS syndromes, never alone, and always pair antibiotics with source control for abscess and necrotising infection.
