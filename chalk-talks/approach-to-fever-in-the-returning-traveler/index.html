    Approach to fever in the returning traveler

    Malaria until proven otherwise, and what else not to miss

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 11 minutes
    Case

    A 32-year-old man presents to the emergency department with fever to 39.1°C, headache, and myalgias. He returned 6 days ago from a 3-week trip to rural Ghana, where he visited family. He took no malaria chemoprophylaxis. He reports mosquito bites, ate street food, swam in freshwater, and had no sick contacts. He is not sexually active on the trip. Exam shows temperature 38.8°C, mild tachycardia, no rash, no jaundice, and no focal findings. Labs are pending. You are the resident asked: what could this be and what do you do tonight?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Apply a structured approach to fever in the returning traveler that rules out life-threatening infection first.
    • Triage malaria risk using geography and timeline and order the right tests without delay.
    • Build an exposure-driven differential using region, activities, and incubation period.
    • Choose an initial workup and know when to admit, isolate, and ask for help.

    Why this presentation deserves a different reflex

    Fever after travel is common, but the stakes are higher than fever in a patient who has not traveled. In the United States the two most frequently identified causes of fever in returned travelers are malaria and dengue, followed by enteric fever (typhoid and paratyphoid), but most patients will have a cosmopolitan cause such as influenza, urinary tract infection, or COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019). The travel history does not replace the usual fever workup — it adds a parallel list of cannot-miss diagnoses where delay harms.

    Two principles organize the workup. First, consider Plasmodium falciparum malaria in every febrile patient from a malaria-endemic area until proven otherwise — even with prophylaxis. P. falciparum can become severe in hours. Second, use time as a filter: incubation, time since return, and duration of fever. A 2-day incubation argues against malaria; fever 6 months after a brief trip argues against most acute tropical infections.

    In the emergency department the question is not "what tropical disease is this?" but "what will kill the patient if I miss it tonight, does this patient need isolation, and have I sent malaria testing now?"

    Pearl

    Fever in a traveler is malaria until proven otherwise if they were anywhere Anopheles mosquitoes transmit — including a single night near an airport or a brief layover counts.

    Common pitfall

    Waiting for the classic paroxysmal fever pattern to suspect malaria. Most patients with P. falciparum present with irregular fever, headache, and myalgias — not the textbook tertian cycle. Do not wait for periodicity.

    First ten minutes — triage, isolation, and vitals that change the plan

    Start with the same rapid assessment you use for any febrile patient, then add three travel-specific screens.

    • Is this unstable? Hypotension, altered mentation, bleeding, or severe hemolysis suggests severe malaria or sepsis — escalate early.
    • Does this patient need isolation? Ask about outbreak exposure and high-risk geography. If Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), viral hemorrhagic fever (Ebola, Lassa), or tuberculosis are plausible, isolate and notify infection prevention before further workup.
    • How long since return? Most imported infections present within 1 month. Fever more than 6 to 12 months later makes acute malaria, dengue, and enteric fever unlikely, though P. vivax and P. ovale can relapse from liver hypnozoites.

    Collect core vitals and quick labs that help everywhere: complete blood count (CBC) with differential, complete metabolic panel, bilirubin, and urinalysis. Thrombocytopenia points toward malaria or dengue; leukopenia toward dengue or acute viral illness; elevated transaminases are common in enteric fever, dengue, and acute viral hepatitis.

    Background

    The incubation period is your most underused test. Malaria usually presents 7 days to several weeks after exposure, dengue 3 to 14 days, typhoid 6 to 30 days, chikungunya 2 to 12 days, and acute human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) 2 to 4 weeks. If the math does not fit, reconsider the diagnosis even if the geography seems perfect.

    Take the travel history that actually helps

    A vague "have you traveled?" is not enough. A focused five-part history gives you the differential in two minutes and saves a scatter of low-yield tests.

    1. Where exactly? Region, rural versus urban, altitude, and season matter more than country alone. Sub-Saharan Africa carries highest risk for P. falciparum; South and Southeast Asia for dengue, chikungunya, and enteric fever; rural freshwater in Africa or Asia for schistosomiasis and leptospirosis. Check Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) country pages when unsure.
    2. When? Dates of travel, date of return, and date fever began. Plot incubation. Also ask about stopovers — a night in Addis Ababa en route elsewhere is an exposure.
    3. What did you do? Mosquito bites and net use, freshwater swimming, animal contact, street food, unpasteurized dairy, sexual activity, medical injections abroad, and activities such as safari or caving. Visiting friends and relatives (VFR) travelers often have higher risk and less prophylaxis.
    4. What protected you? Vaccines, malaria chemoprophylaxis (drug, adherence, and when stopped), insect precautions, and pretravel counseling. Remember prophylaxis can suppress but not always prevent malaria and can prolong incubation.
    5. Who else is ill? Travel companions, household contacts, and recent outbreaks at the destination.

    Finish with host factors: pregnancy, asplenia, immunosuppression, and prior malaria or dengue. Asplenic patients can deteriorate very quickly from malaria or babesiosis.

    Pearl

    VFR travelers returning from West Africa without prophylaxis are the highest-yield group for P. falciparum in the emergency department. Ask explicitly about prophylaxis and do not accept "I took something" — ask the name and whether they finished the course after return.

    A 28-year-old woman returns from Thailand 5 days ago with fever, retro-orbital headache, and myalgias. Platelets are 98,000 per microliter, white cells low. Which exposure history most changes your top diagnosis?

    Dengue is transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes that bite during the day in urban areas. Urban daytime bites in Southeast Asia with fever, retro-orbital pain, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia make dengue the lead diagnosis. Street food points toward enteric infection but is far less specific here, and a clean pool is not a schistosomiasis risk.

    Malaria — the diagnosis you must rule out tonight

    If the patient was in a malaria-endemic area at any point in the relevant window, malaria testing is urgent — not tomorrow morning, tonight.

    How to test

    Order both a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) and thick and thin smears when available. Thick smear detects parasites; thin smear identifies species and quantifies parasitemia. One negative smear or RDT does not exclude malaria — repeat every 12 to 24 hours for at least two more sets if suspicion is high. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is sensitive but rarely available urgently; do not wait for it if severe malaria is possible.

    RDTs for P. falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 can stay positive for weeks after treatment and can miss low parasitemia or non-falciparum species. If your lab cannot read smears promptly at night, call pathology or a reference lab and treat on clinical grounds if severe disease is suspected.

    When to treat before confirmation

    Do not delay therapy for severe malaria awaiting a second test. If pretest risk is high and there is altered consciousness, severe anemia, renal injury, respiratory distress, or high parasitemia, treat as severe malaria and get urgent infectious disease input. In the United States intravenous artesunate is the treatment for severe malaria. For uncomplicated disease, choice depends on species and resistance — confirm species with infectious disease and pharmacy before deciding.

    Pearl

    One negative smear does not exclude malaria. If the story fits, repeat thick and thin smears every 12 to 24 hours for three total sets. Document the plan so the night team does not stop at one.

    A 45-year-old man returned from Nigeria 10 days ago with fever and is now confused and jaundiced. RDT is negative, first smear negative, platelets 62,000 per microliter. What is the best next step?

    Severe malaria kills quickly and smears can be falsely negative early, especially with low or sequestered parasitemia. Altered mentation plus recent West Africa travel mandates repeat malaria testing on a tight schedule, clinical treatment planning for severe disease, and specialist involvement. Dengue does not explain confusion and jaundice well in this context.

    Beyond malaria — a syndrome-based differential

    Once malaria testing is underway, broaden systematically. The most useful split is by accompanying syndrome, not by country list alone.

    • Fever with rash or arthralgia: Think dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus. Dengue often gives high fever, retro-orbital pain, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia, sometimes with mild bleeding; chikungunya is dominated by severe, often persistent polyarthralgia; Zika is frequently mild with rash and conjunctivitis and matters greatly in pregnancy.
    • Fever with abdominal pain or diarrhea: Consider enteric fever (Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi and Paratyphi), amebic liver abscess, and hepatitis A or E. Enteric fever often shows prolonged fever and abdominal tenderness without diarrhea early on.
    • Fever with respiratory symptoms: Remember influenza and COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) first, then add Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), tuberculosis, and endemic fungi if exposures fit.
    • Fever with eosinophilia: Think helminths and other parasites — acute schistosomiasis (Katayama syndrome) weeks after freshwater exposure, strongyloidiasis, or filariasis. Eosinophilia may be absent early.
    • Fever with jaundice: Consider viral hepatitis, leptospirosis, and complicated malaria. Leptospirosis follows freshwater or mud exposure with conjunctival suffusion and renal injury.
    • Undifferentiated fever: Rickettsial spotted fevers (for example African tick bite fever with eschar after safari), leptospirosis, acute human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and enteric fever all belong here.

    Background

    Routine serologies sent in the emergency department often return days after discharge and can mislead if you test for everything. Prioritize tests that change tonight's management — malaria smear, dengue nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) antigen where appropriate, blood cultures, and respiratory viral testing — and let the outpatient or inpatient workup add the rest with intention.

    Pearl

    Always add acute HIV to the differential for febrile returning travelers with sexual exposure or injection risk. It is common, treatable, and routinely missed when the workup stays tropical-only.

    What to do on day one — workup that does not waste time

    Order in parallel, not in sequence.

    • Everyone with fever and relevant geography: Malaria RDT plus thick and thin smears, complete blood count (CBC) and metabolic panel, bilirubin, lactate if ill, blood cultures before antibiotics, urinalysis, and chest radiograph if respiratory symptoms or hypoxia.
    • Add by syndrome: Respiratory viral panel including influenza and COVID-19 testing; stool cultures and liver panel if diarrheal or hepatitis; dengue NS1 antigen and/or PCR if within the first week of illness in a dengue-endemic area, with dengue immunoglobulin M (IgM) later; HIV antigen/antibody plus HIV PCR if acute infection suspected; consider leptospirosis PCR early and paired serology.
    • Do not send in isolation:Typhoid serology (Widal) has poor performance — blood and stool cultures are better. Save convalescent serologies for paired testing with infectious disease guidance.

    Be restrained with empiric antibiotics — they do not treat dengue or malaria. If the patient is unstable, give broad coverage after cultures and get help. For suspected rickettsial disease, do not delay doxycycline. For suspected enteric fever, ceftriaxone is a common empiric choice pending cultures; tailor once susceptibilities return given resistance in South Asia.

    Common pitfall

    Starting a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) for dengue-like illness before dengue is excluded. If dengue is on the differential, avoid NSAIDs and aspirin because of bleeding risk — use acetaminophen for fever while you test.

    A 24-year-old student backpacked in India, ate street food, now has 7 days of fever to 39.5°C, abdominal pain, and constipation. Labs show mild transaminitis and relative bradycardia at 62 beats per minute despite fever. Blood cultures are pending. Which test is most appropriate now?

    This is classic enteric fever until proven otherwise, but blood cultures are the diagnostic standard; Widal testing is insensitive and nonspecific, and leukopenia is not specific. Malaria testing belongs in parallel because it kills faster than typhoid if missed. Empiric therapy comes after cultures.

    When malaria tests are negative — what not to miss

    A negative malaria workup is the start, not the end. Three mistakes are common once the smear is negative.

    First, anchoring on a single exotic diagnosis because the travel seems exotic. Most fevers are still cosmopolitan infections. Look again for pyelonephritis, pneumonia, cellulitis, or viral upper respiratory infection before ordering rare serologies.

    Second, forgetting infections with long tails. P. vivax can relapse months later, visceral leishmaniasis can present months after travel to South Asia or the Mediterranean, and amebic liver abscess can appear weeks after a diarrheal illness has resolved. Ask about prior malaria treatment — if the patient had vivax without primaquine for hypnozoites, relapse is more likely.

    Third, missing public health obligations. Suspected viral hemorrhagic fever, MERS, measles, or tuberculosis triggers isolation and reporting before confirmation. If you are considering one of these, keep the patient masked and in a private room, use appropriate personal protective equipment, and call infection prevention and the health department early.

    Pearl

    Every febrile traveler needs three parallel thoughts: malaria, the most common cosmopolitan cause of fever, and the one exposure-driven infection that fits incubation and activities tonight.

    When to admit, when to call for help, and counseling for next time

    Admit if you cannot exclude malaria, the patient is unstable, has dengue warning signs (abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding), has severe enteric fever, or needs isolation. Stable patients with low-risk travel, a plan for three smears, and a plausible viral syndrome can sometimes be managed as outpatients with clear return precautions and reliable follow-up.

    Call for help early: infectious disease for suspected malaria, severe dengue, or hemorrhagic fever concern; the CDC malaria hotline for artesunate access; and infection prevention for high-consequence pathogens.

    Before discharge, counsel on completing prophylaxis after return, insect repellent with DEET (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide) or picaridin, treated bed nets, avoiding freshwater swimming in schistosomiasis-endemic areas, and seeking care quickly for fever — telling the next clinician exactly where they traveled and when. Record missed vaccines for catch-up.

    Background

    GeoSentinel and similar surveillance networks consistently show that fever in travelers from sub-Saharan Africa is most often malaria, while fever from Southeast Asia is most often dengue, and fever from South Asia often includes enteric fever. Use that as a starting prior, then override it with the individual's exposures and timeline.

    A 19-year-old returned from a freshwater lake in Malawi 4 weeks ago now has fever, dry cough, and peripheral eosinophilia. Malaria smears are negative. What exposure most explains this?

    Katayama syndrome presents 3 to 8 weeks after cercarial exposure during freshwater contact in endemic regions, with fever, cough, and eosinophilia. Mosquito bites explain malaria or dengue but not this timing and eosinophilia. Chlorinated pools do not carry Schistosoma.

    The five-minute version

    • Treat every fever after travel from a malaria-endemic area as malaria until proven otherwise — send RDT plus thick and thin smear immediately and repeat every 12 to 24 hours for three total sets if suspicion remains.
    • Use time as a filter: incubation, time since return, and duration of fever narrow the list faster than a long country-based catalogue.
    • Anchor your history on where exactly, when, and what you did — mosquitoes, fresh water, food and water, animals, sex, and prophylaxis adherence, with special attention to VFR travelers.
    • Test in parallel by syndrome — blood cultures always, respiratory viral testing, dengue NS1 early or IgM later, and HIV testing when exposure or an acute viral syndrome fits — and do not rely on a single serology like Widal.
    • Isolate and notify early if viral hemorrhagic fever, MERS, or tuberculosis are plausible, and call infectious disease quickly for any suspected severe malaria — artesunate saves lives but takes logistics.
