    Labs for a patient with newly diagnosed HIV

    What to order on day one, why each test matters, and what should never delay antiretroviral therapy

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 29-year-old man is seen in primary care after a routine fourth-generation human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antigen/antibody test returned reactive, with a positive HIV-1/HIV-2 differentiation assay for HIV-1. He feels well, reports no fever, weight loss, thrush, or genital symptoms, and has no prior HIV testing on record. His vital signs are normal. You have 15 minutes before his next patient arrives and a long problem list of orders to consider. What do you actually need to send today?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Confirm a new diagnosis of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and explain how cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count, plasma HIV RNA (viral load), and resistance testing guide care.
    • Select baseline labs that change antiretroviral therapy (ART) choice — including hepatitis B virus (HBV) serologies, renal and liver function, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701 when abacavir is considered.
    • Apply a once-at-diagnosis screen for co-infections and opportunistic infection risk — tuberculosis, syphilis, hepatitis A and C, Toxoplasma, and cryptococcal antigen when indicated — without delaying ART.

    Why the first panel matters more than getting every test right

    The day a patient learns they have human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is crowded with emotions, disclosure, and logistics. The baseline panel does three jobs: it confirms the diagnosis, tells you how urgently to act, and prevents an antiretroviral therapy (ART) choice that harms kidneys, liver, or hepatitis B virus (HBV) control. It also establishes a metabolic anchor — when creatinine (Cr), weight, lipids, or glucose rise later, you know whether ART or something else is responsible.

    The Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) and the HIV Medicine Association list the same core set for every adult with newly diagnosed HIV, then add conditional tests by organ function, exposures, and cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count. Think in four buckets: confirm the virus, quantify immune and virologic burden, screen once for treatable co-infections, and capture organ function you will monitor.

    One principle outweighs every checklist below: do not delay ART for labs. Genotype, human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701, and hepatitis results return in days. Start ART the same day or within days and adjust if a result forces a change.

    Pearl

    Draw the baseline panel and offer same-day or rapid ART at the same visit. Waiting for a genotype or human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701 does not justify a delay that lets the virus replicate for another week.

    Background

    The window after a new HIV diagnosis is also the moment patients are most likely to stay engaged. Bundling labs into a single blood draw, adding pregnancy testing for persons of childbearing potential, and scheduling a 1–2 week return for results reduces loss to follow-up.

    Confirm and characterise the virus

    Most new diagnoses arrive as a reactive fourth-generation HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody (Ag/Ab) combination assay, followed by an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay. If that sequence is reactive for HIV-1, confirmatory plasma HIV ribonucleic acid (RNA) — the viral load — serves as both confirmation and baseline. When the initial Ag/Ab is reactive but differentiation is nonreactive or indeterminate, an HIV RNA test distinguishes acute infection (RNA detectable) from a false-positive screen (RNA undetectable).

    Once HIV is confirmed, order three studies that describe the virus itself:

    • Plasma HIV RNA (viral load) — quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in copies per millilitre and log10. This is your treatment-response marker. A viral load drawn before ART lets you document suppression later and, if very high, signals urgency.
    • CD4 count and percentage — absolute count in cells per microlitre and percentage of total lymphocytes. Count drives opportunistic infection prophylaxis and staging; percentage is less variable during acute illness.
    • Genotypic resistance testing — reverse transcriptase and protease genotype plus integrase strand-transfer inhibitor (INSTI) genotype at entry to care, regardless of whether you plan immediate ART. Transmitted drug resistance is uncommon but clinically important, and the result often returns after you have already started therapy — you then tailor if needed.

    Order CD4 and viral load together on day one. Resistance testing requires plasma with detectable virus; if the viral load is undetectable on repeat (for example, an elite controller or a mislabelled acute case), the lab cannot amplify and will cancel the genotype. Do not repeat the fourth-generation screen as your confirmation — use RNA.

    Common pitfall

    Re-checking a reactive fourth-generation assay instead of obtaining an HIV RNA misses acute infection and delays linkage. If Ag/Ab is reactive and differentiation is negative, the next step is plasma HIV RNA, not another antibody test.

    A 29-year-old with reactive HIV Ag/Ab and HIV-1 positive differentiation returns for baseline labs. Which trio is essential before or at ART start?

    HIV RNA and CD4 define baseline burden and immune status; genotype at entry guides ART even if started before the result returns. Repeating the screening Ag/Ab adds nothing once differentiation is positive, and IgM for toxoplasma is not the screening test — immunoglobulin G (IgG) is.

    Labs that determine which ART you can safely use

    Modern ART is forgiving, but three lab groups still change your first prescription.

    Hepatitis B status

    Order hepatitis B virus (HBV) surface antigen (HBsAg), surface antibody (anti-HBs), and core antibody (anti-HBc) for every patient. If HBsAg is positive, choose an ART regimen with two agents active against HBV — typically tenofovir (tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide) plus lamivudine or emtricitabine — and do not stop the HBV-active backbone without hepatology input, because withdrawal can flare hepatitis. If all three are negative, plan vaccination; if anti-HBs alone is positive, document immunity.

    Renal and liver baseline

    Send a comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) including creatinine with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), electrolytes, and hepatic panel, plus phosphate if you may use tenofovir. Add urinalysis with a urine protein–creatinine or albumin–creatinine ratio. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate is avoided or dose-adjusted with renal dysfunction or proteinuria. A baseline also lets you interpret later Cr rises — dolutegravir and cobicistat inhibit tubular creatinine secretion and raise measured Cr by about 0.1–0.15 mg/dL without changing true glomerular filtration rate (GFR).

    HLA-B*5701 and tropism

    Order human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B*5701 only if abacavir is under consideration; a positive result is a hard stop for abacavir because of hypersensitivity risk, and you must not rechallenge. Coreceptor tropism testing is not a baseline for everyone — reserve it for when a chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) antagonist such as maraviroc is contemplated, which is uncommon at entry.

    Add a complete blood count (CBC) with differential, and a pregnancy test for persons of childbearing potential — both influence ART selection and counselling — and document weight, blood pressure, and a medication and allergy history that captures drug–drug interactions.

    Pearl

    Hepatitis B serologies are not academic. An HBsAg-positive patient who stops emtricitabine or tenofovir without a planned HBV-suppressive alternative can develop a severe hepatitis flare. Order the trio before the first prescription and act on it.

    Background

    Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) testing is sometimes ordered reflexively at HIV diagnosis. It is required before dapsone or primaquine, not before ART itself — send it if your prophylaxis plan may include those agents, rather than for every patient on day one.

    Screen once for co-infections you can treat, cure, or vaccinate against

    Many co-infections are silent until ART-induced immune reconstitution or progressive immunodeficiency reveals them. Screening at diagnosis finds them when treatment is simplest.

    • Hepatitis A virus (HAV): anti-HAV immunoglobulin G (IgG). If negative, vaccinate; hepatitis A is more severe with chronic liver disease and is vaccine-preventable.
    • Hepatitis C virus (HCV): anti-HCV antibody with reflex HCV RNA if positive. HCV is curable, interacts with ART choice, and needs prompt referral.
    • Syphilis and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs): syphilis screening (treponemal or reverse-sequence algorithm per lab), plus nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) for Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis at exposed sites — urogenital, rectal, and pharyngeal as indicated by history — and trichomoniasis testing when indicated. Retest at least annually, more often with ongoing risk.
    • Tuberculosis (TB): interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), most often QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus, plus symptom screen and chest radiography if IGRA is positive or symptoms or risk factors exist. A negative IGRA does not exclude active TB — evaluate clinically if cough, fever, or weight loss is present.

    Take a sexual, substance-use, and travel history the same day. It determines which sites to swab and whether to add hepatitis D, strongyloides, or endemic mycosis testing. For many residents, the reflex is to send “everything available” — focus instead on the once-at-diagnosis panel above, then tailor by exposure and geography.

    Pearl

    Anorectal and pharyngeal NAAT matters. A urine-only screen misses the majority of Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis infections in men who have sex with men — ask about sites of exposure and swab accordingly.

    Which hepatitis B serology pattern means you must include tenofovir plus lamivudine or emtricitabine in the ART regimen and avoid stopping them abruptly?

    HBsAg positivity denotes active HBV infection. Tenofovir plus lamivudine or emtricitabine suppresses both HIV and HBV; stopping either without an alternative HBV-active agent risks a hepatitis flare. Isolated core antibody needs clinical interpretation and HBV DNA, not automatic HBV-active ART, while vaccine-type immunity or a fully negative panel does not mandate HBV-active therapy.

    Stratify opportunistic infection risk

    Opportunistic infection screening is selective, not universal. Order based on CD4 count, symptoms, and local epidemiology, and understand what threshold drives prophylaxis.

    • Toxoplasma gondii IgG: send once for everyone. Seropositivity with a low CD4 count guides primary prophylaxis and later informs encephalitis evaluation. Immunoglobulin M (IgM) is not useful for this purpose.
    • Cryptococcal antigen (CrAg): serum cryptococcal antigen for patients with CD4 counts below 100 cells per microlitre, or with headache, fever, or meningeal signs regardless of count. A positive CrAg with no meningitis triggers pre-emptive fluconazole; meningitis needs lumbar puncture.
    • Cytomegalovirus (CMV) and varicella: CMV IgG is optional at entry and mainly helps if retinitis or colitis develops later; varicella-zoster virus IgG is useful if history is unclear.
    • Endemic fungi and parasites: histoplasma antigen/antibody, coccidioidal serology, or strongyloides serology by exposure and geography or symptoms, not for every patient in a low-prevalence setting.
    • Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD): quantitative G6PD level if dapsone or primaquine may be needed for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis; deficiency contraindicates those agents.

    Do not send Toxoplasma IgM, cryptococcal antigen for every patient with a normal CD4 count, or baseline bronchoalveolar lavage without symptoms. The talk paired each test with its threshold because “screen everyone for everything” generates positives you cannot act on.

    Pearl

    Toxoplasma gondii IgG is a once-per-life test. Send it at diagnosis while the CD4 percentage is whatever it is — you will want that answer at 2 a.m. when the same patient returns with ring-enhancing lesions.

    For which patient is serum cryptococcal antigen most appropriate at entry?

    Cryptococcal antigen screening is targeted: CD4 below about 100 cells per microlitre, or any patient with central nervous system symptoms. Universal screening at high CD4 counts is low yield and not recommended. Toxoplasma seropositivity and latent TB are managed separately.

    Baseline organ function, metabolic risk, and safety labs

    The metabolic labs you draw today are the comparator for the next decade.

    • Kidney: creatinine with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinalysis with protein quantification. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate can cause proximal tubulopathy; many clinicians favour tenofovir alafenamide when eGFR is reduced or proteinuria is present.
    • Liver: hepatic panel at baseline and after ART start, particularly with HBV or hepatitis C virus (HCV) co-infection or alcohol use.
    • Haematology: complete blood count (CBC) with differential — cytopenias are common before ART and improve with suppression, but a low baseline informs workup and drug avoidance.
    • Metabolic: fasting lipid panel and fasting glucose or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Integrase strand-transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), particularly dolutegravir and bictegravir, are associated with weight gain; protease inhibitors and some nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) affect lipids and glucose. Document weight and blood pressure.
    • Pregnancy: urine pregnancy test for persons of childbearing potential; consider vitamin D or bone density by risk, not universally on day one.

    Re-measure creatinine and hepatic panel 2–8 weeks after ART initiation, then every 3–6 months with HIV RNA and CD4. If creatinine rises modestly after dolutegravir or cobicistat without a rise in cystatin C or blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and without proteinuria, recognise benign inhibition of creatinine secretion before labelling it kidney injury.

    Pearl

    Do not interpret a 0.1 mg/dL creatinine bump two weeks after dolutegravir or cobicistat as acute kidney injury until you have checked the pattern. Recheck cystatin C or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), review proteinuria, and look for other nephrotoxins first.

    Background

    Coreceptor tropism testing was once ordered at entry to preserve a future maraviroc option. With INSTI-based regimens now standard, tropism testing at diagnosis has largely disappeared from practice — order it only if you are actually considering a CCR5 antagonist.

    Put it together: an order set that still lets you start ART today

    For the vignette patient — well, no opportunistic infection symptoms, reactive Ag/Ab with HIV-1 differentiation positive — a same-day plan looks like this:

    1. Confirm: plasma HIV RNA (viral load) — also your baseline — plus document the Ag/Ab and differentiation already done.
    2. Immune and virologic baseline: CD4 count and percentage, genotypic resistance testing (reverse transcriptase, protease, and INSTI).
    3. ART-safety labs: HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc; HAV IgG; HCV antibody with reflex RNA; CMP with eGFR, phosphate, hepatic panel; urinalysis with protein ratio; CBC; pregnancy test when appropriate; HLA-B*5701 if abacavir is possible.
    4. Infection screen: syphilis test, gonorrhoea/chlamydia NAAT by exposure site, IGRA with symptom review and chest film if indicated, toxoplasma IgG, and G6PD if prophylaxis may require dapsone.
    5. Metabolic baseline: fasting lipids, fasting glucose or HbA1c, weight and blood pressure.
    6. Conditional by CD4: serum cryptococcal antigen if CD4 <100 cells/µL or central nervous system symptoms; evaluate for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis at <200 cells/µL.

    Plan an early return at 1–2 weeks to review genotype, HLA-B*5701, hepatitis, and renal labs; check HIV RNA at 2–8 weeks for a log10 drop and CD4 every 3–6 months until stable. Reinforce condom use, partner testing, and undetectable equals untransmittable (U=U) once viral load is sustained undetectable.

    Pearl

    The most dangerous “abnormal lab” after a new HIV diagnosis is the one you forgot to send. A missing HBsAg or baseline creatinine forces a phone call to change ART after the patient has already left with a bottle. Use a standardised panel so you do not rely on memory at the end of a long day.

    A newly diagnosed patient has CD4 180 cells/µL, HIV RNA 85,000 copies/mL, negative HBsAg, and eGFR 58 mL/min with 2+ protein on dipstick. HLA-B*5701 and genotype are pending. Which statement best reflects next steps?

    ART should not wait for genotype or HLA-B*5701 — start a regimen appropriate to renal function and HBV status and modify later if needed. Cryptococcal antigen is targeted to low CD4 or symptoms, not universal, and toxoplasma IgG is sent once at entry for everyone, not only those with very low counts.

    Common pitfall

    Ordering toxoplasma IgM, cytomegalovirus (CMV) PCR, or cryptococcal antigen universally wastes resources and creates false positives. Match each opportunistic infection test to seroprevalence logic or a CD4 threshold — IgG once, antigen when risk is real.

    The five-minute version

    • Confirm with plasma HIV RNA plus the reactive Ag/Ab and differentiation already done; interpret discordant screens with RNA, not repeat antibody testing.
    • Send CD4 count and percentage, HIV RNA (viral load), and genotypic resistance testing including integrase at entry — and start ART without waiting for results.
    • Send HBsAg, anti-HBs, and anti-HBc, comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinalysis, and complete blood count (CBC); add HLA-B*5701 only if abacavir is considered, and pregnancy testing when appropriate.
    • Screen once for hepatitis A IgG, hepatitis C antibody (reflex RNA), syphilis, gonorrhoea/chlamydia nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) by exposure site, and tuberculosis by interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) with chest imaging if indicated.
    • Stratify opportunistic infection risk: Toxoplasma gondii IgG once for all, cryptococcal antigen if CD4 <100 cells/µL or neurologic symptoms, and assess Pneumocystis prophylaxis around CD4 <200 cells/µL; check glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) only if dapsone or primaquine may be used.
    • Capture fasting lipids and glucose or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) plus weight as your metabolic baseline, then recheck HIV RNA, CD4, creatinine, and hepatic panel on a clear follow-up schedule.
