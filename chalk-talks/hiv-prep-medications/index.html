    HIV PrEP medications

    Choosing and monitoring oral and injectable pre-exposure prophylaxis

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 24-year-old man who has sex with men comes for a routine primary care visit. He reports condomless anal sex with two partners in the past 3 months, one partner with unknown human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status. He had a rectal chlamydia infection 6 months ago, treated. He is interested in HIV prevention but says he “is not great at taking a pill every day” and asks what options exist beyond condoms. He has no chronic medical conditions, takes no regular medications, and his last HIV test 2 months ago was negative.

    How do you counsel him about pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), choose a regimen, and make a follow-up plan he can actually stay on?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Identify who should be offered PrEP and how to frame the conversation routinely.
    • Compare the three FDA-approved PrEP regimens — including dosing, eligibility, and key trade-offs — to choose a regimen with the patient.
    • List the required baseline workup before starting PrEP and the ongoing monitoring schedule.
    • Counsel patients on adherence, missed doses, and what to do around gaps in coverage.

    Why PrEP belongs in primary care

    PrEP is antiretroviral medication taken by people without human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) to prevent acquisition. Daily oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and every-2-month cabotegravir each reduce sexual HIV risk by greater than 90 percent in trials; effectiveness in practice tracks adherence.

    The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2021 PrEP guideline recommends informing all sexually active adults and adolescents about PrEP and offering it to anyone who requests it — do not wait for the patient to ask.

    Substantial risk includes recent bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI), condomless anal or vaginal sex with partners of unknown HIV status, having a partner with HIV who is not virologically suppressed, and sharing injection equipment. Screen by behaviour, not identity, and remember adolescents weighing at least 35 kg can receive PrEP.

    Pearl

    Make PrEP an opt-out conversation: “I talk to all my patients about HIV prevention — would it be okay to spend 1 minute on PrEP?” It normalises the offer and doubles the chance a patient will say yes if they need it.

    Background

    HIV incidence remains about 32,000 new U.S. diagnoses yearly, concentrated in the South and among young men who have sex with men and Black and Latino patients — yet PrEP uptake is lowest there. The implementation gap exceeds the pharmacology gap.

    The PrEP menu at a glance

    As of the 2021 CDC guideline updated through 2022, there are three Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved PrEP options for adults and adolescents at least 35 kg. A fourth — twice-yearly lenacapavir — was FDA-approved in June 2025 and is discussed below as an emerging option that postdates the original talk.

    With perfect adherence, effectiveness is comparable. Trials HPTN 083 and HPTN 084 showed cabotegravir superior to daily FTC/TDF only because adherence to pills was imperfect.     Pearl

    If the patient can name a reason they will miss pills — “I travel, I’m home late, I hide meds from family” — that is a vote for injectable PrEP, even if they initially say “pills are fine.” Match the pharmacology to the life, not the other way around.

    Update since the talk

    Lenacapavir (Yeztugo) 927 mg subcutaneously every 26 weeks was FDA-approved for PrEP in June 2025 after PURPOSE 1 and 2 showed near-complete protection. CDC guidance had not yet incorporated it when this page was adapted.

    Your patient says he has trouble remembering a daily pill but can get to clinic every 2 months. Which in-force FDA-approved option best matches his pattern?

    Cabotegravir long-acting was superior to daily FTC/TDF in populations where adherence was imperfect precisely because it removes the daily-pill requirement. 2-1-1 dosing is not FDA-approved and was studied only in men who have sex with men — it is not a substitute for approved daily or injectable regimens in routine U.S. practice. Tenofovir alone is not PrEP.

    Oral PrEP in detail: FTC/TDF versus FTC/TAF

    Both pills contain emtricitabine (FTC) 200 mg plus a tenofovir prodrug. Protection builds after starting daily dosing: about 7 days for receptive anal sex and about 21 days for receptive vaginal sex and for injection drug use.

    FTC/TDF 200 mg/300 mg by mouth daily. Approved for all groups including receptive vaginal sex and injection drug use. Requires estimated creatinine clearance (CrCl) at least 60 mL/min. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) can lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) by a few points and reduce bone density by 1–2 percent in the first year; both largely reverse after stopping. Preferred if HBV infection is present because TDF suppresses HBV.

    FTC/TAF 200 mg/25 mg by mouth daily. Approved only for men and transgender women at risk through sex, not for receptive vaginal sex — the DISCOVER trial enrolled only that population. Can be used down to CrCl 30 mL/min, with fewer renal and bone effects than FTC/TDF, but more weight gain (~1–2 kg) and higher lipids.

    Common pitfall

    Do not prescribe FTC/TAF to a cisgender woman at risk through vaginal sex. Efficacy in that population was not established, and FTC/TDF or cabotegravir should be used instead. Pharmacists will flag this — but the resident is ultimately responsible.

    What about on-demand or 2-1-1 dosing?

    The IPERGAY 2-1-1 regimen (two pills 2–24 hours before sex, then one at 24 and 48 hours) worked in men who have sex with men with infrequent sex but is not FDA-approved and was not studied in cisgender women, heterosexual men, or people who inject drugs. The CDC does not recommend it as a substitute for daily dosing.

    A 29-year-old cisgender woman with a partner with HIV who is not on treatment asks for PrEP and prefers a pill. Her creatinine clearance is 95 mL/min. Which oral option is FDA-approved for her?

    FTC/TDF is the only oral PrEP approved for receptive vaginal exposure. FTC/TAF was studied only in men and transgender women, and its label explicitly excludes those at risk through receptive vaginal sex. 2-1-1 is not FDA-approved and was studied only in men who have sex with men.

    Injectable cabotegravir: who it helps and how it works

    Cabotegravir is an integrase strand transfer inhibitor given as 600 mg (3 mL) intramuscularly in the gluteal muscle. After two injections 1 month apart, it is given every 2 months. An optional 4-week oral cabotegravir 30 mg daily lead-in to check tolerability is allowed, but direct-to-injection is now standard.

    It reaches protective levels within days and persists for months (half-life ~40 days). That tail protects briefly after a missed dose but can select integrase resistance if HIV is acquired while drug levels are low.

    Best for patients who struggle with daily pills or prefer discretion. Contraindications are prior hypersensitivity and unknown HIV status. No renal adjustment. Injection-site pain (~80 percent, mild) is most common, then headache and fatigue. It does not treat HBV.

    Pearl

    For injectable PrEP, the appointment is the adherence. Build the recall system before the first shot: schedule the next injection before the patient leaves, add phone and portal reminders, and have a plan for a 7-day window on either side of the due date.

    Common pitfall

    Never let a patient drift for months after a missed cabotegravir dose with no coverage. The tail can select resistance. If they are more than 7 days late, use the re-initiation schedule (repeat the 1-month interval) and bridge the gap with oral FTC/TDF or FTC/TAF until levels are restored — or switch formally to oral PrEP if injections are no longer feasible.

    A patient on cabotegravir misses his injection by 5 weeks and reports condomless sex last week. HIV antigen/antibody test is negative today. What is the best next step?

    Cabotegravir delays seroconversion so antibody tests may be negative early. Human immunodeficiency virus ribonucleic acid (RNA) testing is required when injection delays overlap with possible exposure. A 5-week delay exceeds the 7-day window, so the initiation interval must be repeated and oral bridging prevents a resistance-prone tail period. Also assess whether PEP is indicated for the last-week exposure.

    Before you prescribe: the non-negotiable workup

    Every PrEP start requires the same safety checklist, whether oral or injectable. Do not prescribe PrEP if HIV status is unknown or if the patient has signs of acute HIV.

    Pearl

    An indeterminate HIV test is a stop sign, not a yellow light. Hold PrEP, obtain RNA, and get same-day ID or clinic support before the patient leaves. Starting two drugs during acute infection is how resistance happens.

    Follow-up, monitoring, and what to do when doses are missed

    The CDC schedule below applies to all three regimens, with drug-specific tweaks.

    Missed oral doses. Take a missed dose when remembered. With fewer than 4 doses taken per week, vaginal protection falls first. If 7 or more consecutive days were missed with recent condomless sex, test for HIV with RNA before resuming. For planned stops, continue 7 days after last sex; restart 7 days (rectal) to 21 days (vaginal) before risk resumes.

    Late injections. A 7-day window on either side of the due date is allowed. If 8 days to 4 weeks late, inject and resume every 2 months; if >4 weeks late, repeat the 1-month initiation pair and bridge with daily oral FTC/TDF or FTC/TAF until levels are restored, or switch to oral PrEP if injections remain impractical.

    Background

    Minor creatinine bumps on TDF often reflect inhibition of tubular secretion rather than true injury. A rise of 0.1–0.2 mg/dL within the normal range does not mandate stopping if creatinine clearance remains ≥60, but any clearance drop below threshold, new proteinuria, or hyperphosphatemia should prompt renal evaluation and consideration of FTC/TAF or cabotegravir.

    A patient on FTC/TDF has a creatinine clearance of 58 mL/min, down from 82 six months ago. She is asymptomatic and sexually active. What is the best action?

    FTC/TDF requires creatinine clearance at least 60 mL/min. A sustained fall below threshold warrants holding the drug, repeating labs to confirm, looking for reversible causes (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, dehydration), and switching to FTC/TAF if the exposure is not vaginal or to injectable cabotegravir. Cutting pills is not an approved strategy. Stopping permanently is not required if an alternative regimen is feasible.

    Practical counselling and troubleshooting

    Side effects are usually early and mild. FTC/TDF and FTC/TAF can cause nausea or headache for 1–2 weeks — “start-up syndrome” that resolves. Cabotegravir causes injection pain for 1–2 days; rotation, ice, and activity advice help. Modest weight gain on FTC/TAF or cabotegravir is common.

    PrEP does not prevent other STIs. Condoms, regular STI screening, hepatitis B vaccination if non-immune, and linkage to medication for opioid use disorder and syringe services for people who inject drugs remain essential.

    Pregnancy and HBV matter for choice. FTC/TDF has the most pregnancy safety data and is CDC-preferred when pregnancy is planned; cabotegravir is not contraindicated in pregnancy. Oral PrEP suppresses HBV — brief stops can trigger alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and hepatitis B virus (HBV) deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) flares, so co-manage with hepatology.

    Cost and access. Generic FTC/TDF is inexpensive; FTC/TAF and cabotegravir remain costly. Most insurance covers PrEP without cost sharing, but verify. If cabotegravir is denied, start oral FTC/TDF same day and appeal.

    Pearl

    Same-day start wins — if baseline labs are pending and HIV antigen/antibody is negative with no acute symptoms, give PrEP today and follow up results within a week. Requiring a second visit loses 1 in 3 starts.

    When to get help

    Call for ID or pharmacy backup if: HIV test is indeterminate or RNA positive; creatinine clearance falls below threshold; patient is surface antigen positive for hepatitis B; pregnancy is planned on cabotegravir; or there is a gap longer than 4 weeks in injections with ongoing risk. A short curbside prevents long-term harm.

    A 22-year-old on FTC/TDF reports mild nausea and loose stools for 4 days after starting last week, now improving. HIV test is negative, creatinine clearance 88 mL/min. What do you advise?

    Start-up syndrome — mild gastrointestinal symptoms and headache in the first 2 weeks — is common with FTC/TDF and FTC/TAF, mild, self-limited, and not a reason to stop or switch. Reassurance plus a plan to check in preserves adherence. Switching is reserved for persistent intolerance or threshold issues.

    The five-minute version

    • Offer PrEP routinely to all sexually active adults and adolescents — do not wait to be asked. Recent STI, condomless sex with partners of unknown status, or sharing injection equipment are practical triggers.
    • Three FDA-approved options exist: FTC/TDF daily (all groups, needs CrCl ≥60), FTC/TAF daily (men and transgender women only, needs CrCl ≥30, not for vaginal exposure), and cabotegravir 600 mg intramuscular every 2 months (all groups, no renal threshold, needs RNA monitoring). Twice-yearly lenacapavir was approved in 2025 and is not yet in CDC guidance.
    • Before starting, always check HIV (antigen/antibody plus RNA if recent exposure or acute symptoms), hepatitis B serologies, creatinine/clearance, pregnancy when relevant, and STI screening. Never start two-drug PrEP with undiagnosed HIV.
    • Follow up every 3 months (oral) or at each injection with HIV testing, STI screening, adherence and pregnancy review, creatinine every 6 months on tenofovir, and re-assess need annually.
    • Manage gaps deliberately: missed oral doses — take when remembered; ≥7 days missed — RNA test before resuming. Late cabotegravir — 7-day window is okay, beyond that bridge with oral PrEP and repeat the initiation sequence if >4 weeks late.
    • Same-day start after a negative rapid HIV test is safe and improves uptake; mild nausea early or injection-site pain do not mean failure; and cost should not end the conversation — start generic FTC/TDF today if needed and sort coverage in parallel.
