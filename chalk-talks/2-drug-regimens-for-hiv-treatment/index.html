    2-Drug Regimens for HIV Treatment

    When two drugs are enough — and when they are not

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA Chalk Talk faculty (transcript corrupted — reconstructed from guidelines) Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 32-year-old man with newly diagnosed human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection comes for antiretroviral therapy (ART) counselling. HIV ribonucleic acid (RNA) is 42,000 copies per millilitre (copies/mL), cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count 580 cells per microlitre, hepatitis B surface antigen negative, creatinine 0.9 mg/dL, genotype pending. No prior ART or virologic failure and no other medicines. He asks whether two drugs could work and whether an injectable might be an option once suppressed.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Explain why three-drug ART became standard and what changed to make two-drug regimens viable.
    • Identify the guideline-endorsed two-drug regimens and where each fits — initial therapy, switch while suppressed, and long-acting injectable.
    • List the key eligibility checks and exclusions before starting or switching to a two-drug regimen, including hepatitis B virus (HBV) status and resistance history.
    • Apply a pre-switch checklist to decide whether a patient can safely step down to or start a two-drug regimen.

    Why three drugs became standard and why we are revisiting it

    Two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) plus a third agent — originally a protease inhibitor, later a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) or an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) — became standard because under-treatment bred resistance and early agents needed partners. Triple therapy also hedged against transmitted resistance while genotype was pending. The price was extra exposure: renal and bone effects with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, weight and metabolic signals with tenofovir alafenamide, and lifelong pill burden.

    Second-generation INSTIs changed the equation. Dolutegravir has a high barrier to resistance and a long half-life. Paired with a forgiving NRTI or NNRTI, it keeps suppression without a third drug — but only the studied pairs, in the right populations, have proved non-inferior to three drugs.

    Background

    The idea is not new. Early two-drug attempts with low-barrier agents failed and selected resistance. The modern two-drug regimens all include dolutegravir or long-acting cabotegravir — agents with a high resistance barrier and no need for a pharmacologic booster — plus a second agent chosen for compatibility and forgiveness, not potency alone.

    Pearl

    Two drugs can work, but only the studied pairs count. Dolutegravir plus lamivudine, dolutegravir plus rilpivirine, and cabotegravir plus rilpivirine are not interchangeable with any other two-drug combination.

    The two-drug regimens that made it into guidelines

    As of the 2024–2026 Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) and International Antiviral Society-USA guidelines, three two-drug strategies are endorsed, each for a different moment. All assume no active hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection needing therapy, no known resistance to the components, and no interacting medicines that lower levels.

    Common pitfall

    Starting a two-drug regimen as rapid start before hepatitis B serologies and genotype are known risks silent compromise. If hepatitis B surface antigen is positive you need a tenofovir-containing regimen, and if transmitted NRTI or INSTI mutations are present the “two” may functionally be one.

    Your clinic offers same-day ART to the patient in the vignette before hepatitis B serologies return. Which approach best balances rapid start with safety if you are considering dolutegravir plus lamivudine?

    When you must start before hepatitis B virus results are known, a tenofovir-containing three-drug regimen covers hepatitis B virus and hedges against high HIV RNA or transmitted resistance. Dolutegravir plus rilpivirine is not for initial therapy in naive patients, and dolutegravir plus lamivudine should only start once you know hepatitis B surface antigen is negative, HIV RNA is below the threshold, and no lamivudine or dolutegravir resistance is present. Delaying ART is inferior.

    Dolutegravir plus lamivudine — a starter and a switch option

    Dolutegravir plus lamivudine is the only two-drug regimen with randomised data for initial therapy. GEMINI-1 and GEMINI-2 randomised treatment-naive adults to dolutegravir plus lamivudine versus dolutegravir plus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine; suppression below 50 copies/mL was non-inferior through 148 weeks with no emergent INSTI resistance.

    Guidelines narrow who qualifies at start: HIV RNA at or above 500,000 copies/mL, hepatitis B surface antigen positive, known M184V/I or INSTI resistance, or need for rapid start before genotype is known all point away from this pair. Creatinine clearance below 50 mL/min excludes the fixed-dose tablet, and pregnancy favours three-drug ART.

    As a switch, TANGO randomised adults suppressed on tenofovir alafenamide-based triple therapy to stay or switch to dolutegravir plus lamivudine and maintained suppression with fewer renal and bone signals. Switch eligibility mirrors the naive exclusions plus no prior virologic failure and no archived NRTI or INSTI resistance.

    Pearl

    Think of dolutegravir plus lamivudine as tenofovir-sparing, not NRTI-sparing — you still rely on lamivudine activity. Archived M184V, even from a regimen years ago, disqualifies it.

    Background

    Lamivudine (3TC) and emtricitabine share the M184V pathway. A documented M184V/I on any prior genotype or a history of prolonged lamivudine or emtricitabine failure should be treated as lamivudine resistance, even if the most recent genotype shows wild type because resistant virus may be archived.

    A 45-year-old woman suppressed below 50 copies/mL for 18 months on bictegravir, tenofovir alafenamide, and emtricitabine asks to reduce pills because of weight gain. Hepatitis B surface antigen negative, HIV RNA history never above 20,000 copies/mL, no prior failure, genotype at diagnosis wild type, creatinine clearance 85 mL/min. What is the best next step?

    She is an ideal candidate for dolutegravir plus lamivudine switch: stably suppressed, no failure, no archived resistance, hepatitis B negative, and adequate renal function. Dolutegravir plus rilpivirine is also a switch option but brings a food requirement and more drug interactions, with no advantage here. Archived M184V disqualifies dolutegravir plus lamivudine even when suppressed, and cabotegravir plus rilpivirine would require full resistance and injection feasibility checks.

    Dolutegravir plus rilpivirine and cabotegravir plus rilpivirine — the suppressive switch and the injectable

    Both are switch regimens for suppressed patients, not starters for viraemic patients. SWORD-1 and SWORD-2 randomised suppressed adults to stay on three-drug ART or switch to dolutegravir plus rilpivirine; ATLAS, FLAIR and ATLAS-2M did the same for long-acting cabotegravir plus rilpivirine. All were non-inferior when eligibility was respected.

    Dolutegravir plus rilpivirine oral

    Dolutegravir 50 mg plus rilpivirine 25 mg once daily with a meal — food raises rilpivirine absorption. Proton pump inhibitors are contraindicated and histamine-2 receptor blockers and antacids need spacing. Eligibility requires suppression below 50 copies/mL for at least 6 months, no prior failure, and no known INSTI or NNRTI resistance. If hepatitis B surface antigen is positive, dropping tenofovir risks flare unless hepatitis B virus is separately treated.

    Cabotegravir plus rilpivirine long-acting

    Two gluteal intramuscular injections at the same visit. An optional oral lead-in of cabotegravir 30 mg plus rilpivirine 25 mg for about 28 days was studied, but direct-to-inject is now allowed. Loading is cabotegravir 600 mg plus rilpivirine 900 mg, then 400 mg plus 600 mg monthly or 600 mg plus 900 mg every 2 months after two monthly loads. Windows are tight — late injections need an oral bridge.

    Risk for confirmed virologic failure clusters with higher body mass index, prior failure, HIV-1 subtype A1 or A6, and archived rilpivirine mutations. Review all prior genotypes, confirm reliable attendance, and avoid inducers such as rifamycins, certain anticonvulsants, and systemic glucocorticoids. Pregnancy and psychiatric history warrant counselling.

    Pearl

    Adherence does not disappear with injections — it changes shape. A patient who misses injection windows and cannot take an oral bridge is at higher risk on long-acting therapy than on daily oral ART.

    Pearl

    Rilpivirine needs acid and food. Proton pump inhibitors and a meal-free morning dose quietly turn two active drugs into one.

    A 29-year-old man suppressed below 50 copies/mL for 2 years on dolutegravir, tenofovir alafenamide, and emtricitabine has well-controlled gastroesophageal reflux on omeprazole 20 mg daily. He wants cabotegravir plus rilpivirine every 2 months to avoid daily pills. Hepatitis B surface antigen negative, no prior failure, genotypes wild type, no missed visits history. What is the key barrier?

    This question tests nuance. Oral rilpivirine absorption collapses with proton pump inhibitors, making dolutegravir plus rilpivirine inappropriate on omeprazole. Long-acting injectable rilpivirine does not depend on gastric pH, so the omeprazole is not a contraindication to cabotegravir plus rilpivirine. Both every-month and every-2-month dosing exist, no CD4 threshold defines eligibility, and oral lead-in is now optional. The correct action is to verify archived genotypes, prior failure history, and injection feasibility before scheduling.

    Who should not get a two-drug regimen

    Success means selecting out those who need three. Guidelines and trial exclusions converge on a short list.

    • Hepatitis B virus co-infection requiring therapy. Lamivudine alone is inadequate for hepatitis B virus and selects hepatitis B virus resistance; dropping tenofovir risks hepatitis flare on stopping. Keep tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide in the regimen unless hepatitis B virus is separately and fully treated and hepatology concurs.
    • Active high-level viraemia or unknown resistance at start. Do not start dolutegravir plus lamivudine when HIV RNA is 500,000 copies/mL or higher, when baseline genotype shows M184V/I or INSTI mutations, or when you cannot yet exclude them. Treat first with a three-drug regimen and reconsider later.
    • History of virologic failure or archived resistance. Any prior failure or documented major NRTI, NNRTI, or INSTI mutation that compromises a component disqualifies that pair, even if the current HIV RNA is undetectable.
    • Pregnancy or trying to conceive at initiation. Data on two-drug initial therapy in pregnancy are insufficient; maintain a guideline-preferred three-drug regimen.
    • Significant drug interactions or intolerance that underdose a component. Rilpivirine with proton pump inhibitors, carbamazepine, rifampin, rifabutin, phenytoin, or systemic dexamethasone, and dolutegravir or cabotegravir with potent inducers that lower exposure, should prompt a different strategy.
    • Inability to meet administration requirements. Rilpivirine needs food; cabotegravir plus rilpivirine needs reliable gluteal injections, cold-chain and visit adherence, and a defined oral bridge plan for interruptions.
    Common pitfall

    Equating “undetectable now” with “no archived resistance.” A wild-type genotype drawn while suppressed tells you nothing about proviral deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) archive. Dig up every prior genotype, treatment history, and failure narrative before you narrow to two.

    Pearl

    Hepatitis B status is the first branch point for every two-drug decision. If you do not know hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B surface antibody, and hepatitis B core antibody, you are not ready to drop tenofovir.

    What to check before you switch or start

    Use a consistent checklist so nothing is missed.

    1. Confirm the moment. Initial therapy in a naive patient (only dolutegravir plus lamivudine qualifies) versus switch while suppressed (all three possible). Never start a viraemic patient on dolutegravir plus rilpivirine or cabotegravir plus rilpivirine.
    2. Pull the full history. Every ART regimen, duration, reason for change, and every prior genotype — not just the last HIV RNA.
    3. Verify virologic status. Document HIV RNA below 50 copies/mL and months suppressed for switches, baseline HIV RNA and CD4 for naive starts, and at least 6 months suppression where required.
    4. Screen hepatitis B virus and tuberculosis therapy. Hepatitis B surface antigen, surface antibody, core antibody, hepatitis B virus DNA if needed, and current or planned rifamycin use.
    5. Check kidneys, pregnancy, and interactions. Creatinine and creatinine clearance for dolutegravir plus lamivudine, pregnancy testing where relevant, and a full review including antacids and acid blockers.
    6. Confirm feasibility. Food for rilpivirine, injection clinic capacity, travel and insurance continuity, and an oral bridge plan if injections will be more than 7 days late.
    7. Document and monitor. Record why two drugs were chosen, what was excluded, and when to recheck HIV RNA — typically 4 to 8 weeks after change, then every 3 months, with renal and hepatitis B virus monitoring if tenofovir was stopped.

    If any check fails, stay on three drugs. A failed two-drug switch that selects INSTI or NNRTI resistance costs more than one fewer pill saves.

    A 38-year-old man with HIV RNA below 50 copies/mL for 9 months on efavirenz, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, and emtricitabine has no prior genotypes — he started therapy abroad and records are unavailable. Hepatitis B surface antigen negative, creatinine clearance 110 mL/min. He asks to switch to dolutegravir plus lamivudine to modernise therapy. What is the safest answer?

    Without treatment or genotype history you cannot exclude archived NRTI, NNRTI, or INSTI resistance. Remaining suppressed on a failing-prone history does not prove wild type. The safe move is to switch to a contemporary three-drug regimen with a high barrier and complete the evaluation, not to narrow to two drugs where hidden resistance would leave one active agent. Long-acting cabotegravir plus rilpivirine also requires no prior failure and no known resistance.

    The five-minute version

    • Two drugs are enough only for specific pairs: dolutegravir plus lamivudine (initial or switch), dolutegravir plus rilpivirine (switch only), and cabotegravir plus rilpivirine long-acting (switch only).
    • Do not start any two-drug regimen with active hepatitis B virus needing therapy, high HIV RNA at or above 500,000 copies/mL for dolutegravir plus lamivudine naive use, known or archived resistance to either component, or prior virologic failure.
    • Rilpivirine needs food and acid — proton pump inhibitors contraindicate oral rilpivirine, and all rilpivirine needs inducer-free co-medicines and adherence to timing.
    • For injectable cabotegravir plus rilpivirine, success depends on reliable injection visits and a defined oral bridge plan; missed injections need prompt oral coverage.
    • When in doubt, start or stay on three drugs until hepatitis B status, genotype, pregnancy, renal function, and full history clearly support narrowing.
