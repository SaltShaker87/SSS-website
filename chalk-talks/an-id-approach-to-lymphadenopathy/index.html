    An ID approach to lymphadenopathy

    Sorting signal from noise when nodes stay enlarged

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 9 minutes
    Case

    A 24-year-old man has 3 weeks of sore throat, fever, fatigue, and bilateral neck swelling. Examination shows 2 to 3 cm tender anterior and posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, pharyngeal erythema without exudates, palatal petechiae, and a palpable spleen tip. A second patient, a 38-year-old woman with untreated human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, has a 2-month history of painless, matted right cervical nodes that are firm and nontender with slightly erythematous overlying skin. Both wonder whether antibiotics will help.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Distinguish normal or reactive nodes from abnormal lymphadenopathy by site, size, and duration.
    • Separate localised from generalised lymphadenopathy and build an infectious differential for each.
    • Select a focused history, examination, and initial tests that avoid low-yield workups.
    • Decide when to observe, when to test, and when to refer for tissue diagnosis.

    What counts as abnormal

    Palpable nodes are common and often benign. Most adults have small, soft, shotty cervical or inguinal nodes from prior infections. The infectious diseases (ID) task is recognising when a node is too large, in the wrong place, or present too long.

    Use site-specific thresholds. Cervical and axillary nodes greater than 1.0 cm, epitrochlear nodes greater than 0.5 cm, any palpable supraclavicular node, and inguinal nodes greater than 1.5 to 2.0 cm are generally abnormal. Supraclavicular and epitrochlear nodes are almost never normal. Distinguish a stable 0.8 cm shotty node present for years from a new 2 cm tender node that appeared over days.

    Duration matters. Acute (less than 2 weeks) is usually reactive infection. Subacute (2 to 6 weeks) and chronic (greater than 6 weeks) raise concern for mycobacterial, fungal, malignant, or systemic disease. Consistency helps: soft and tender suggests acute infection, rubbery and nontender suggests lymphoma or chronic viral disease, hard and fixed suggests metastases, and matted or fluctuant suggests Mycobacterium tuberculosis or suppurative bacterial adenitis. Tenderness alone does not reassure; lymphoma can be tender and cat-scratch disease due to Bartonella henselae remains tender for weeks.

    Pearl

    Supraclavicular nodes are never watch-and-wait. Even a 0.5 cm supraclavicular node needs prompt explanation, usually imaging and referral.

    Localised versus generalised changes everything

    Before ordering tests, decide whether disease is localised to one region or generalised to two or more noncontiguous regions. This branch point narrows the differential more than any laboratory test.

    Localised adenopathy points to pathology in its drainage area. Anterior cervical nodes drain the pharynx and oral cavity. Isolated tender anterior cervical nodes with pharyngitis suggest group A Streptococcus, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), or acute HIV. Posterior cervical nodes are classic for EBV and toxoplasmosis due to Toxoplasma gondii. Unilateral axillary or epitrochlear adenopathy 1 to 3 weeks after a kitten scratch suggests B. henselae. Isolated inguinal nodes follow sexually transmitted infections such as syphilis due to Treponema pallidum or lower extremity skin infection.

    Generalised adenopathy implies systemic immune activation: acute viral illness, disseminated infection, autoimmune disease, drug reaction, or lymphoma. In ID practice the leading infectious causes are EBV, CMV, acute HIV seroconversion, secondary syphilis, toxoplasmosis, and tuberculosis (TB). Drugs such as phenytoin can mimic a mononucleosis-like syndrome.

    A 22-year-old student has 10 days of fatigue, sore throat, bilateral tender posterior cervical nodes, and atypical lymphocytosis. Rapid streptococcal antigen is negative and amoxicillin produced a diffuse rash. What is most likely?

    Posterior cervical nodes, marked fatigue, palatal petechiae, splenomegaly, atypical lymphocytosis, and rash after aminopenicillin are classic for EBV. Group A Streptococcus favours abrupt onset, anterior nodes, and exudates without atypical lymphocytes. Acute HIV and CMV can mimic EBV, but the rash and posterior nodes point to EBV. Test with heterophile or EBV serology, not another antibiotic.

    History and examination that matter

    Ask duration and pace, pain and tenderness, sore throat or dental pain, skin wounds or animal contact, sexual history with genital lesions, tick or insect exposure, travel and TB contacts, sick contacts, recent medicines in the last 8 weeks, and systemic symptoms including fever, night sweats, weight loss, and rash. Record HIV risk and immunosuppression.

    Examine systematically: largest node size, number of regions, consistency, mobility versus fixation, tenderness, erythema, warmth, fluctuance, and sinus tracts. Look beyond the node: oropharynx, dentition, scalp, skin of the drained area for inoculation lesions, genital examination for inguinal disease, liver and spleen, and full skin survey.

    Target tests. For a mononucleosis-like syndrome, a complete blood count (CBC) with differential often shows atypical lymphocytosis with mild transaminitis; heterophile or EBV-specific serology confirms. For persistent generalised or unexplained nodes, obtain HIV antigen/antibody and syphilis screening when risk exists. Add toxoplasma, CMV, or TB testing with interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) based on exposures.

    Background

    Atypical lymphocytes are activated CD8 T cells responding to EBV-infected B cells, not tumour cells. Their presence helps separate mononucleosis from streptococcal pharyngitis, where neutrophilia without atypical forms is typical.

    Pearl

    Posterior cervical or generalised nodes with prolonged fatigue point to EBV, CMV, acute HIV, or toxoplasmosis. Anterior, very tender nodes with abrupt fever and exudates point toward group A Streptococcus.

    Infectious patterns to recognise

    Four syndromes cover most referrals. Acute bilateral cervical nodes with pharyngitis in adolescents and young adults: EBV presents with 1 to 3 cm tender cervical nodes, fever, pharyngitis with or without exudates, palatal petechiae, and splenomegaly persisting 2 to 4 weeks. CMV is similar but pharyngitis and splenomegaly are less prominent and heterophile is negative. Acute HIV, 2 to 4 weeks after exposure, causes fever, small generalised nodes, pharyngitis, myalgias, mucocutaneous ulcers, and rash. Group A Streptococcus is abrupt with anterior nodes and exudates without generalised nodes.

    Regional subacute adenitis after animal contact: cat-scratch disease due to B. henselae begins as a papule 3 to 10 days after a scratch, followed 1 to 3 weeks later by tender regional nodes, often axillary or epitrochlear, that may suppurate. Tularemia due to Francisella tularensis and suppurative S. aureus adenitis produce marked erythema and warmth.

    Chronic matted cervical nodes: TB lymphadenitis (scrofula) presents as nontender, matted cervical nodes, sometimes with violaceous skin or sinus tracts, often 2 to 4 cm. Histology shows caseating granulomas; acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stain, culture, or nucleic acid testing confirms. In children 1 to 5 years, a single unilateral violaceous node with a clear chest radiograph suggests nontuberculous mycobacteria, often Mycobacterium avium complex.

    Generalised nodes with systemic illness: beyond EBV, CMV, and acute HIV, secondary syphilis produces generalised nontender nodes, rash including palms and soles, mucous patches, and condylomata lata. Disseminated TB and histoplasmosis cause mediastinal or generalised nodes with fever.

    Pearl

    Include acute HIV in every mononucleosis workup. A same-day HIV antigen/antibody plus RNA when exposure was recent changes counselling and transmission risk immediately.

    A 19-year-old has a tender 3 cm right axillary node for 3 weeks after a kitten scratch on the same arm. The node is fluctuant without cellulitis. What is best?

    Cat-scratch disease is usually self-limited. Needle aspiration can relieve suppuration; incision and drainage risks a chronic sinus. Azithromycin may help severe cases. Biopsy and PET CT are excessive for a classic exposure-linked node.

    Workup that avoids low-value testing

    Classify first, then test within the branch. If a tender cervical node is explained by proven group A streptococcal pharyngitis or a hand wound explains an epitrochlear node, treat the source and observe. Nodes enlarge 1 to 2 weeks after infection and remain palpable 3 to 4 weeks after resolution.

    For classic mononucleosis, confirm with CBC with differential, heterophile or EBV serology, and HIV screening. Do not routinely add toxoplasma or CMV panels unless heterophile is negative or presentation is atypical. Liver tests help separate EBV from streptococcal disease.

    For subacute or chronic localised nodes without a clear source, or any supraclavicular node, obtain neck ultrasound (US) to characterise necrosis and matting and to guide sampling, plus chest imaging when mediastinal disease or TB risk exists. Avoid whole-body PET CT as an initial screen for uncomplicated cervical nodes.

    For generalised adenopathy persisting beyond 2 to 4 weeks without a viral diagnosis, order HIV antigen/antibody, syphilis testing, CBC with differential, and targeted serologies based on epidemiology. Do not send broad panels on everyone.

    Common pitfall

    Starting antibiotics for every enlarged cervical node obscures mononucleosis, selects for resistance, and guarantees a rash if EBV is the cause. If streptococcal testing is negative and generalised features are present, test for EBV and HIV before prescribing.

    A 38-year-old woman with treated HIV has had painless matted 2 cm right cervical nodes for 8 weeks with night sweats. Chest radiograph is clear and IGRA is newly positive. Next step?

    Chronic matted nodes with systemic symptoms and new IGRA positivity suggest TB lymphadenitis even with a clear chest film. Tissue with granulomas and microbiology is diagnostic; empiric antibiotics and watchful waiting beyond 4 to 6 weeks are not appropriate.

    When tissue is the answer

    Refer for tissue when a node is greater than 2 cm and persists beyond 4 to 6 weeks without explanation, when a supraclavicular or hard fixed node is present, when generalised nodes remain unexplained after initial serologies, or when B symptoms (fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss greater than 10 percent) accompany adenopathy.

    Excisional biopsy is preferred over fine-needle aspiration (FNA) when architecture matters. Lymphoma versus reactive hyperplasia requires architecture and clonality that FNA often cannot provide. FNA with flow cytometry may suffice for suspected metastases or high surgical risk, but for matted nodes where TB or lymphoma is considered, excisional biopsy has the highest yield.

    Tell pathology before the procedure that infection is possible. Request fresh tissue held for microbiology plus formalin for histopathology: aerobic and anaerobic bacterial culture, AFB stain with mycobacterial culture and nucleic acid amplification where available, fungal stain and culture, and histology with haematoxylin and eosin, AFB, and Grocott stains. For Bartonella or Toxoplasma, consider immunostains or PCR if routine cultures are negative. Save serum before antibiotics or steroids.

    Background

    Granulomas are not TB-specific. B. henselae, Toxoplasma, histoplasmosis, sarcoidosis, and lymphoma can all produce granulomas; correlation with stains, cultures, and epidemiology establishes the cause.

    Pearl

    Tell pathology you are considering infection before the biopsy. A node divided correctly in the procedure room beats a second operation because all tissue went to formalin.

    Pitfalls that cause harm

    Three errors recur. First, anchoring on infection for every node. Hard, fixed, nontender nodes greater than 2 cm, supraclavicular nodes, or nodes that grow as weight falls should raise suspicion for malignancy, not a third antibiotic course.

    Second, misusing serology. Heterophile can be negative in the first week of EBV. Isolated EBV IgG without IgM usually means past infection. Toxoplasma IgM can persist for months to years, so a single IgM does not prove acute disease; avidity testing matters.

    Third, giving steroids or broad antibiotics before biopsy. Corticosteroids can shrink lymphoma and obscure diagnosis, and premature antibiotics sterilise cultures. Obtain cultures and arrange tissue before empiric therapy unless the patient is unstable. Counsel EBV patients to avoid contact sports for at least 3 to 4 weeks.

    Common pitfall

    Do not give corticosteroids to shrink an undiagnosed neck mass. You may temporarily normalise the examination and delay lymphoma or mycobacterial diagnosis.

    Pearl

    Every adult with unexplained generalised lymphadenopathy needs HIV testing, and any patient with generalised or inguinal nodes plus rash needs same-day syphilis testing.

    A 29-year-old man has 6 weeks of painless generalised nodes including epitrochlear nodes, diffuse macules on palms and soles, and oral mucous patches after new sexual partners. Best next test?

    Generalised nontender nodes with palmoplantar rash, mucous patches, and epitrochlear nodes is classic secondary syphilis due to T. pallidum. Same-day syphilis testing outranks EBV or toxoplasma panels, and tissue before serology is unnecessary when the syndrome is recognisable.

    The five-minute version

    • Decide localised versus generalised first: one draining region versus two or more noncontiguous regions changes the differential more than any lab test.
    • Supraclavicular and epitrochlear nodes are never normal; any node greater than 2 cm persisting beyond 4 to 6 weeks without explanation needs tissue.
    • Posterior cervical or generalised nodes with fatigue, atypical lymphocytes, and splenomegaly point to EBV, CMV, acute HIV, or toxoplasmosis — separate from anterior streptococcal nodes by history and CBC, not empiric amoxicillin.
    • Ask exposures that change pretest probability: cats and B. henselae, sexual history and syphilis, TB contacts, and recent HIV risk.
    • Prefer excisional biopsy over FNA when lymphoma or TB is likely; tell pathology to hold fresh tissue for AFB, bacterial, and fungal cultures before the procedure.
    • Every patient with unexplained generalised nodes needs same-day HIV screening, and those with generalised or inguinal nodes plus rash need same-day syphilis testing; avoid steroids before biopsy.
