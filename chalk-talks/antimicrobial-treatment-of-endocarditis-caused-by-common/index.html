    Antimicrobial treatment of endocarditis caused by common organisms

    Which organism, which valve, and how long — a resident's guide to getting the regimen right

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 58-year-old man with poor dentition is admitted with 3 weeks of low-grade fever, night sweats, and 4 kg weight loss. Two sets of blood cultures grow Streptococcus mitis with a penicillin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.06 mcg per mL. A new systolic murmur is noted. Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) shows a 9 mm vegetation on the native mitral valve without abscess. He reports a rash with penicillin as a child. The team asks what antibiotic to start, for how long, and whether he needs gentamicin.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Choose first-line therapy for infective endocarditis (IE) caused by viridans group streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus species, and HACEK (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella) organisms based on susceptibility and valve type.
    • Explain why valve type (native versus prosthetic) and organism susceptibility determine duration and whether combination therapy is needed.
    • Identify when aminoglycoside (gentamicin) synergy adds toxicity without benefit and when dual beta-lactam therapy is preferred.
    • Outline practical steps for empiric therapy, outpatient planning, and when to involve surgery.

    Before you pick an antibiotic — the framework

    Infective endocarditis is a biofilm infection on a valve surface. Bacteria are not floating in blood alone but embedded in vegetation where drug penetration is poor and the inoculum is high. That is why endocarditis needs bactericidal agents, prolonged courses, and higher doses than the same organism would need for bacteraemia alone. Treatment is defined by three variables: the organism and its susceptibility, whether the valve is native or prosthetic, and whether there are complications such as abscess, heart failure, or emboli.

    Do not start antibiotics until you have drawn two to three sets of blood cultures, unless the patient is in septic shock. Cultures after antibiotics are usually negative and leave you treating blind. Once cultures are drawn, start empiric therapy promptly and narrow when the organism returns. For native valve endocarditis (NVE) and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), involve infectious diseases, cardiology, and cardiothoracic surgery early.

    Duration is counted from the first day of effective therapy after bacteraemia clears, not from admission. Repeat blood cultures every 24 to 48 hours until negative, look for metastatic foci, and obtain a TEE in most adults — transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) misses small vegetations and abscesses.

    Background

    The 2015 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement on IE in adults remains the backbone for antimicrobial choices in the United States. It groups regimens by organism and by penicillin MIC rather than by empirical preference. The principles below follow that statement and subsequent stewardship updates.

    Pearl

    Culture first, then treat. Two to three blood culture sets before antibiotics save more lives than an early extra dose of vancomycin ever will.

    Viridans group streptococci and Streptococcus gallolyticus — susceptibility drives the choice

    Viridans group streptococci (including Streptococcus mitis, Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Streptococcus mutans) and Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly Streptococcus bovis) are the classic causes of subacute native valve IE after dental or colonic sources. The single most important number is the penicillin MIC.

    When the penicillin MIC is 0.12 mcg per mL or less, the organism is considered penicillin-susceptible. For NVE, aqueous crystalline penicillin G or ceftriaxone for 4 weeks is standard. A 2-week course of penicillin or ceftriaxone plus gentamicin for the first 2 weeks is an alternative for uncomplicated NVE in patients without renal dysfunction, but most clinicians now prefer the 4-week monotherapy to avoid aminoglycoside toxicity.

    When the penicillin MIC is 0.12 to 0.5 mcg per mL (relatively resistant), add gentamicin for the first 2 weeks to penicillin or ceftriaxone and treat for 4 weeks total for NVE. When the MIC is greater than 0.5 mcg per mL, treat as for enterococcal IE and consult infectious diseases.

    For PVE caused by penicillin-susceptible streptococci, treat for 6 weeks with penicillin or ceftriaxone; many add gentamicin for the first 2 weeks for PVE unless renal impairment argues against it. For true immediate penicillin hypersensitivity, vancomycin for 4 weeks (NVE) or 6 weeks (PVE) is the alternative.

    Always ask about the colon. Streptococcus gallolyticus bacteraemia, especially with endocarditis, is associated with colonic neoplasia and merits colonoscopy in adults.

    Common pitfall

    Do not default to vancomycin for streptococcal IE because of a vague childhood rash. Verify the allergy. Penicillin and ceftriaxone are more bactericidal, better tolerated, and allow outpatient ceftriaxone once daily. Use vancomycin only for true immediate hypersensitivity.

    The patient in the vignette has NVE due to penicillin-susceptible Streptococcus mitis (MIC 0.06). His penicillin rash as a child was mild and non-immediate. What is the best next step?

    For penicillin MIC ≤0.12, 4 weeks of penicillin or ceftriaxone suffices for NVE. A 2-week combination course is only for selected uncomplicated cases. A remote mild rash is usually not an immediate hypersensitivity; ceftriaxone is preferred over vancomycin after clarifying the allergy, with allergy input if needed. Oral therapy is not adequate for IE.

    Pearl

    For streptococcal IE, the MIC tells you the duration and whether gentamicin adds anything. MIC ≤0.12 means 4 weeks of a single beta-lactam for native valves.

    Staphylococcus aureus — MSSA versus MRSA and why gentamicin no longer helps native valves

    Staphylococcus aureus causes acute, destructive IE with high rates of embolization, abscess, and death. It affects both native and prosthetic valves and frequently seeds the spine, brain, and lungs in people who inject drugs. The key distinction is methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) versus methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

    For MSSA NVE, a beta-lactam is superior to vancomycin. Use nafcillin or oxacillin 12 g per day IV divided, or cefazolin 2 g IV every 8 hours (6 g per day) with normal renal function, for 6 weeks. Cefazolin is better tolerated than nafcillin for long courses but avoid it with central nervous system infection due to limited penetration. Do not add gentamicin for MSSA or MRSA NVE — it worsens renal injury without improving cure.

    For MRSA NVE, use vancomycin with therapeutic monitoring, or daptomycin 8 to 10 mg per kg IV daily for 6 weeks if vancomycin cannot be used. Daptomycin is inactivated by surfactant and cannot be used with pneumonia.

    For PVE due to Staphylococcus aureus, duration is at least 6 weeks. Traditionally rifampin and gentamicin were added for the first 2 weeks, but rifampin must never start until bacteraemia has cleared or resistance emerges. Many teams now omit gentamicin for PVE due to nephrotoxicity and use the core beta-lactam or vancomycin plus rifampin after clearance.

    Common pitfall

    Using vancomycin for MSSA endocarditis because it feels broader is not broader — it is worse. MSSA treated with vancomycin has higher failure rates than with nafcillin, oxacillin, or cefazolin. Reserve vancomycin for true MRSA or severe beta-lactam allergy.

    A 32-year-old woman who injects drugs has tricuspid NVE due to MSSA. Blood cultures clear on day 2 of nafcillin. She has normal renal function. What is the best regimen?

    MSSA NVE needs 6 weeks of a beta-lactam. Vancomycin is inferior for MSSA. Gentamicin adds nephrotoxicity without survival benefit for native valve staphylococcal IE. Two weeks is far too short, and rifampin is not a first-line partner for native valve disease nor started on day 1.

    Pearl

    If it is MSSA, use a beta-lactam. Every day on vancomycin for MSSA is a day with higher failure risk.

    Enterococcus faecalis and faecium — two beta-lactams are better than one

    Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium cause IE with a reputation for relapse if undertreated. They are tolerant to cell-wall agents alone, which is why monotherapy with penicillin or ampicillin fails. Traditional teaching was ampicillin plus gentamicin, but high-level aminoglycoside resistance and renal injury have shifted practice toward dual beta-lactam therapy.

    For penicillin-susceptible Enterococcus faecalis NVE or PVE, ampicillin plus ceftriaxone for 6 weeks is now preferred. The pair is synergistic by saturating different penicillin-binding proteins, works against many high-level aminoglycoside-resistant strains, and spares the kidney. Give ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours plus ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours.

    Ampicillin plus gentamicin for 4 to 6 weeks remains an option when the isolate is not highly resistant and the patient can tolerate it, with dosing by ideal body weight and monitoring of levels, renal function, and hearing.

    Enterococcus faecium is often ampicillin- and vancomycin-resistant. Therapy depends fully on susceptibilities: vancomycin-susceptible isolates may be treated with vancomycin plus gentamicin, but vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) needs infectious diseases input for agents such as daptomycin or linezolid and often needs surgical evaluation.

    Background

    High-level aminoglycoside resistance means synergy with gentamicin is lost. The lab reports it as high-level gentamicin resistance or gentamicin MIC greater than 500 mcg per mL. When present, gentamicin adds only toxicity.

    A 71-year-old man has PVE due to ampicillin-susceptible Enterococcus faecalis with high-level gentamicin resistance. Creatinine is 1.6 mg per dL. What is the best regimen?

    Mono-therapy fails for enterococcal IE. Gentamicin is both ineffective in high-level resistance and nephrotoxic with a borderline creatinine. Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone gives synergy without aminoglycoside and is the contemporary standard for susceptible Enterococcus faecalis PVE or NVE.

    Pearl

    For Enterococcus faecalis IE, think two drugs for six weeks. Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone has largely replaced ampicillin plus gentamicin, especially when the kidney is at risk.

    HACEK and other gram-negatives plus empiric therapy when you do not yet know

    HACEK organisms grow slowly but the treatment is straightforward. For NVE, ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily for 4 weeks is effective. For PVE or complicated disease, extend to 6 weeks. Ampicillin plus gentamicin is listed in older tables but ceftriaxone monotherapy is simpler and better tolerated.

    True gram-negative bacillary endocarditis (outside HACEK) is uncommon and usually healthcare-associated. It often requires combination therapy with a beta-lactam plus an aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone guided by susceptibilities and surgical consultation — do not extrapolate from HACEK.

    When you must start empiric therapy before cultures return for suspected NVE, cover Staphylococcus aureus, streptococci, and enterococci — commonly vancomycin plus ceftriaxone. For suspected PVE or early prosthetic infection, broaden to vancomycin plus gentamicin plus cefepime or ceftriaxone, then narrow rapidly when cultures return.

    A 45-year-old man has native valve IE but blood cultures are still pending. He is stable, has no prosthetic material, and no drug allergy. What empiric regimen is most appropriate while awaiting cultures?

    Empiric NVE therapy should cover MSSA, MRSA, streptococci, and enterococci. Vancomycin covers MRSA and enterococci pending susceptibilities, ceftriaxone covers streptococci including Streptococcus gallolyticus and MSSA. Ceftriaxone alone misses MRSA, piperacillin-tazobactam alone misses the need for reliable MRSA activity, and oral therapy is inadequate.

    Pearl

    HACEK is easy: ceftriaxone 4 weeks for native valves, 6 weeks for prosthetic. Save the complex combinations for non-HACEK gram-negatives.

    From inpatient to outpatient — duration, monitoring, and when to call surgery

    Duration thresholds are strict: viridans NVE 4 weeks, prosthetic 6 weeks; Staphylococcus aureus NVE and PVE at least 6 weeks; Enterococcus and HACEK NVE 4 to 6 weeks, PVE 6 weeks. Gentamicin when used is only for the first 2 weeks; rifampin for staphylococcal PVE is added only after bacteraemia clears.

    Monitor toxicity weekly: creatinine and electrolytes for beta-lactams and vancomycin, blood counts for nafcillin and ceftriaxone, liver tests for rifampin, creatine kinase for daptomycin, and vancomycin levels per pharmacy protocol. Repeat blood cultures until negative and repeat TEE if fever or emboli persist.

    Plan outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) early. Ceftriaxone and daptomycin once daily are OPAT-friendly; nafcillin every 4 hours is hard at home, so cefazolin every 8 hours is often used. Confirm stable housing, reliable access, and safety of a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC). Early oral step-down after an initial IV course is selective and needs infectious diseases input.

    Call surgery early for heart failure from valve destruction, perivalvular abscess or fistula, persistent bacteraemia beyond 5 to 7 days on appropriate therapy, large vegetations with recurrent emboli, or prosthetic dehiscence.

    Common pitfall

    Counting duration from the admission date instead of the first negative culture shortens therapy. Document the date cultures cleared, then count forward. A week of positive cultures followed by 4 weeks from admission is really only 3 weeks of effective therapy.

    A patient with MSSA PVE cleared bacteraemia on hospital day 4. The team started vancomycin, then added rifampin on day 2 while still bacteraemic. What needs correction?

    Rifampin started during bacteraemia selects resistance and can render it useless. Start rifampin only after blood cultures are negative and a primary active agent is on board. For MSSA, a beta-lactam beats vancomycin. Gentamicin for 6 weeks would be nephrotoxic, and stopping early guarantees relapse for PVE which needs at least 6 weeks.

    Pearl

    For staphylococcal prosthetic valve IE, rifampin is a companion, not a starter. Never start it while the blood is still positive.

    The five-minute version

    • Draw two to three blood culture sets before any antibiotics, then start empiric therapy — vancomycin plus ceftriaxone for NVE, broaden for PVE — and narrow once the organism is known.
    • Viridans streptococci and Streptococcus gallolyticus: use the penicillin MIC. MIC ≤0.12 means 4 weeks of penicillin or ceftriaxone for native valves and 6 weeks for prosthetic; add gentamicin only for relatively resistant isolates.
    • Staphylococcus aureus: MSSA needs a beta-lactam (nafcillin, oxacillin, or cefazolin) for 6 weeks; MRSA needs vancomycin or daptomycin for 6 weeks. Do not add gentamicin for native valves, and only add rifampin for prosthetic valves after bacteraemia clears.
    • Enterococcus faecalis: ampicillin plus ceftriaxone for 6 weeks is preferred over ampicillin plus gentamicin, especially with renal dysfunction or high-level aminoglycoside resistance.
    • HACEK: ceftriaxone 4 weeks native, 6 weeks prosthetic. Count duration from first negative culture, monitor for toxicity weekly, plan outpatient parenteral therapy early, and call surgery for heart failure, abscess, persistent bacteraemia, or recurrent emboli.
