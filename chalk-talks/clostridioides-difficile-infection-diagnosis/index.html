    Clostridioides difficile infection — diagnosis

    When to test, which assay to trust, and what a positive result actually means

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty — transcript not recoverable, reconstructed from guidelines Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 8 minutes
    Case

    A 68-year-old man on ceftriaxone for pneumonia develops five watery stools overnight with mild cramping, no blood. He had one dose of senna for constipation two days ago. He is afebrile with a soft abdomen. The night nurse asks whether to send a C. difficile test.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Decide when diarrhoea warrants testing for Clostridioides difficile.
    • Explain what glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), toxin enzyme immunoassay (EIA), and nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) each detect.
    • Interpret concordant and discordant two-step results at the bedside.
    • Apply diagnostic stewardship to avoid false positives from laxatives, formed stool, and repeat testing.

    Only test diarrhoea that needs explaining

    Clostridioides difficile infection is a clinical diagnosis supported by a test, not made by one. Clostridioides difficile, formerly Clostridium difficile, colonises 15–30% of hospitalised adults without symptoms. In colonisation the organism and even the toxin gene may be present but little toxin is made and there is no diarrhoea. A test for the organism will therefore be positive in many people without infection. The history matters more than the assay.

    Test when there is unexplained new-onset unformed stool — at least three loose stools in 24 hours that take the shape of the container (Bristol Stool Scale type 5–7) — without a more likely cause. Ask about laxatives in the past 48 hours, new enteral feeds or supplements, bowel preparation, chemotherapy, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) flare, and ischaemic colitis. Recent antibiotics, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, age over 65, and prior C. difficile raise risk but do not justify testing formed stool or diarrhoea with a clear alternative cause.

    Do not test formed stool, asymptomatic patients, or a single loose stool after a laxative. Most laboratories now reject formed specimens. Do not order a test of cure — C. difficile deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) can remain detectable for weeks after recovery — and do not repeat a negative test within seven days during the same episode unless the picture changes.

    Pearl

    If stool does not take the shape of the container, do not send it. Formed stool should not be tested for C. difficile.

    Common pitfall

    Testing because a patient is “high risk” without clinically significant diarrhoea turns colonisation into a reportable “infection” you feel obliged to treat.

    One loose stool six hours after senna, then one formed movement and no further diarrhoea. Next step?

    One loose stool after a laxative does not meet the threshold for unexplained clinically significant diarrhoea. Testing would most likely detect colonisation and drive unnecessary treatment. Correct the confounder and observe. Formed stool should not be tested, and empiric therapy without a syndrome is not indicated.

    What each assay actually detects

    Three assays dominate practice, and their targets explain their trade-offs.

    • Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) detects an enzyme made by all C. difficile, toxigenic and non-toxigenic. It is a sensitive screen for organism presence but not specific for infection.
    • Toxin enzyme immunoassay (EIA) for toxin A/B detects free toxin protein. It is specific for disease but insensitive, typically 60–80% versus reference methods, and misses low-toxin infections.
    • Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), usually polymerase chain reaction (PCR), detects the toxin B gene (tcdB). It is highly sensitive for a toxigenic strain (about 90–95%) but cannot tell if toxin is being made now, so it cannot distinguish colonisation from infection and can remain positive for weeks after recovery.

    Reference standards — cell cytotoxicity neutralisation assay and toxigenic culture — are accurate but slow. They define how we judge routine assays, not what you wait for.

    Pearl

    NAAT is a test for a gene, not for toxin. A positive NAAT says “a toxigenic strain is here.” Only the patient can tell you whether there is disease.

    Why most hospitals use a two-step algorithm

    A single sensitive test would suffice if every patient tested had unexplained watery diarrhoea. In practice testing is uneven and false positives cause harm — isolation, antibiotics, longer stay, and delayed diagnosis of the true cause. Most centres therefore pair sensitivity with specificity.

    Know which algorithm your laboratory uses:

    • GDH plus toxin EIA with NAAT as arbiter: Both done together. Both positive suggests infection; both negative makes infection unlikely. GDH-positive and toxin-negative reflexes to NAAT — a positive NAAT then requires clinical correlation.
    • GDH plus NAAT, or NAAT plus toxin EIA: variants on the same principle — a sensitive screen plus a specific toxin or gene test rather than NAAT alone.

    The 2017 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) guideline advises institutions without strict criteria to ensure appropriate testing to use a stool toxin test as part of a multistep algorithm — for example GDH plus toxin EIA, GDH plus NAAT, or NAAT plus toxin EIA — rather than NAAT alone. Where stewardship is strong and only unexplained clinically significant diarrhoea is tested, NAAT alone may be acceptable, but many centres keep the algorithm because stewardship often fails overnight.

    Check your record. You may see three lines — GDH, toxin, NAAT — or a single “positive by NAAT.” A single line hides whether toxin was ever sought.

    A 74-year-old woman on levofloxacin has six watery stools in 24 hours, no laxatives, tachycardia and white cells 14,000 per microlitre. GDH positive, toxin EIA negative, NAAT positive. Best interpretation?

    Toxin EIA is specific but insensitive, so a single negative does not exclude infection. With a toxigenic strain detected and clinically significant unexplained diarrhoea with systemic features, manage as infection. The same pattern with one loose stool after laxatives would point to colonisation.

    How to handle discordant results

    “PCR positive, toxin negative” is the commonest source of confusion. It does not automatically equal infection. Patients who are NAAT-positive but toxin-negative are on average less ill and more likely to have an alternative cause than toxin-positive patients; many improve without C. difficile therapy.

    Ask four questions:

    1. Does the diarrhoea meet the threshold? Three or more unformed stools in 24 hours, new onset, unexplained.
    2. Is there a more likely cause? Active laxatives, tube feeds, bowel prep, IBD, or other medicines.
    3. Does the course fit? Fever, cramping, leukocytosis, acute kidney injury or profuse watery stool favours infection; a single loose stool does not.
    4. What does the full algorithm say? GDH-negative makes infection unlikely; GDH-positive/toxin-positive supports it; GDH-positive/toxin-negative/NAAT-positive is indeterminate.

    Document your reasoning: “toxigenic C. difficile by NAAT, toxin negative, one loose stool after prep — colonisation, not infection; monitor, no repeat test” prevents reflexive treatment by the next team.

    Pearl

    GDH-positive, toxin-negative, NAAT-positive is the fork in the road. Mild self-limited diarrhoea after laxatives usually means colonisation; profuse watery diarrhoea on antibiotics without alternative cause favours infection.

    Pitfalls before the specimen reaches the lab

    Most errors are pre-analytical — ordering and collection, not analysis.

    • Formed stool: Should be rejected. A formed NAAT-positive is almost always colonisation.
    • Laxatives or bowel prep within 48 hours: Senna, bisacodyl, lactulose, polyethylene glycol and enemas cause diarrhoea that triggers testing. Hold as-needed laxatives and re-evaluate.
    • Tube feeds: New feeds and sorbitol-containing medicines often cause loose stools — review nutrition history first.
    • Delayed transport: Toxin degrades at room temperature, increasing false-negative toxin EIA. Send fresh stool promptly or refrigerate at 2–8°C.
    • Repeat toxin EIAs: Serial toxin EIAs after a negative add little. If suspicion remains high, discuss a single NAAT-based retest rather than daily toxin tests.
    Common pitfall

    Repeating toxin EIA daily “to be sure” wastes time. Use the algorithm and, if needed, a single documented NAAT-based reassessment.

    Formed stool (Bristol type 3) sent “because he is high risk” returns NAAT-positive, toxin EIA-negative, one movement per day. Appropriate response?

    Formed stool should never have been tested. NAAT-positive with toxin-negative from formed stool is colonisation. Treatment and isolation for active infection are not warranted. Feedback prevents recurrence.

    When to repeat — and when to stop

    After a negative result, do not repeat within seven days during the same episode unless diarrhoea persists without alternative cause and the first specimen was unformed and timely. A single negative NAAT has high negative predictive value. One repeat on fresh unformed stool after 48 hours may be reasonable when suspicion remains very high — document why.

    After a positive result, do not test for cure. Colonisation can persist for weeks to months; NAAT stays positive after symptoms resolve. Return toward baseline stool frequency and form defines response, not a negative assay. Retesting after improvement creates a new “positive” that may be mislabelled as recurrence.

    Suspected recurrence requires new unexplained diarrhoea after complete resolution of the prior episode. Post-infectious bowel symptoms with occasional formed stools plus a positive NAAT is not relapse.

    Pearl

    Never order a test of cure. DNA can persist for weeks; clinical recovery is the marker of response.

    Four days after treatment for toxin-positive infection the patient has two formed stools daily and mild cramping. A repeat NAAT sent “to check cure” is positive. What now?

    Repeat NAAT after improvement is not a test of cure. DNA can persist for weeks; improvement in frequency, form, pain and fever defines response. Do not prolong antibiotics on the basis of a lingering positive.

    Diagnostic stewardship on rounds

    Accuracy depends more on stewardship than on a new kit. Three habits help:

    1. Count and characterise before ordering. How many stools in 24 hours, Bristol type, laxatives, enemas, bowel prep, or new feeds? “One loose stool after lactulose” or “formed” means do not test. Note it clearly.
    2. Know the algorithm and read every line. Does your lab report GDH, toxin, and NAAT together, or NAAT alone? When you see “positive,” find the toxin line. Teach the team: “GDH-positive, toxin-negative, NAAT-positive — correlation needed.”
    3. Protect isolation and treatment for those who need them. True infection warrants contact precautions and review of antibiotics, acid suppression, and antimotility agents. Colonisation does not — over-isolation harms too.

    Send fresh unformed stool promptly, avoid urine contamination, refrigerate at 2–8°C if delayed, and do not send rectal swabs unless validated. When the algorithm is negative but diarrhoea persists, revisit laxatives, feeds, and medicines that cause diarrhoea, and consider other infectious and non-infectious causes.

    Background

    Requiring an order question — “three or more unformed stools in 24 hours without laxatives?” — and blocking repeats within seven days while rejecting formed stool cuts reported healthcare-onset events by 30–50%, largely by preventing detection of colonisation.

    The five-minute version

    • Only test unexplained new-onset watery diarrhoea — three or more unformed stools (Bristol 5–7) in 24 hours — after excluding laxatives within 48 hours, tube feeds, and other causes. Never test formed stool or send a test of cure.
    • GDH screens for organism, toxin EIA detects free toxin (specific but insensitive), NAAT detects the toxin gene (sensitive but cannot distinguish colonisation).
    • Most hospitals use a two-step algorithm, often GDH plus toxin EIA with NAAT as arbiter. Read every line; a single “positive” without toxin is incomplete.
    • GDH-positive, toxin-negative, NAAT-positive is indeterminate. Treat only when diarrhoea is clinically significant and unexplained; otherwise it is usually colonisation.
    • Do not repeat a negative test within seven days unless diarrhoea persists without explanation, and never monitor therapy with repeat testing.
