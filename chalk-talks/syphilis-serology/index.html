    Syphilis serology

    How to read treponemal and nontreponemal tests without getting burned

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 10 minutes
    Case

    A 32-year-old man presents to the emergency department with a painless genital ulcer that healed two weeks ago and now has a diffuse macular rash on his palms and soles, sore throat, and generalised lymphadenopathy. He reports a new sexual partner in the past three months. A routine prenatal panel from his partner, who is 10 weeks pregnant, returns with a positive syphilis screen. You are called to interpret both results and advise what to do next.

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Distinguish nontreponemal tests (rapid plasma reagin and Venereal Disease Research Laboratory) from treponemal tests and explain what each measures.
    • Compare the traditional and reverse syphilis screening algorithms and decide when a third test is needed.
    • Interpret common result patterns, including biological false positives and treated past infection.
    • Apply titre thresholds and fourfold changes to assess disease activity and response to therapy.
    • Identify when to pursue cerebrospinal fluid testing and how pregnancy changes management.

    Why syphilis serology trips everyone up

    Treponema pallidum cannot be cultured routinely — we see the immune response, not the organism. That response is measured by two different families of tests, and most confusion comes from mixing up what each family tells you.

    Clinically syphilis moves through primary (chancre), secondary (disseminated rash and mucosal lesions), latent (asymptomatic with positive serology), and tertiary stages, but serology does not stage disease. A titre is not a stage. Stage comes from symptoms, examination, history, and exposure; serology supports or refutes it. A positive result can also mean past treated infection or a false positive, because antibodies persist differently.

    Background

    We use direct detection of T. pallidum (darkfield microscopy, polymerase chain reaction) rarely on the ward. The familiar tests are the Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test (1946) and rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test (1950s), followed by treponemal antigen tests — all indirect measures.

    Two families of tests

    Keep the two families separate in your head. Nontreponemal tests measure antibody against cardiolipin-cholesterol-lecithin antigen — a marker of cell damage, not a syphilis-specific antigen. Treponemal tests measure antibody that binds T. pallidum proteins directly.

    Nontreponemal tests are the only ones reported as a titre (1:2, 1:4, 1:8, doubling each step). Higher titre often means more active disease, but very early primary and late latent disease can both be low, and titres can decline without treatment in late disease.

    Treponemal tests often become reactive one to two weeks earlier than nontreponemal tests and usually remain reactive for life after treatment. A reactive treponemal test does not tell you whether infection is active — the key fact for overnight call.

    Pearl

    Nontreponemal titre tracks activity; treponemal result tracks exposure. Only one of them should move with treatment.

    Background

    The RPR and VDRL are equally valid nontreponemal tests but are not interchangeable numerically — a 1:16 on RPR is not equivalent to 1:16 on VDRL, and you should follow titres with the same test in the same laboratory.

    Traditional versus reverse screening

    Both algorithms answer the same question — does this person have syphilis right now — but they start at opposite ends. Knowing which algorithm your laboratory uses explains most of the confusing call results.

    Traditional algorithm

    The laboratory starts with a nontreponemal test (RPR or VDRL). If nonreactive, screening stops. If reactive, a treponemal test confirms. This approach is inexpensive and correlates with active disease, but it misses very early primary syphilis (before reagin appears) and some late latent disease where the nontreponemal test has reverted to nonreactive.

    Reverse algorithm

    The laboratory starts with an automated treponemal test (EIA or CIA). If nonreactive, screening stops. If reactive, a nontreponemal test with titre is done. If the treponemal test is reactive but the nontreponemal test is nonreactive, a second, different treponemal test (usually TP-PA) adjudicates. About 60 to 70 percent of those discordant results are past treated infection or late latent infection; the remainder are false-positive treponemal screens.

    Pearl

    If your report says EIA reactive, RPR nonreactive, look for the second treponemal result before you decide. Without it you are guessing.

    Common pitfall

    Treating every reactive treponemal screen as active syphilis. The reverse algorithm increases detection of past infection; overtreatment is common and causes real harm in pregnancy and in penicillin-allergic patients.

    How to read titres and the result patterns you will see

    Three rules make most reports make sense.

    1. Titres are reported as the reciprocal of the last reactive dilution. 1:64 is higher than 1:8. A change of two dilutions (for example, 1:64 to 1:16) is a fourfold change and is clinically significant; a change of one dilution (1:64 to 1:32) is within laboratory variation and is not.
    2. The same specimen tested by different laboratories or by RPR versus VDRL can differ by one dilution without meaning anything has changed. Always compare titres from the same test in the same laboratory.
    3. The prozone phenomenon — falsely low or nonreactive nontreponemal result because antibody excess blocks the flocculation reaction — can occur in secondary syphilis and in pregnancy when titres are very high. If the clinical picture strongly suggests syphilis and the RPR is nonreactive, ask the laboratory to dilute the specimen further.

    Pearl

    A low titre (1:1 to 1:4) with a reactive treponemal test is the hardest pattern. Do not assume false positive — take a sexual history, check for prior treatment records, and examine for chancre, rash, condyloma lata, or lymphadenopathy.

    Background

    Biological false-positive nontreponemal tests are typically low titre (1:8 or less) and transient. Antiphospholipid syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, malaria, endocarditis, recent vaccination, and pregnancy are classic triggers. A treponemal test will be nonreactive.

    A 28-year-old man with no prior syphilis history has an EIA reactive, RPR nonreactive, TP-PA reactive result. He has no symptoms. What is the most likely explanation?

    Treponemal tests stay reactive for life, so EIA reactive with TP-PA reactive and RPR nonreactive means treponemal exposure has occurred. When history is negative and examination is unrevealing, this pattern is often late latent syphilis (infection more than 12 months ago or of unknown duration) or previously treated infection with records you cannot find. The RPR is not falsely positive here — it is correctly nonreactive, as expected in late disease.

    A patient with a known RPR of 1:128 two weeks ago now has an RPR of 1:32 drawn in a different laboratory. Has she had a significant response?

    A change from 1:128 to 1:32 is two dilutions and would be a fourfold decline if both results came from the same laboratory using the same method. Between laboratories, one dilution of difference is within expected variation, so you need a same-lab comparison before calling it a response. Also, a single post-treatment titre too early can be misleading.

    Stage, neurosyphilis, and when to tap

    Staging drives treatment — penicillin remains the drug of choice for all stages, but dose and duration differ by stage, so getting the stage right matters. Stage by history and examination, not by titre alone.

    • Primary and secondary: chancre, disseminated rash (often palms and soles), mucous patches, condyloma lata, lymphadenopathy. Usually nontreponemal titre at least 1:8 and treponemal reactive. Early chancre can be seronegative — repeat in one to two weeks.
    • Early latent: asymptomatic, acquired within past 12 months — requires documented seroconversion, fourfold titre rise, recent compatible symptoms, or a partner with primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis.
    • Late latent / unknown duration: asymptomatic without evidence of acquisition within 12 months; titre often 1:4 or less or nonreactive.

    Neurosyphilis can occur at any stage. Test cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for neurologic, ophthalmic, or auditory symptoms, tertiary signs, or serologic treatment failure. The CSF-VDRL test is specific but not sensitive (reactive confirms, nonreactive does not exclude). The CSF FTA-ABS test is sensitive but not specific and is most useful when nonreactive. Pleocytosis and elevated protein support the diagnosis; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection complicates interpretation.

    Common pitfall

    Ordering a lumbar puncture for every patient with latent syphilis and a positive serology. In an immunocompetent, neurologically normal patient with no auditory or ophthalmic symptoms, routine CSF examination is not required. Reserve it for symptoms, treatment failure, or when you would do it anyway.

    Pearl

    Any patient with syphilis and visual changes, hearing loss, or cranial nerve findings needs urgent ophthalmology or otolaryngology evaluation and CSF testing — ocular and otosyphilis can progress quickly and change therapy to a neurosyphilis regimen.

    A 45-year-old man with late latent syphilis (RPR 1:4, TP-PA reactive) has no headache, no vision or hearing changes, and a normal neurologic examination. HIV test is negative. Does he need a lumbar puncture before treatment?

    Routine lumbar puncture in an otherwise well, neurologically intact patient with late latent disease is not recommended. Treatment as late latent is appropriate, with close clinical and serologic follow-up. A lumbar puncture is reserved for neurologic, ophthalmic, or auditory signs, evidence of tertiary disease, or serologic treatment failure.

    Pregnancy, newborns, and HIV co-infection

    Two populations change your threshold to act.

    Pregnancy

    Syphilis in pregnancy can cause stillbirth and congenital disease. Screen at the first prenatal visit and repeat at 28 weeks and delivery when risk or prevalence is high. Treat without waiting for confirmatory testing when suspicion is strong. Only penicillin treats the fetus — desensitise for allergy. A fourfold higher infant nontreponemal titre than the mother (for example, infant 1:32 vs maternal 1:8) suggests congenital infection, but absence does not exclude it; evaluate by examination, long-bone films, and CSF testing as indicated.

    HIV co-infection

    Test every patient with syphilis for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and vice versa. Serologic responses are similar, though titres may be higher. Use the same indications for CSF examination as in HIV-negative patients, with a lower threshold to evaluate neurologic symptoms.

    Background

    Congenital syphilis is defined by maternal history, infant examination, titre comparison, and direct testing together — not a single value. Involve paediatrics and obstetrics early.

    Following titres after treatment and the serofast state

    Nontreponemal titres are the follow-up tool. Treponemal tests are not used to judge cure.

    • Repeat the same nontreponemal test in the same laboratory at 6, 12, and 24 months (more often in pregnancy or if reinfection is a concern).
    • An adequate response is a fourfold (two-dilution) decline — expected by 6 to 12 months in early syphilis; slower in late latent disease.
    • A fourfold rise suggests reinfection or treatment failure — re-treat, re-stage, retest for HIV, and evaluate partners.
    • Failure to decline fourfold by 12 to 24 months without reinfection should prompt re-evaluation for neurosyphilis and stage, not automatic retreatment.

    Some patients remain serofast — titre declines but stabilises at 1:1 to 1:8 for years despite adequate therapy and no reinfection, especially after late latent treatment. With documented prior treatment, no new exposure, and no signs, a stable low titre is usually serofast, not failure. Verify history and examine rather than retreating on the number alone.

    Pearl

    Document the titre, the test type, and the date of treatment in the discharge summary. Future clinicians cannot judge a fourfold change if they cannot find the baseline.

    Common pitfall

    Retesting with a treponemal EIA to check for cure. It will stay reactive and will mislead you and the patient into thinking treatment has not worked.

    A 26-year-old man treated for secondary syphilis (baseline RPR 1:64) returns at 7 months with an RPR of 1:16, no new sexual exposures, and no symptoms. How do you counsel him?

    A decline from 1:64 to 1:16 is two dilutions — a fourfold fall — which meets the expected response for early syphilis at 6 to 12 months. Complete reversion to nonreactive often takes 12 to 24 months or longer. A treponemal test would remain reactive and does not assess cure, and a lumbar puncture is not indicated in an asymptomatic patient with an adequate titre response.

    The five-minute version

    • Nontreponemal tests (RPR, VDRL) measure anti-cardiolipin antibody and are reported as titres that track disease activity; treponemal tests (TP-PA, FTA-ABS, EIA, CIA) measure anti-T. pallidum antibody and usually stay reactive for life.
    • Traditional screening starts with RPR/VDRL, reverse screening starts with a treponemal EIA/CIA — know which your laboratory uses so you can interpret discordant results.
    • EIA reactive, RPR nonreactive, TP-PA reactive usually means late latent or past treated syphilis; EIA reactive, RPR nonreactive, TP-PA nonreactive is usually a false-positive screen.
    • A fourfold (two-dilution) change in the same nontreponemal test is significant; one dilution is laboratory noise. Follow titres in the same laboratory with the same test type.
    • Stage by symptoms, examination, and history — not by titre alone — and reserve cerebrospinal fluid testing for neurologic, ophthalmic, or auditory findings, tertiary signs, or serologic failure.
    • In pregnancy treat with penicillin without delay when syphilis is suspected, and remember that a stable low titre after documented adequate therapy is often a serofast state, not failure.
