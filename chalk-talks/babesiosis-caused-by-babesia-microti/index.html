    Babesiosis caused by Babesia microti

    A tick-borne red cell parasite that looks like malaria but is not

    Source: IDSA Chalk Talk series Original speaker: IDSA faculty Adapted: August 2026 Read time: 9 minutes
    Case

    A 68-year-old man from coastal Rhode Island presents in late July with 5 days of fever to 38.8°C, sweats, fatigue, and dark urine. He clears brush on weekends and recalls a tick bite 2 weeks ago. He had a splenectomy 20 years ago. Exam shows fever, mild jaundice, and tachycardia. Labs: hemoglobin 9.2 g/dL, platelets 68,000 per µL, total bilirubin 3.1 mg/dL (indirect), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 680 U/L, haptoglobin undetectable, creatinine 1.4 mg/dL. He was started on ceftriaxone for sepsis. What else should you send now?

    By the end of this talk you will be able to
    • Identify who is at risk for severe babesiosis and how Babesia microti spreads.
    • Recognize the clinical and lab pattern of babesiosis and distinguish it from malaria and anaplasmosis.
    • Choose thin blood smear versus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and use parasitemia to guide care.
    • Select treatment by severity and host factors and know when exchange transfusion is considered.

    Why babesiosis matters on call

    Babesia microti is an intraerythrocytic protozoan parasite — it lives inside red blood cells (RBCs) — and is the main cause of human babesiosis in the United States. It shares its vector, the black-legged tick Ixodes scapularis, with Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease) and Anaplasma phagocytophilum (anaplasmosis). The white-footed mouse is the reservoir; deer amplify tick numbers but are not infected.

    Its range has expanded beyond New England and the upper Midwest, with peak transmission May through October. Many infections are asymptomatic, but severe disease clusters predictably.

    Babesiosis is also transfusion transmitted. Babesia survives blood banking, and asymptomatic donors have transmitted infection, leading to Food and Drug Administration (FDA) donor screening in endemic states since 2020. Think babesiosis 1 to 4 weeks after a tick bite or 1 to 9 weeks after transfusion, even without a recalled bite — more than half of patients never notice the tick.

    Background

    Other species cause disease elsewhere: Babesia divergens in Europe and Babesia duncani on the US West Coast can cause similar or more fulminant illness. Unless noted, this talk refers to B. microti in the northeastern and midwestern United States.

    Pearl

    Think babesiosis when fever, hemolysis, and thrombocytopenia follow summer outdoor exposure in an endemic region — especially with prior asplenia, even decades ago.

    How the parasite makes people sick

    After 36 to 72 hours of nymphal feeding, sporozoites enter blood and invade RBCs. B. microti divides by budding into up to four merozoites, sometimes seen as the pathognomonic tetrad or Maltese cross on Giemsa- or Wright-stained thin smear. Most infected cells show only pleomorphic ring forms that are easy to miss or confuse with Plasmodium falciparum.

    Parasitized RBCs hemolyze intravascularly and by splenic clearance, so asplenia, age over 50 years, and immunosuppression — especially rituximab, malignancy, and HIV with low CD4 count — predispose to high parasitemia. Hemolysis raises LDH and indirect bilirubin and lowers haptoglobin; thrombocytopenia alone does not mean DIC.

    One tick can carry several pathogens: 10 to 20 percent of patients also have Lyme disease. A positive Lyme test does not explain hemolysis — persistent fever or cytopenias should prompt testing for co-infection.

    Pearl

    Babesia has no liver stage. Relapse is not from dormant liver forms like Plasmodium vivax — it is persistent blood-stage infection when immunity is impaired or therapy is too short.

    Who gets sick and what it looks like

    Many healthy young adults have a self-limited flu-like illness or no symptoms. When symptomatic, patients report fever, chills, sweats, headache, myalgias, arthralgias, anorexia, and marked fatigue, sometimes with cough, nausea, abdominal pain, or dark urine from hemoglobinuria.

    Exam may show fever, mild splenomegaly, jaundice, or pallor. Rash is not a feature — erythema migrans points to Lyme co-infection. Severe hemolysis can cause acute kidney injury, ARDS, heart failure, DIC, and shock, especially when parasitemia tops 4 to 10 percent or the spleen is absent.

    Labs often flag the diagnosis before the smear: normocytic anemia with reticulocytosis, platelets often 40,000 to 100,000 per µL, and normal or low leukocytes — atypical for sepsis — plus indirect hyperbilirubinemia, high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and mildly raised AST and ALT. Hemolytic anemia plus thrombocytopenia in summer should put babesiosis at the top.

    Common pitfall

    Do not anchor on bacterial sepsis. In an older or asplenic patient with summer exposure, order a stat thin smear and Babesia PCR on the same draw — waiting for consult costs a day.

    How to make the diagnosis

    Thin smear is fastest. On Wright- or Giemsa-stained thin smear, an experienced microscopist may see intraerythrocytic rings of varying size, occasional extracellular forms, and rarely tetrads. The smear also quantifies percent parasitemia (infected RBCs divided by total RBCs times 100), which guides severity and response. Sensitivity falls below 0.1 percent parasitemia, after antibiotics, or when the lab is not told to look for parasites. Order “thick and thin smears for malaria/babesiosis” and tell the lab you suspect Babesia.

    Whole-blood PCR is more sensitive, distinguishes B. microti from Plasmodium, and stays positive weeks after smears turn negative — the test of choice when smear is negative but suspicion remains.

    Serology detects immunoglobulin G (IgG) and helps weeks after exposure when PCR is negative. A single titer cannot separate acute from past infection; a fourfold rise or high titer with compatible illness supports recent infection, but antibody can persist for years.

    Background

    Hemoglobinuria and spherocytes can mimic autoimmune hemolysis. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test is usually negative — do not start steroids before smear and PCR.

    A 72-year-old asplenic man with fever, hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL, platelets 55,000 per µL, LDH 900 U/L, and 2 weeks ago camping on Cape Cod has smear read as “rare rings, extracellular forms, no pigment.” What next?

    Rings without pigment and with extracellular forms favor Babesia, but low parasitemia cannot be eyeballed reliably. In an asplenic older adult with hemolysis after tick exposure, send PCR, quantify parasitemia, and start therapy — serology reflects past exposure. Malaria drugs do not treat Babesia.

    Pearl

    Always quantitate parasitemia on the initial and follow-up smears. The percentage drives admission, exchange transfusion, and stop decisions.

    Treatment for the patient in front of you

    Severity and immunity set regimen and duration. Admit initially to track hemolysis and organ function — asplenic and immunocompromised patients can worsen quickly.

    For mild to moderate disease in an immunocompetent host, atovaquone plus azithromycin is preferred. It matches clindamycin plus quinine for non-severe disease and is far better tolerated. Usual adult course: atovaquone 750 mg orally with a fatty meal every 12 hours plus azithromycin 500 mg day 1 then 250 mg daily, for 7 to 10 days. Symptoms and parasitemia often improve within 48 hours; smear is often negative within a week, though PCR may stay positive longer.

    For severe disease — parasitemia generally 10 percent or greater, marked hemolysis, pulmonary or renal injury, hypotension, or rapid progression in an asplenic or older host — traditional guidance favors clindamycin plus quinine: clindamycin 600 mg orally every 8 hours or 300 to 600 mg intravenously every 6 hours plus quinine 650 mg orally every 8 hours. Quinine toxicity (cinchonism: tinnitus, hearing loss, vertigo, nausea, hypoglycemia, QT prolongation) often limits it, so many centers now use atovaquone plus azithromycin even for severe disease with close monitoring, reserving clindamycin/quinine for critical illness or persistent high parasitemia.

    Immunocompromised, asplenic, or rituximab-exposed patients often need at least 6 weeks and until smears are negative for 2 weeks — relapse is common if you stop at 10 days. Coordinate duration with infectious diseases and pharmacy; never use a single drug alone.

    Supportive care: transfuse for symptomatic anemia, protect kidneys from hemoglobinuria, watch electrocardiogram (ECG) and glucose if using quinine, and consider exchange transfusion for parasitemia 10 percent or greater with severe hemolysis or organ dysfunction. Steroids have no role.

    Common pitfall

    One tick can deliver Babesia, Lyme, and anaplasmosis together. Finding one should prompt testing for the others when the picture fits — each needs different therapy.

    Antibabesial regimens for adults — verify all doses with pharmacy
    Scenario Regimen (adult) Typical duration
    Mild to moderate, immunocompetent Atovaquone 750 mg orally every 12 hours with food plus azithromycin 500 mg day 1 then 250 mg daily 7 to 10 days; until smear negative and better
    Severe (≥10 percent or organ dysfunction) Clindamycin 600 mg orally every 8 hours (or 300–600 mg intravenously every 6 hours) plus quinine 650 mg orally every 8 hours; atovaquone plus azithromycin alternative with monitoring 7 to 10 days minimum; longer if immunocompromised
    Immunocompromised, asplenic, or B-cell depleted Atovaquone plus azithromycin preferred; clindamycin plus quinine if critical At least 6 weeks and until smear negative 2 weeks

    A 58-year-old woman with well-controlled HIV (CD4 650 per µL), 1.5 percent parasitemia, mild anemia, and no organ dysfunction has B. microti infection. Best initial therapy is?

    Mild to moderate disease in an immunocompetent host does well with atovaquone plus azithromycin. Chloroquine/primaquine targets malaria liver stages, not Babesia. Even low parasitemia is treated when symptomatic; 10 percent marks exchange consideration, not whether to treat.

    An asplenic 64-year-old man has 12 percent parasitemia with ARDS and creatinine 2.3 mg/dL on atovaquone plus azithromycin. What next?

    Parasitemia 10 percent or greater with organ failure is the trigger to consider exchange transfusion plus ongoing antibabesials. Single-drug therapy fails; doxycycline covers anaplasmosis, not babesiosis.

    Follow-up, persistence, and counseling

    Repeat CBC, metabolic panel, LDH, haptoglobin, and quantitative parasitemia daily until improving. Fever often resolves in 48 to 72 hours and parasitemia falls by half in the same window. If parasitemia rises or organ injury progresses after 48 hours of adherent therapy, check adherence (atovaquone needs fatty food), absorption, and discuss regimen change or exchange with infectious diseases.

    PCR can stay positive for weeks to months after smears turn negative and symptoms resolve. In an immunocompetent patient who finished 7 to 10 days, has negative smears, and feels better, do not prolong antibiotics for lingering PCR positivity. Asplenic or immunocompromised patients can have persistent smear positivity for weeks; they need serial smears and should stay on therapy at least 2 weeks past the last positive smear — often 6 weeks total — with close post-treatment monitoring.

    Counsel that babesiosis is not person-to-person contagious except via transfusion and rare congenital routes. Prior infection does not guarantee lasting immunity, and transfusion deferral may apply. Emphasize tick prevention: permethrin-treated clothing, diethyltoluamide (DEET) or picaridin, daily tick checks with fine-tipped tweezers, and showering after outdoor work. No human vaccine exists.

    Pearl

    Smear negativity, not PCR negativity, is the practical stop signal for the immunocompetent host. Use PCR to confirm and to follow the immunocompromised host, but do not chase PCR in a well patient who finished a course.

    Background

    Transfusion-associated babesiosis prompted FDA donor screening in endemic states. If your patient donated or received blood in the past 3 months and now has babesiosis, notify the blood bank — lookback matters.

    Special populations and the broader differential

    Age over 50 years, asplenia, malignancy, and B-cell immunosuppression each raise risk of severe and relapsing disease. Assume higher parasitemia than the first smear shows and admit with a lower threshold; involve infectious diseases early and plan for extended therapy. Well-controlled HIV with preserved CD4 behaves like the immunocompetent host; risk tracks immunosuppression, not HIV alone.

    In pregnancy, infection can be severe and rarely transmits to the newborn. Data are limited, but atovaquone plus azithromycin is generally preferred over quinine regimens; involve obstetrics and infectious diseases early. In children, use weight-based dosing — do not extrapolate adult doses.

    Keep the differential broad when hemolysis and fever do not fit tick exposure. Thrombotic microangiopathies, autoimmune hemolysis, and malaria in travelers can mimic babesiosis. A patient returning from West Africa with similar rings needs urgent malaria management — pigment, gametocytes, or tropical travel without tick exposure should push you toward malaria until PCR sorts it out.

    Pearl

    In summer in an endemic area, “viral syndrome with thrombocytopenia” is babesiosis and anaplasmosis until proven otherwise — not a virus. A CBC and smear often beat a viral panel.

    A healthy 34-year-old woman finished 10 days of atovaquone plus azithromycin for 0.8 percent B. microti parasitemia. She feels well, hemoglobin is rising, day-14 smear is negative but PCR is positive. What now?

    Lingering PCR after a completed course and negative smear in a well immunocompetent host reflects DNA, not necessarily viable parasites. Extend only for persistent smear positivity in immunocompromised or asplenic patients.

    The five-minute version

    • Suspect babesiosis in summer with fever, hemolytic anemia (high LDH, low haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin), and thrombocytopenia after tick or transfusion exposure in the NE or upper Midwest — especially with asplenia, age over 50, or B-cell immunosuppression.
    • Diagnose with thin smear (pleomorphic rings, rare tetrads/Maltese cross, quantitate percent parasitemia) plus whole-blood PCR for B. microti; single serology shows past exposure.
    • Treat mild to moderate disease with atovaquone plus azithromycin 7 to 10 days; reserve clindamycin plus quinine for severe disease or when atovaquone/azithromycin cannot be used — watch for quinine QT prolongation and hypoglycemia.
    • Consider exchange transfusion for parasitemia 10 percent or greater with organ dysfunction or refractory disease despite therapy.
    • Extend to at least 6 weeks and until smear-negative 2 weeks in asplenic or immunocompromised hosts prone to relapse; otherwise smear negativity ends therapy, not PCR.
    • Screen for Lyme and anaplasmosis co-infection, notify the blood bank for transfusion cases, and counsel on tick checks, DEET/picaridin, permethrin clothing, and prompt removal.
