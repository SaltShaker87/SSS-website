background.txt

Background

Gram-negative bacteremia is a major cause of illness and death in hospitalized patients, yet the data to guide how long to treat it were thin. Current guidelines allowed anything between 7 and 14 days, and that uncertainty was usually resolved by treating longer. Shorter courses could mean fewer drug-related adverse events, less resistance, and fewer superinfections such as C. difficile. This trial tested whether 7 days of covering antibiotics was noninferior to 14 days in patients who had already stabilized.

methods.txt

Methods

DesignRandomized, multicenter, open-label (analyst-blinded), 1:1 parallel-group noninferiority trial at 3 academic centers (2 in Israel, 1 in Italy), January 2013 to August 2017.
PopulationHospitalized adults with aerobic gram-negative bacteremia who were afebrile and hemodynamically stable for at least 48 hours by day 7 of covering antibiotics. Patients with an uncontrolled focus, polymicrobial growth, or immunosuppression such as neutropenia or HIV were excluded. 604 patients were randomized (306 patients to 7 days, 298 patients to 14 days).
Intervention7 days of covering antibiotic therapy, counted from the first day of covering antibiotics (empirical or directed).
Comparator14 days of covering antibiotic therapy. Drug choice, oral switch, and discharge timing were left to the treating physicians in both arms.
Primary endpointComposite at 90 days of all-cause mortality; relapse, suppurative, or distant complications; and readmission or extended hospitalization (>14 days).
Follow-up90 days from randomization, by telephone interview at days 30 and 90 plus healthcare databases.

“The noninferiority margin was set at 10%.” The authors based it on the U.S. FDA's recommendation for trials of complicated urinary tract infection, judging it acceptable given the ecological gain of using fewer antibiotics.

results.xls

Results

Of the 604 patients patients, the source was urinary in 68% and the pathogens were mostly Enterobacteriaceae (90%). Ninety-day follow-up for the primary outcome was completed for everyone. The primary composite outcome occurred in 45.8% of the 7-day group versus 48.3% of the 14-day group: a risk difference of −2.6% (95% CI −10.5% to 5.3%), establishing noninferiority against the 10% margin.

“The primary composite outcome of mortality, clinical failure, readmissions, or extended hospitalization at 90 days occurred in 140 of 306 patients in the short-duration group (45.8%) compared with 144 of 298 in the long-duration group (48.3%) (RD, -2.6% [95% CI, -10.5% to 5.3%]), establishing noninferiority.” — the paper

The per-protocol analysis (92% of patients received the planned duration ±2 days) agreed: 45.7% versus 47.8%, risk difference −2.1%. Ninety-day mortality did not differ (11.8% vs 10.7%; risk difference 1.0%).

Two secondary outcomes favored the short course. Total antibiotic days to day 90 fell to a median of 10 days versus 16 days, and patients got back to their baseline activity sooner (median 2 weeks vs 3 weeks). Resistance development, new infections, hospital days, and adverse events were similar.

conclusions.txt

Conclusions

In hospitalized patients with gram-negative bacteremia who reached clinical stability before day 7, a 7-day antibiotic course was noninferior to 14 days for the composite of death, clinical failure, readmission, or prolonged hospitalization at 90 days. The short course also meant fewer cumulative antibiotic days and a faster return to baseline functional status.

Limitations

Enterobacteriaceae dominated (about 90%), so the results apply less to nonfermenters such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii. The cohort started from 7-day survivors who were already stable, with no uncontrolled source, so it is not comparable to all bacteremia cohorts. The trial was open-label, could not show an effect on resistance, and the potential to shorten hospital stay was not fully realized. Noninferiority was not shown in the small subgroups with inappropriate empirical therapy or MDR pathogens.

What changed in practice

This was the first randomized trial of antibiotic duration in gram-negative bacteremia, and it made 7 days a reasonable default for stable, uncomplicated cases with source control. The authors call cutting treatment to 7 days “an important antibiotic stewardship intervention.” Patients who are not yet stable, have an uncontrolled source, or have nonfermenter infections sit outside the trial's evidence.
