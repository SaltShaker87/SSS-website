background.txt

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is often described as a looming threat, but earlier estimates covered only specific pathogen-drug combinations in a few places. Without a global number, AMR was hard to compare with HIV, malaria, or heart disease when governments set priorities. This Global Burden of Disease analysis set out to produce the most comprehensive estimate yet of how many deaths bacterial AMR causes, where, and from which bugs and drugs.

methods.txt

Methods

DesignSystematic analysis and predictive statistical modelling within the Global Burden of Disease framework; not a trial.
Scope23 pathogens and 88 pathogen-drug combinations in 204 countries and territories, for the year 2019.
DataSystematic literature reviews, hospital systems, surveillance systems, and other sources: 471 million individual records or isolates and 7585 study-location-years. Models filled in locations with no data.
Two counterfactualsAttributable deaths: compared with a world where drug-resistant infections were replaced by drug-susceptible ones. Associated deaths: compared with a world where drug-resistant infections were replaced by no infection at all.
Model chainDeaths where infection played a role, then the share by infectious syndrome, then by pathogen, then the share resistant to each antibiotic, then the excess risk of death from that resistance.
Uncertainty95% uncertainty intervals from 1000 posterior draws; models cross-validated for out-of-sample predictive validity.

The two counterfactuals answer different questions. “Attributable” is what resistance itself costs (if these infections were treatable). “Associated” is the total burden of resistant infections (if they had not happened at all).

results.xls

Results

An estimated 4.95 million deaths were associated with bacterial AMR in 2019, including 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to it (95% UI 0.911 to 1.71 million).

“there were an estimated 4.95 million (3.62–6.57) deaths associated with bacterial AMR in 2019, including 1.27 million (95% UI 0.911–1.71) deaths attributable to bacterial AMR.” — the paper

Measured by associated deaths, AMR would have been the third leading cause of death in 2019, behind only ischaemic heart disease and stroke. Even by the stricter attributable measure it would have ranked 12th, ahead of both HIV and malaria.

The burden was uneven. The death rate attributable to resistance was highest in western sub-Saharan Africa (27.3 per 100 000) and lowest in Australasia (6.5 per 100 000). Lower respiratory infections were the most burdensome syndrome, with more than 1.5 million deaths associated with resistance.

A handful of bugs did most of the damage. Six pathogens (E. coli, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa) accounted for 73.4% of attributable deaths. One pathogen-drug pair, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), alone caused more than 100 thousand attributable deaths.

conclusions.txt

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of the global burden of bacterial AMR. AMR is a leading cause of death around the world, with the highest burdens in low-resource settings, and a small number of pathogen-drug combinations drive most of it.

Limitations

Data were sparse in many low- and middle-income countries, so estimates there lean heavily on modelling. There is no universal laboratory standard for resistance, and breakpoints changed over time, so classification was heterogeneous. Passive surveillance may overestimate resistance if cultures are drawn mainly after treatment fails, and hospital data in LMICs may skew toward urban or sicker patients. Community- and hospital-acquired infections could not be separated well.

What changed in practice

GBD AMR turned AMR from an abstract warning into a ranked cause of death, giving policy makers a number on par with HIV and malaria. It is now the standard citation for AMR's scale in global policy, and it points stewardship, vaccines, infection prevention, and new-drug efforts at the pathogens and regions that matter most.
