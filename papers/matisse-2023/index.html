MATISSE: Bivalent RSV Prefusion F Vaccine in Pregnancy to Protect Infants

background.txt

Background

RSV is a leading cause of lower respiratory tract illness and hospitalization in young infants, and the first months of life are the most dangerous. Infants are too young to be vaccinated well themselves, but antibodies made by a vaccinated mother cross the placenta. MATISSE tested whether a single dose of a bivalent RSV prefusion F (RSVpreF) vaccine given during pregnancy could protect newborns through their first months.

methods.txt

Methods

DesignPhase 3, randomized (1:1), double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 18 countries; this is the prespecified interim analysis.
PopulationHealthy pregnant women at 24 through 36 weeks' gestation and their infants. 3682 women received vaccine and 3676 placebo; 3570 and 3558 infants were evaluated.
InterventionA single intramuscular injection of 120 µg bivalent RSVpreF vaccine (RSV A and B prefusion F proteins).
ComparatorPlacebo injection.
Primary endpointTwo primary end points in infants: medically attended severe RSV lower respiratory tract illness, and medically attended RSV lower respiratory tract illness of any severity, each within 90, 120, 150, and 180 days after birth.
Follow-upInfants through 24 months of age for safety (first trial year); efficacy through 180 days and beyond.

Success required the lower boundary of the confidence interval for vaccine efficacy to be above 20% (99.5% CI at 90 days; 97.58% CI at later intervals).

results.xls

Results

Within 90 days of birth, medically attended severe RSV lower respiratory tract illness occurred in 6 infants in the vaccine group and 33 in the placebo group: vaccine efficacy 81.8% (99.5% CI 40.6 to 96.3). By 180 days it was 19 versus 62 cases, efficacy 69.4%.

“Medically attended severe lower respiratory tract illness occurred within 90 days after birth in 6 infants of women in the vaccine group and 33 infants of women in the placebo group (vaccine efficacy, 81.8%; 99.5% CI, 40.6 to 96.3)” — the paper

The second primary end point, RSV lower respiratory tract illness of any severity, fell short: 24 versus 56 cases within 90 days, efficacy 57.1% (99.5% CI 14.7 to 79.8), which did not meet the statistical success criterion. Efficacy against RSV-associated hospitalization was 67.7% within 90 days and 56.8% within 180 days.

No safety signals were detected in mothers or in infants and toddlers up to 24 months. Adverse events within 1 month were similar: 13.8% versus 13.1% of women and 37.1% versus 34.5% of infants.

conclusions.txt

Conclusions

RSVpreF vaccine given during pregnancy was effective against medically attended severe RSV lower respiratory tract illness in infants, with protection strongest in the first 90 days and still present at 180 days. No safety concerns were identified. Efficacy against RSV illness of any severity did not meet the prespecified success criterion.

Limitations

Women with high-risk pregnancies, including those at current risk of preterm birth, with multiple pregnancies, or with a previous infant with a significant congenital anomaly, were excluded, though their babies may be at higher risk of severe RSV. Data from low-income countries, where the vaccine could matter most, were limited. The trial was not powered to compare efficacy by RSV subgroup, and this was an interim analysis.

What changed in practice

MATISSE led to the first maternal RSV vaccine (Abrysvo), giving families a second way to protect newborns alongside the infant monoclonal antibody nirsevimab. U.S. approval limited use to 32 through 36 weeks' gestation and timed vaccination to the RSV season, partly because of a numerical, nonsignificant imbalance in preterm births, so the choice between maternal vaccine and infant antibody is now a standard prenatal conversation.
