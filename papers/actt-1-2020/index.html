background.txt

Background

Early in the pandemic, several drugs had been tried against Covid-19, but no antiviral had been shown to work. Remdesivir, an inhibitor of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, was an early candidate because it inhibits SARS-CoV-2 in vitro. ACTT-1 was the NIH-sponsored, double-blind, placebo-controlled test of whether it actually helped hospitalized patients. This page covers the final report.

methods.txt

Methods

DesignDouble-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial at 60 trial sites and 13 subsites in the United States, Europe, and Asia; randomization stratified by site and disease severity.
PopulationAdults hospitalized with Covid-19 and evidence of lower respiratory tract infection. 1062 patients underwent randomization (541 patients to remdesivir, 521 patients to placebo).
InterventionIntravenous remdesivir: 200 mg loading dose on day 1, then 100 mg daily for up to 9 more days.
ComparatorMatching placebo for up to 10 days. Both groups received standard supportive care.
Primary endpointTime to recovery: the first day, within 28 days after enrollment, in category 1, 2, or 3 of the eight-category ordinal scale (discharged, or hospitalized only for infection-control or other nonmedical reasons).
Follow-upDaily assessment through day 29. The primary outcome was originally day-15 clinical status and was changed to time to recovery in response to outside evidence that Covid-19 can run a longer course.

The key secondary outcome was clinical status at day 15 on an eight-category ordinal scale, from 1 (not hospitalized, no limits on activity) to 8 (death).

results.xls

Results

Remdesivir shortened recovery: a median of 10 days versus 15 days with placebo (rate ratio for recovery 1.29; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.49; P<0.001). Patients on remdesivir were also more likely to be better on the ordinal scale at day 15 (odds ratio 1.5).

“Patients in the remdesivir group had a shorter time to recovery than patients in the placebo group (median, 10 days, as compared with 15 days; rate ratio for recovery, 1.29; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.12 to 1.49; P<0.001)” — the paper

The benefit was clearest in patients on low-flow oxygen (ordinal score 5; rate ratio 1.45). Confidence intervals were wide for patients not on oxygen, on high-flow oxygen, or on mechanical ventilation or ECMO, where the rate ratio was 0.98.

Kaplan-Meier mortality by day 29 was 11.4% with remdesivir versus 15.2% with placebo (hazard ratio 0.73; 95% CI 0.52 to 1.03), a difference that was not statistically significant. Serious adverse events were less common with remdesivir (24.6% vs 31.6%), and the initial hospital stay was shorter (median 12 days vs 17 days).

conclusions.txt

Conclusions

Remdesivir was superior to placebo in shortening the time to recovery in adults hospitalized with Covid-19 and lower respiratory tract infection. The authors suggest it may also have prevented progression to more severe respiratory disease and reduced the use of scarce hospital resources.

Limitations

The mortality difference did not reach statistical significance. Subgroup estimates outside the low-flow oxygen group had wide confidence intervals, and the median recovery time for ventilated patients could not be estimated, suggesting follow-up was too short for that group. A normal saline placebo was used at some sites because matching placebo ran short, with the infusions masked to keep the blind.

What changed in practice

ACTT-1 was the first double-blind trial to show that an antiviral helps hospitalized Covid-19 patients. Remdesivir became a standard option for inpatients, especially those on low-flow oxygen, and the benefit persisted after adjusting for steroid use, suggesting it may add to dexamethasone.
