What is AI?

A big umbrella term. Most of the excitement is about one small corner of it.

Transition: before we talk about ChatGPT specifically, let’s place it on the map.

The family tree
Artificial intelligence
Machine learning
Deep learning
Generative AI

Each circle is a type of the one around it

AI
Any computer doing something we’d call “smart”
Machine learning
Learns patterns from examples instead of hand-written rules
Deep learning
Machine learning using very large layered networks
Generative AI
Deep learning that makes new things: text, images, audio
LLM
Generative AI for language. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini.

The key idea: “AI” is a huge category. A sepsis alert, a radiology flagging tool, and ChatGPT are all AI, but they are very different animals.

Rule-based example to contrast with machine learning: an old-school drug interaction checker is a lookup table written by people. Machine learning instead learns the pattern from thousands of examples.

LLM stands for large language model. “Large” refers to the size of the model and the amount of text it learned from.

Two kinds you already meet at work

Specialist tools vs. general-purpose assistants

Narrow AI

Does one job, tested for that job

  • Flags a possible PE or brain bleed on a CT
  • Sepsis or deterioration early-warning scores
  • Reorders the radiology worklist by urgency

Usually FDA-cleared for a specific use. Been around for years.

Large language models

Does almost any text task, tested for none

  • Drafts notes, letters, teaching cases, exam questions
  • Summarizes papers, explains concepts at any level
  • Increasingly reads images and documents too

Flexible and fluent. Responsibility for checking sits with you.

Radiology fellows likely live with narrow AI daily. Ask them: what tools are in your workflow right now? How often do you override them?

The contrast that matters: narrow tools are validated for one specific task. LLMs are general-purpose, so nobody has validated them for the specific thing you are about to ask. That shifts the checking burden onto the user.

How does an LLM work?

One trick, repeated very fast: predict the next word.

This is the most important conceptual section. If they leave understanding only one thing, it should be next-word prediction.

Try it · the one trick

It picks the next word from a list of likely options

A patient with MRSA bacteremia should be started on IV  
Click a few times. Then turn the randomness up and try again.

Model’s options for the next word

Illustrative numbers for teaching. Real models weigh tens of thousands of options at every step.

Click “Predict next word” five or six times at the default setting. Vancomycin wins most of the time, daptomycin sometimes.

Now drag randomness toward 2.0 and click again until cefazolin or nafcillin shows up (they turn red). Those are fluent, plausible-sounding, and wrong for MRSA. The model did not “decide” to be wrong; it rolled the dice on a low-probability word.

Takeaway: every answer is built one word at a time, each one a weighted guess. That is why the same question can give different answers, and it is the seed of hallucination.

The official term for the randomness dial is “temperature”. Most chat apps set it for you.

Small print

Actually, it predicts word-pieces called tokens

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is resistant to ertapenem

Rule of thumb

One token is about ¾ of an English word. Common words are one token; rare medical terms get chopped up.

Why you’ll hear it

Limits, speed, and price are all counted in tokens. “This chat is too long” means too many tokens.

Split shown is illustrative; each model chops text a little differently.

Keep this light. The point is vocabulary: when they see “tokens” in pricing, limits, or a paper, they know it means chunks of text.

Fun aside: because it sees word pieces rather than letters, older models famously struggled to count letters in a word.

How it learned

Training looks a lot like medical training

1 · Pretraining

Reads a huge library of text: web pages, books, articles. Learns to predict the next word.

Like the preclinical years: absorbing everything, not yet useful at the bedside.

2 · Instruction tuning

Trained on examples of good question-and-answer conversations.

Like clerkships: learning how to actually talk to people and answer the question asked.

3 · Human feedback

People rate answers; the model is nudged toward the ones rated better.

Like attending feedback: shapes style, safety, and helpfulness.

Then: frozen

Its knowledge stops at a cutoff date. It does not learn from your chats.

Like a graduate who never reads another journal.

The analogy is not perfect but it sticks. The important end point: once training finishes, the model’s built-in knowledge is frozen. It knows nothing that happened after its cutoff unless the app looks it up for it (that’s the harness, coming up).

Step 3 also explains a failure mode we’ll discuss later: because it was rewarded for answers people liked, it can lean toward telling you what you want to hear.

“Does it learn from my chats?” Not in real time. Some companies may use conversations to train future versions depending on settings; ChatGPT for Clinicians says it does not.

Its working memory
What the model can see
Hidden instructions from the app: “You are a helpful assistant…”
Explain the Duke criteria to a third-year student.
Sure. The Duke criteria help decide whether a patient has endocarditis…
📎 uploaded-paper.pdf (the whole text is pasted in here)

The context window is the chart open in front of it

  • Each time, the model re-reads the whole conversation to write the next reply
  • If it isn’t in the window, the model can’t see it
  • Windows are big but finite. Very long chats get fuzzy at the edges
  • A new chat is a blank chart, unless the app adds “memory”

Clinical framing: the model is like a consultant who only knows what’s in the chart you hand them. Nothing else. They will not remember the patient tomorrow unless someone writes it in.

Notice the yellow line at the top: apps quietly add their own instructions before your message. That’s part of the harness. Two apps using the same model can behave differently partly because of those hidden instructions.

“Memory” features in ChatGPT work by saving notes and pasting them back into the window. The model itself still remembers nothing.

Why it makes things up
It was trained to produce text that sounds right. Being right is a side effect.

No built-in fact checker. It doesn’t look things up unless the app makes it.

Gaps get filled with plausible text: references, doses, guideline years.

It sounds equally confident when it’s right and when it’s wrong.

This connects the next-word demo to hallucination. A fabricated citation is just a very plausible sequence of words: real-sounding authors, a real journal, a believable year.

Newer models and better harnesses (web search, citations) have made this much less common, but not zero. The confident tone is the trap, especially for learners.

The engine, the fuel, and the car

Model, runtime, and harness: three words that explain why “ChatGPT” means so many different things.

Transition: so far we’ve talked about the model. But nobody uses a model directly. You use a product built around one. That’s what this section is about.

The analogy · click a part

Engine = the model

The trained LLM. All the raw power, but on its own it just sits on a stand.

Fuel = the runtime

The computers and electricity that actually run the engine, every time you hit enter.

Car = the harness

Everything that turns raw power into a trip: steering, wheels, GPS, trunk, seatbelts.

Driver = you

Your prompt is the steering. You decide where it goes, and you’re responsible for the drive.

Click each part on the right to spotlight it. Click again to show the whole car.

The key message: an engine on a stand goes nowhere. What you experience as “ChatGPT” is a whole car: an engine (the model), fuel to run it (the runtime), and a body built around it (the harness).

Why I added the driver: it reminds them that the human steers and is accountable, which is the theme for Part 5.

The engine is the model

What it is

A very large file of numbers (called weights) produced by training. GPT-5.4, Claude, Gemini, and Llama are engines.

What it does

One thing only: given some text, predict the next token. Over and over.

What it can’t do alone

Search the web, open your files, remember you, or check its work. All of that is added by the car.

Why it matters

Bigger, newer engines reason better and know more. But a great engine in a bad car is still a bad ride.

When people say “GPT-5” or “Claude Opus” they’re naming an engine. Companies release new engines every few months.

Llama is worth naming because it’s an “open-weight” model: anyone can download the engine and run it on their own computer. That’s how hospitals can run AI without sending data outside.

The fuel is the runtime

What it is

The hardware and software that load the engine and run it: specialized chips (GPUs) in a data center, or even a laptop.

Every reply burns fuel

Each token is computed fresh. Longer answers and longer chats use more.

Where you feel it

Speed, “you’ve hit your limit,” free vs. paid tiers, and outages when the data center is overloaded.

Where it runs = where your data goes

Cloud runtime: your text travels to the company’s servers. Local runtime: it never leaves the building.

Tokens are like gallons: companies price and limit usage by tokens. Free tiers are a small tank.

The last box is the one that matters for medicine. Where the runtime sits determines where patient data would go. That’s why some hospitals are interested in running open-weight engines on their own machines.

Personal aside if you like: I run models on my own GPU machines at home. Same engines, my own fuel.

On your original framing (wheels = harness): the wheels are a great piece of it, the part that touches the road. I widen it to “the whole car around the engine” so it can hold all of these features.

Most of what differs between ChatGPT, OpenEvidence, the AI in Epic, and Claude is the harness, not just the engine.

Where things are heading

When the car drives itself: agents

An agent is a harness that lets the model take many steps on its own: search, read, write, check, and try again.

  • Deep research: reads dozens of sources and writes a report
  • Coding agents: build and test software from plain-English requests

More autonomy means more power and more to supervise.

Same engine, but the harness keeps handing the model’s output back to it along with the results of tools, in a loop. That’s all an “agent” is.

Personal example if you want: the apps I’ve built were made this way, with a coding agent doing the typing while I direct and check. I’m not a programmer by training.

Teaching link: more autonomy is like giving a senior resident more independence. Great when supervised well, risky when not.

Where it breaks

Knowing the failure modes is what separates a safe user from a lucky one.

Transition: now that we know how it works, the ways it fails become predictable rather than mysterious.

Five failure modes

Each one follows from how it works

Making things up

Fabricated references, doses, or guideline details, delivered confidently.

Check anything you’d act on

Agreeing with you

Leans toward the answer you seem to want, even when your premise is wrong.

Ask it to argue the other side

Out of date

Knowledge stops at its cutoff. New guidelines and drugs may be missing.

Use a car with search and citations

Bias

Reflects gaps and stereotypes in the text it learned from.

Watch who’s missing from the examples

Privacy

What you type goes to the runtime. Patient details may leave the hospital.

Hospital-approved tools only

And for learners

Over-reliance: skipping the thinking the exercise was meant to build.

Design for it, don’t just ban it

Tie each back: making things up comes from next-word prediction; agreeing comes from human-feedback training; out of date is the frozen engine; privacy is where the runtime lives.

The rule of thumb
AI writes the first draft.
You sign the final one.

Verify anything you’d act on or teach, against a primary source.

Disclose to learners when materials were AI-assisted.

Model it: let learners watch you check the output.

Same principle as supervising a resident: you can delegate the task, not the responsibility.

Disclosure is a norm worth setting early in their careers as educators. It also models honesty for learners.

Where the evidence is

What the research says so far

Meta-analysis · 2026

20 randomized trials, 1,413 learners

Generative AI improved immediate knowledge and skills scores. Almost no data on long-term retention.

Promising, but short-term
Adv Med Educ Pract · 2026

ChatGPT vs. Google and PubMed for case reasoning

Crossover trial, 46 second-year students. Reasoning improved with both tools; neither clearly won.

A tool, not a shortcut
JMIR Med Educ · 2026

ChatGPT inside team trauma simulations

Pilot RCT, 41 students. No gain in decisions or teamwork; AI teams talked less and deferred to the tool.

Over-reliance is real
Perspect Med Educ · 2026

AI-generated vs. real teaching cases

RCT, 386 learners. Test scores were similar, but non-inferiority wasn’t shown and AI cases felt less realistic.

Close, not equal
Queen’s University · IAMSE 2026

AI grading the quality of narrative feedback

2,766 radiology EPA comments. Fine-tuned GPT models rated feedback quality with high accuracy.

The runtime choice, in practice

Big picture: early randomized evidence is encouraging for short-term learning, but follow-up is short, interventions vary, and the trials that looked at real teamwork found over-reliance.

Meta-analysis: 20 RCTs, 1,413 participants, positive pooled effects on immediate knowledge and clinical skills; long-term retention barely studied.

Kasetsart crossover (Nartthanarung et al.): both ChatGPT and conventional search improved clinical reasoning; authors could not attribute gains to one tool.

Turku trauma pilot (Pakkasjärvi et al.): unstructured ChatGPT access did not help; teams deferred to the tool and talked less. Students received no prompting training, which is exactly what today’s session addresses.

Perspectives on Medical Education RCT (Zuo et al.): Gemini-generated, expert-reviewed surgical cases vs. real cases; scores 4.61 vs. 4.95 out of 10, non-inferiority not demonstrated.

Queen’s (Kwan et al.): fine-tuned GPT-4o/4.1 scored narrative EPA feedback on the QuAL instrument; directly relevant to the radiology fellows and to how CCCs could use AI.
